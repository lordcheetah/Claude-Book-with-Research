# We The People — Series Overview

## What this series is
A narrative history of the American founding told through the lives of the men and women who created the United States — from the decade of colonial unrest before the Revolution through the end of the Washington Administration. The series follows real historical figures with the depth and interiority of literary fiction, grounded in the documentary record: their letters, diaries, speeches, and contemporary accounts.

## Tone and genre
Historical narrative fiction. Authoritative but intimate — we are inside these people's heads, watching history happen to them before it was history. Serious without being solemn; the founders were often funny, petty, ambitious, and afraid, and the series doesn't smooth that out. Respectful of the historical record while honest about its contradictions (especially on slavery and the rights of women).

## Target audience
Readers who love history but want to feel it rather than study it. Fans of Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall, Ron Chernow's Hamilton (the biography), and Colson Whitehead's historical fiction. People who know the broad outlines of the founding but want to understand what it felt like from inside.

## Scope
- Books planned: 4 (see books.md)
- Time span covered: approximately 1765–1797 (Stamp Act crisis through end of Washington Administration)

## Core premise
The United States was not inevitable. It was built by specific people with specific fears, ambitions, and blind spots — people who disagreed violently about what they were building and why. The series asks: how did these imperfect, contradictory people create something that outlasted them?

## What makes this series distinct
- Follows the founders as people, not monuments — including their failures, hypocrisies, and the costs their choices imposed on others
- Includes Abigail Adams as a full POV character, and makes the domestic and excluded experience of the founding part of the narrative
- Rigorously grounded in the primary sources (letters, diaries, speeches) while reading as fiction, not biography
- Does not look away from slavery as the central moral contradiction of the founding
- Covers the full arc through the Washington Administration, not just the Declaration and war
