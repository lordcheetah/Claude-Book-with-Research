# Series Characters — We The People

## Primary POV characters

### Thomas Paine (1737–1809)
English-born radical; arrives in Philadelphia November 1774. Working-class background (corset maker, excise officer) sets him apart from the gentleman founders. Author of *Common Sense* (1776) and *The American Crisis*. The outsider whose clarity about independence forced everyone else to say what they actually wanted. Central in Books 1–2; referenced in Books 3–4.

### Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826)
Virginia planter-lawyer-architect; author of the Declaration of Independence. The idealist whose words defined the republic and whose life contradicted them. Enslaved approximately 600 people across his lifetime. Close friendship with Madison. Complicated relationship with Adams. Minister to France (Book 2/3 transition). Secretary of State under Washington (Book 4). The moral contradiction of the founding made flesh.

### Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790)
Eldest of all the founders; self-made printer and scientist turned statesman. Pennsylvania's colonial agent in London for decades before the Revolution. America's first international celebrity. Ambassador to France (Books 2–3). Died 1790 — present through Books 1–3, referenced in 4. The link between the old colonial world and the new republic.

### George Washington (1732–1799)
Virginia planter and veteran of the French and Indian War. Commander in Chief of the Continental Army (Books 1–2). First President (Books 3–4). The indispensable man — everyone's choice for leader, and the one person who could have made himself king and chose not to. Enslaved approximately 300 people at peak. Deeply private; his inner life must be extrapolated carefully.

### John Adams (1735–1826)
Massachusetts lawyer and Harvard graduate. Principled, argumentative, vain, and honest to a fault. Defended British soldiers after the Boston Massacre. Continental Congress delegate. Nominated Washington as Commander in Chief. Diplomatic postings in Europe (Books 2–3). Vice President and 2nd President (Book 4). Died on July 4, 1826 — same day as Jefferson.

### Abigail Adams (1744–1818)
Self-educated, razor-sharp, deeply principled. John's most trusted advisor throughout his career. Managed the family farm and finances during his long absences. Author of the "Remember the Ladies" letter. Her perspective provides the series' most direct access to the domestic reality of the Revolution and the experience of those excluded from formal power. POV character throughout all books.

### James Madison (1751–1836)
Youngest of the major founders; small in stature, sickly, with extraordinary intellectual capacity. Virginia delegate; read extensively in preparation for the Constitutional Convention. Architect of the Virginia Plan; co-author of the Federalist Papers. Pushed for the Bill of Rights. Minor figure in Book 1 (age 24 at Declaration); becomes central in Books 3–4.

## Secondary characters (recurring)

### Alexander Hamilton (c. 1755–1804)
Born in Nevis, West Indies; orphaned young; came to America on the strength of a letter he wrote after a hurricane. Washington's aide-de-camp during the war (Book 2); author of 51 of 85 Federalist Papers (Book 3); first Secretary of the Treasury and architect of the American financial system (Book 4). The great antagonist of Jefferson and Madison in the Washington Administration — their conflict over the national bank, assumption of state debts, and the proper scope of federal power defines Book 4's political drama. Brilliant, aggressive, and constitutionally incapable of letting a slight pass; his feud with Adams helps elect Jefferson in 1800; his feud with Burr ends at Weehawken in 1804. Not the progressive icon of the musical: genuinely elitist, skeptical of democracy, with a complicated relationship to slavery. Minor/cameo in Book 1 (college student publishing political pamphlets); secondary in Book 2; significant in Book 3; central in Book 4.

### Benedict Arnold (1741–1801)
Connecticut merchant-turned-soldier; one of the most effective battlefield commanders on the American side. Wounded at Quebec and Saratoga; repeatedly passed over for promotion by Congress despite genuine heroics. His legitimate grievances accumulate alongside debt, pride, and his marriage to Loyalist Peggy Shippen, until he negotiates to hand West Point to the British (1780). Captured British officer John André is hanged; Arnold escapes to the British side. The betrayal devastates Washington personally. His arc — from indispensable hero to the name Americans cannot say without contempt — is the war's central moral crisis. Primary POV character in Book 2; minor presence in Book 1 (early war actions).

### George Mason (1725–1792)
Virginia planter and neighbor of Washington; largely self-educated from one of the finest private libraries in the colonies. Author of the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) — the document Jefferson drew on for the Declaration and Madison drew on for the Bill of Rights. Attended the Constitutional Convention in 1787 despite chronic poor health and a genuine preference for private life; grew increasingly alarmed at what the Convention produced; refused to sign on three grounds: no Bill of Rights, excessive Senate power, and the protection of the slave trade until 1808. Published "Objections to This Constitution of Government," the foundational Anti-Federalist document. Was vindicated when Madison proposed the Bill of Rights in 1789. The constitutional dispute ruptured his friendship with Washington. Secondary in Book 1 (Virginia Declaration of Rights); central in Book 3 (Convention and ratification debate).

### Mercy Otis Warren (1728–1814)
Massachusetts-born writer, playwright, and historian; sister of James Otis Jr. (the "taxation without representation" advocate); wife of Plymouth patriot James Warren; close friend and correspondent of Abigail Adams. Published political satires lampooning Loyalists in the early 1770s; wrote the Anti-Federalist pamphlet "Observations on the New Constitution" under the pseudonym "A Columbian Patriot" (1788); published the first major history of the American Revolution (1805). Where Abigail provides the domestic-management perspective on the Revolution, Warren provides the Boston intellectual-political perspective — she is in the radical network through her family and her own voice, a published political writer at a time when that was not expected of women. Her eventual criticism of John Adams in her history permanently broke their friendship. Secondary in Book 1; growing significance through Book 3.

### Samuel Adams (1722–1803)
John's older cousin; Boston radical and political organizer. Essential to the early colonial resistance movement. Heavy presence in Book 1; fades in later books as the scene shifts south and to Europe.

### Patrick Henry (1736–1799)
Virginia orator and firebrand; "Give me liberty or give me death." Suspicious of centralized power — an Anti-Federalist, complicates the Constitution story in Book 3.

### Martha Washington (1731–1802)
Washington's wife; managed Mount Vernon; spent winters with the army at Valley Forge. Practical, dignified, devoted. Important presence in Washington scenes throughout.

### Sally Hemings (1773–1835)
Enslaved woman at Monticello; half-sister of Jefferson's deceased wife Martha; mother of several of Jefferson's children. The DNA evidence confirms the relationship. Present in Jefferson scenes; her perspective must be handled with care — she is not a supporting character in his story but a person with her own.

### John Jay (1745–1829)
New York lawyer and diplomat; co-author of Federalist Papers; negotiated Treaty of Paris; first Chief Justice of the Supreme Court.

### James Wilson, Roger Sherman, Gouverneur Morris
Key figures at the Constitutional Convention (Book 3).

## Character relationship map

### The Virginia axis
Jefferson — Madison — Washington: fellow Virginians, neighbors in a political culture; allied on most things, diverging on the role of centralized government

### The Massachusetts axis
John Adams — Abigail Adams — Samuel Adams: Boston / New England perspective; suspicious of southern power; committed Puritan-influenced sense of virtue in public life

### The Franklin connection
Franklin knows everyone; his London years and diplomatic career make him the most internationally connected figure; his friendship with Jefferson (Paris years) is warm and literary

### The Paine problem
Paine has ideological allies among the founders but no social ones — his class background puts him permanently outside the circle of gentlemen who will actually govern; this tension is load-bearing

### Adams–Jefferson
The great friendship and rivalry of the founding generation; close allies in the Revolution, political opponents in the 1790s, eventual reconcilers through letters in old age; both died July 4, 1826

### Hamilton–Jefferson
The central political conflict of Book 4: Federalist vs. Democratic-Republican; strong central government vs. strict construction; commercial republic vs. agrarian republic; Britain vs. France. Both men are inside the Washington Administration and both are fighting for the soul of the republic they helped build. Jefferson eventually resigns.

### Hamilton–Adams
Mutual contempt that proves mutually destructive: Hamilton's efforts to undermine Adams's re-election in 1800 contribute to Jefferson's victory — Hamilton's ideological enemy. An object lesson in what happens when personal animosity overrides political strategy.

### Hamilton–Washington
The closest thing to a father-son relationship in the founding: Washington gives Hamilton the platform his ambition requires; Hamilton gives Washington the intellectual firepower his administration needs. Hamilton is different around Washington — the aggression is tempered, the deference is genuine.

### Arnold–Washington
The other surrogate-son relationship, and the one that ends in betrayal. Washington championed Arnold repeatedly — absorbed political cost defending him to Congress, personally talked him out of resigning multiple times. The discovery of the treason is one of Washington's few documented moments of visible emotional disturbance. "Whom can we trust now?" he said, looking at the evidence. The question hangs over Book 2.

### Mason–Washington
Lifelong neighbors and friends whose constitutional disagreement becomes a genuine rupture. Their estates are five miles apart; their families intertwined; their collaboration built Virginia's pre-Revolutionary politics. When Mason refuses to sign the Constitution that Washington has presided over, both men hold to their positions and neither quite forgives the other before Mason's death in 1792.

### Warren–Abigail Adams
The friendship that runs through the whole series: two exceptional women who found each other through correspondence and held on. Warren is the published political writer Abigail is not allowed to be; Abigail is the insider whose husband is at the center of events. Their letters cross the decades. The eventual rupture — Warren's history criticizes John Adams; John ends the friendship; Abigail is caught between — is one of the series' quieter costs.
