# Magic Systems & Fantasy Worldbuilding

Fantasy-specific craft: designing magic systems and the genre conventions that
fantasy readers expect. The general worldbuilding chain (planet → biology →
culture → …) lives in the `civilization-builder` skill and
`designing-worlds.md`; this file covers the part those don't — **magic** and
fantasy genre craft. (Adapted and translated from a Chinese-language fantasy
worldbuilding skill by wordflowlab.)

## Quick reference

| Element | Guiding principle | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Magic system** | Must have clear rules | Limitations matter more than power |
| **World setting** | Internally self-consistent | Every rule has a cause |
| **Races / creatures** | Distinct and logical | Avoid being humans-with-a-coat-of-paint |
| **Historical depth** | At least three generations | The past shapes the present |
| **Political structure** | Power distribution is clear | Conflict has roots |

## Sanderson's Three Laws of Magic

**First Law** — *An author's ability to solve conflict with magic is directly
proportional to how well the reader understands that magic.*
- If magic resolves a plot problem, the reader must understand its rules first
  (or the resolution feels like a cheat / deus ex machina).
- **Soft** (mysterious) systems are for atmosphere and wonder.
- **Hard** (explicit-rules) systems are for solving problems.

**Second Law** — *Limitations are more interesting than powers.*
- Powerful magic needs a powerful cost.
- Limitations create conflict and force strategy.
- A perfect, unconstrained system creates no drama.

**Third Law** — *Expand what you already have before adding something new.*
- Deepening an existing element beats bolting on a new one.
- Interconnected systems are stronger than isolated ones.
- Complexity should grow organically, not by accretion.

## Designing a hard magic system

Specify all four, plus the boundary:

1. **Energy source** — Where does the magic come from? Finite or infinite? Can
   it be exhausted?
2. **Rules of use** — Who can use it? How is the ability acquired? What
   conditions are required? What are the constraints?
3. **Cost / consequence** — What does using it cost? What happens with overuse?
   Are there long-term effects?
4. **Possible vs. impossible** — What can it do, what can it explicitly *not*
   do, and where is the boundary?

> **Worked example.** *System:* elemental manipulation. *Source:* each person is
> born with an affinity for one element. *Rules:* you can only manipulate your
> own element, and that element must be present nearby. *Limits:* mental fatigue;
> overuse causes elemental backlash. *Cost:* casting draws on life force and
> requires rest to recover. *Forbidden:* you cannot create an element, only move
> it; you cannot manipulate elements inside a living body.

## Designing a soft magic system

- Rules are not fully known to the reader.
- Used for atmosphere, wonder, and awe — not to conveniently resolve the main
  conflict.
- Good fits: ancient/partly-lost magic, divine power beyond mortal understanding,
  background spectacle, subplots that don't decide the climax.

A common, robust pattern is **hard where it solves problems, soft where it
creates wonder** — explicit rules for the magic the protagonist wields to win,
mystery for the magic that sets mood and scale.

## Common pitfalls

- **Over-explanation.** Spending chapters explaining the system and stopping the
  story. *Fix:* show the system in action; explain only what a character needs;
  let the reader assemble some of it; information must serve plot or character.
- **Inconsistent rules.** Bending the magic for plot convenience. *Fix:*
  establish the major rules early; track what's been established; foreshadow any
  exception; make characters solve problems creatively *within* the limits.
- **Default medieval Europe.** Every fantasy a reskin of the same setting. *Fix:*
  draw on other cultures and periods; blend elements; build genuinely novel
  social structures; ask how magic itself would reshape a society's development.
- **The Chosen One trap.** The hero matters because of prophecy/bloodline rather
  than action. *Fix:* let the protagonist become special through choices; if a
  prophecy exists, make them work to fulfil it; subvert or deconstruct the trope;
  focus on growth, not innate gift.

## Fantasy subgenres (reader expectations differ)

- **High / epic fantasy** — fully invented world, good-vs-evil, epic scale and
  stakes, magic woven into the world's fabric.
- **Low fantasy** — real world plus magical elements, smaller personal stakes,
  rare and mysterious magic, grounded tone.
- **Urban fantasy** — modern city setting, hidden magical world, often
  detective/mystery elements, two worlds colliding.
- **Dark fantasy** — moral grey, horror and violence, anti-hero protagonists,
  harsher consequences.

## The iceberg principle & reveal timing

**Create 100%, reveal 10–20%.** The reader doesn't need to know everything;
deep creation produces confidence and consistency. The author knows far more
than is shown.

Reveal on a curve, not in a dump:
- **Chapter 1** — basic worldview (not an exposition lecture), one striking
  magical/fantastical element, the protagonist's place in the world.
- **First 25%** — core magic-system rules, the major peoples/cultures, the
  central conflict and its world-roots.
- **Middle** — deepen established elements; complicate the politics; reveal how
  history shapes the present.
- **Late** — connect all the world threads; reveal deep history; show how the
  world's elements resolve (or fail to resolve) the conflict.

## What fantasy readers want — and what frustrates them

**Want:** an immersive, believable world; a consistent and interesting magic
system; complex cultures and politics; a fresh take on classic tropes; a world
that feels bigger than the story.

**Frustrates:** inconsistent world rules; plot-convenient magic; a thin
medieval-Europe knockoff; a world without depth; information dumps instead of
organic reveal.

## Magic-system / fantasy worldbuilding checklist

- [ ] The magic system has clear rules and limitations
- [ ] Geography and climate shape culture and plot
- [ ] At least three generations of historical depth
- [ ] Multiple political powers with clear motives
- [ ] Economy and trade have a logical basis
- [ ] Races/cultures have internal diversity (not monolithic)
- [ ] Magical creatures have an ecological niche and a purpose
- [ ] Religion/belief systems have real influence
- [ ] Social structure has clear class lines and mobility
- [ ] All rules stay consistent across the whole story

> **Remember:** great worldbuilding is the *foundation* of a story, not its
> purpose. The world serves character and plot; the characters' actions are
> shaped and limited by the world's rules. Balancing depth against narrative
> flow is the whole game.
