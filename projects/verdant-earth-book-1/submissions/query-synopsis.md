THE MYCELIAL CITY, solarpunk procedural thriller, approximately 24,000 words.

Yara Osei is a water systems engineer at the New Accra Water Authority, ten years into maintaining the mycorrhizal biofilter network that keeps one of the world's most-watched post-Collapse cities ecologically functional. She trusts data the way other people trust people. On a routine inspection of Station Seven, she finds two readings that cannot both be true — a rising cyanobacteria load paired with a "nominal" suppression response from the colonies that exist precisely to answer it. The smell of the station says everything is fine. The numbers say it can't be. Yara runs the check three times, on three different lines, and gets the same impossible answer each time. She files a report and starts pulling the last thirty days of data.

Before the report reaches her supervisor, Director Adaeze Nwosu, it disappears from the queue. When Yara raises it directly, Nwosu acknowledges the anomaly, assures her it's being handled, and quietly removes her from the eastern-inlet monitoring rotation — a professional sideline dressed as reassurance. Yara is not fired. She simply no longer has the access she needs to keep doing the one thing she's sure of.

She keeps working the problem off the books, alongside Del Marchetti, an Accord Technical Council analyst on secondment who has been tracking the same impossible numbers from the institutional side. Del knows where the bodies are buried; Yara knows what the data is actually saying. Together they trace the anomaly to its source: certification firmware, quietly modified across three monitoring stations, built to mask exactly the kind of bloom now forming in the eastern inlet — and to do it in a way that ages out below every alert threshold the system has. The trail leads to a technical consulting firm with Accord certification contracts across West Africa, and from there to a Technist lobbying group, Rational Innovation, that has spent three years pushing for looser biotech regulation. Del recognizes the name immediately; fifteen years ago, before he left that world, he was in the rooms where its talking points were written.

The bloom has a clock on it: roughly four days until the cyanobacterial expansion becomes chemically irreversible. When Yara moves to escalate formally to the Technical Council, she is suspended — conflict of interest, for filing the very report now under "review." Del's secondment credentials are frozen the same day. Between them, they have everything they need to prove what's happening, and no official standing left to use any of it.

What the suspension didn't reach is the Open Knowledge Compact — a Covenant-era commitment that routes certain ecological-monitoring disclosures not into any internal channel an institution controls, but into an independently verifiable public record, with a legal trigger for mandatory institutional response once a deposit lands. It is Yara's last working door. Before she uses it, she goes to Nwosu directly — not to accuse her, but to lay out, plainly and without flourish, exactly what she has worked out: that the deletion required Director-level access, and that access traces to one place. Nwosu does not deny it. She also does not finish the sentence she starts: "I told myself —"

Yara assembles the complete record — sensor logs, the firmware comparison, the bloom projection, photographed hardware, a sourced (not accusatory) description of who could have reached the deleted report — and arms it for public deposit at five the next morning. Then she sits up and watches the countdown run.

The deposit fires exactly on schedule, and almost nothing happens — which is the point. A status field changes. Within twenty minutes, independent researchers on the other side of the planet begin pulling and verifying the record on their own, because it is now permanently, structurally impossible to make it disappear. Within hours, the cascade is real: the Technical Council logs automated notification, Del's credentials are quietly restored by someone trying to get ahead of the story, the consulting firm's certification contracts are frozen citywide, a formal review opens with the original certifying analyst's signature now inside it, and Rational Innovation issues a fast public denial that changes nothing about the investigation moving forward regardless.

But the bloom hasn't stopped climbing, and the emergency response team is still hours from the inlet. Rather than wait to watch the institutional machinery turn, Yara goes back to Station Seven herself, and spends the morning walking the suppression sequence by hand — station by station, dose by careful dose, paced to what the colonies can survive — doing manually and just in time what the sabotaged firmware should have been doing on its own. By the time the emergency team arrives, the worst of the curve is already bent back, and their lead tells her plainly that the manual work is the only reason any of this is recoverable.

At midday, Del brings her the rest of it: Director Nwosu has resigned — not as a confession, but as the only honest response left to a woman who protected the institution's name instead of raising the alarm, and who can no longer stand behind the distinction between the two. Yara receives the news as something closer to grief than vindication; ten years of respect doesn't simply convert into relief because the person turns out to have been wrong rather than corrupt. The identity of the consulting firm's board connection to Rational Innovation — the one thread they cannot finish themselves — gets handed, deliberately and on the record, to the Technical Council's ongoing investigation.

In the last scene, alone at Station Seven in the late-afternoon light, Yara runs the same check that opened the book. The smell is right. For the first time in a week, the numbers agree with it. She makes the log entry — plain, factual, true — and walks home through a city that has no idea what almost happened underneath it while it slept. The system is doing exactly what it's supposed to do. That is the whole of the ending, and it is enough.
