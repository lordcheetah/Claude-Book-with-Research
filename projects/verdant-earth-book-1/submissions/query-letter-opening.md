# Query Letter Opening — The Mycelial City

The biofilter at Station Seven smelled right, which was the first thing wrong. That single contradiction — a system telling its engineer two things that cannot both be true — is the engine of THE MYCELIAL CITY, a 24,000-word solarpunk procedural thriller about what happens when the data is correct, the institution is not, and the only person positioned to do anything about it is the one person that institution has just decided to quietly sideline.

Yara Osei has spent ten years keeping New Accra's mycorrhizal water-treatment network running, and she trusts what a sensor log tells her more than she trusts most people. When she finds a pattern that shouldn't exist — and watches her report about it vanish before it reaches her supervisor's desk — she doesn't get angry. She starts pulling thirty days of raw data. What she and a wry, institutionally-fluent Accord analyst named Del Marchetti eventually uncover is a deliberately masked algal bloom on a four-day clock to chemical irreversibility, a certification firm with ties to a biotech-deregulation lobby, and a director who would rather manage the story than raise the alarm. When their official channels are frozen on the same afternoon, the only door left open is the one channel a suspension can't touch — a public record that, once filed, cannot be quietly taken back. THE MYCELIAL CITY is about what it costs to use that door, and what it's worth protecting once you have.

Readers who loved the lived-in optimism of Becky Chambers's *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* or the institutional-ecological tension of Annalee Newitz's *The Terraformers* will find a kindred world here — a future that has already done the hard work of becoming worth saving, and a protagonist who proves that competence, applied honestly and on time, is its own kind of heroism.
