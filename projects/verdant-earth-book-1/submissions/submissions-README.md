# Submission Materials — The Mycelial City

Generated from the completed manuscript (6 chapters, ~24,000 words), the original story synopsis (`story/synopsis.md`), and the character bibles for Yara Osei and Del Marchetti.

## Files

- **logline.md** — 35-word elevator pitch, built around Yara's defining trait (trusts data over authority) and the central mechanism (the one credential her suspension couldn't reach).
- **back-cover-blurb.md** (~198 words) — opens on the manuscript's actual first line ("The biofilter at Station Seven smelled right, which was the first thing wrong"), which tested as the strongest hook in the book — better than any line written specifically for marketing. Ends on the unresolved tension of the suspension and the choice still ahead of her; does not reveal the resolution.
- **query-synopsis.md** (~900 words) — full plot arc in third person present tense, including the ending, as agents expect. Follows the book's actual structure beat-for-beat rather than the original planning synopsis (see note below on where they diverge).
- **query-letter-opening.md** — three paragraphs: hook (the same opening-line contradiction), expanded pitch, and comp titles.

## What to customize

- **Comp titles**: I used Becky Chambers's *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* (2021) and Annalee Newitz's *The Terraformers* (2023) — both recent, both successful without being the kind of runaway bestseller that makes a comp feel presumptuous, and both genuinely adjacent in tone (solarpunk warmth + institutional/ecological tension). If you're querying an agent with known preferences, swap in titles from their MSWL or recent sales.
- **Agent-specific framing**: The query letter opening doesn't address any individual agent's stated interests — add a line at the top of paragraph one if you're customizing for someone specific (e.g., an agent who's said they want "hopeful climate fiction" or "procedural thrillers with heart").
- **Word count framing**: ~24,000 words sits at novella/short-novel length. If you're querying agents who represent novellas specifically, that's a selling point — lead with it. If you're positioning this as the opener of a series (which the series bible confirms it is), you may want to note that explicitly, since agents evaluating series openers sometimes want to know the planned scope up front.

## Where the finished book diverged from the original synopsis

Worth knowing, in case it changes how you'd want to position the book:

- **The original synopsis compresses the confrontation and resolution into a faster sequence than the finished book actually uses.** In the manuscript, Yara's direct confrontation with Director Nwosu happens *before* the deposit fires (end of Ch. 5) — and lands as a quiet, evidence-laying-flat scene rather than an accusation. The original synopsis implied the deposit itself would be the moment of confrontation; in the finished book, the confrontation is personal and the deposit is procedural, and keeping them separate turned out to be the stronger choice. The query synopsis above reflects what the book actually does.
- **Nwosu's resignation lands as confirmation of a *shape*, not a *fact*.** The original synopsis states more plainly that "she was protecting the city's reputation, not participating in the sabotage." The finished book is more careful than that — it deliberately preserves ambiguity about how much Nwosu actually knew, and frames her resignation as an action that *fits* Yara's private theory without ever proving it. If an agent asks about the antagonist, this nuance — institutional self-protection rather than cartoonish villainy — is one of the book's actual selling points, not a loose end.
- **The center of gravity shifted slightly from "uncovering the cover-up" toward "what you do once the truth is finally out and the crisis still isn't over."** The original pitch leans hardest on the investigation. The finished book's most memorable sustained sequence is arguably the long, dialogue-free stretch where Yara does the actual physical remediation work alone at Station Seven — competence as the climax, rather than confrontation. If you find yourself wanting to lead with a different emphasis than what's drafted here (e.g., foregrounding that sequence over the institutional-thriller throughline), that's a reasonable instinct and worth discussing before finalizing.

## Is this the right emphasis?

The materials above lead with **Yara and the data anomaly** as the hook, and the **institutional cover-up** as the central conflict — which matches both the original synopsis and the finished book's actual structure. Two things worth a second look:

1. Should the blurb foreground **Del** more? He's a strong, voice-distinct deuteragonist with his own quiet arc (a "moral accounting" with his Technist past that closes, restrained, in the final chapter) — but the book is unambiguously Yara's, and the materials reflect that. If you want a version that gives Del more marketing weight (useful if you're positioning this for readers who want a strong central partnership/banter dynamic), say so and I can draft an alternate blurb.
2. The **solarpunk-and-hopeful** angle is real and load-bearing (the book's whole thesis is "a world worth protecting, protected by people doing their jobs well") but it's easy for a thriller-forward blurb to undersell it. If you're querying agents who specifically want hopeful/solarpunk SF rather than thriller-forward SF, I'd shift the blurb's opening beat to lead harder with "the city works" before introducing the crack in it — happy to draft that version too.
