# Chapter 2: The Missing Report

She had given herself one night to let it be a system error, and one night was enough.

Yara reached the Water Authority building before the sun had cleared the Upper Terraces, before the front desk was staffed, before anyone else on the mid-terrace floor had thought about coffee. She'd slept badly — a few hours, then awake at four with the readings already running behind her eyes, and at five she'd given up and dressed in the dark. The office took her credentials without comment. The treatment ponds in her window held the early light flat and grey, the sky not yet committed to morning.

She sat down and ran the search she'd run last night. EI-2091-447. *No record found.* The same answer. She queried the submission pipeline log for her own credentials across the day she'd filed — every action her account had taken, in or out of the incident system. The log returned three entries. A flow-check sign-off at the mid-wetlands node. A sensor-swap confirmation at Station Eleven. Both present, both timestamped, both exactly where she'd left them. The report was not the third entry.

There was no third entry. It wasn't that her day had been erased. The flow check and the sensor swap had been allowed to stand. The report, specifically, had been taken out from underneath them.

She moved to the intake log next. When any document entered the incident queue, the system fired an automated receipt — a small immutable record, separate from the document itself, the way a postal stamp stayed on the envelope after the letter was gone. EI-2091-447 should have had a receipt: EI-2091-447-RCPT, generated the instant the system told her *Report received.* She searched for it.

It wasn't there.

Yara sat with that for a moment, very still, her hands flat on the desk. The intake system did not delete receipts. It couldn't, not through any interface she had ever touched; the receipt log was append-only by design, the whole point of it. Deleting the report was one kind of access. Reaching into the receipt log and lifting out the stamp was another kind entirely — deeper, and cleaner, and not available to anyone who'd simply made a mistake. This was not a misfile. A misfile left a receipt. A misfile left a trail you could walk back. There was no trail. Someone had gone in after both.

She opened her field notebook to the page from yesterday. EI-2091-447, in her own hand, the ink slightly dragged where she'd capped the pen too soon. The only surviving official evidence that the report had ever existed. Below it, in the same blunt pencil, she wrote one line: *Intake receipt also deleted. Not a misfile.*

She considered filing again. The anomaly was still in the raw data; she had the draft saved locally; she could resubmit inside ten minutes. But a second identical report, filed before anyone had acknowledged the first, with no receipt and no record to anchor it to — that read as error, or as agitation, and she had nothing to prove the first one but a paper number and her own word. Filing again would spend the evidence and buy nothing. She needed Nwosu. Whatever Nwosu knew or didn't, this could not stay in a field notebook in Yara's kit bag.

Before she went up, she checked the lab.

The assay results were in — flagged available overnight, sitting in the queue with the small green marker that meant *ready.* She opened them before she did anything else.

Three physical samples from Station Seven's inlet outflow, drawn yesterday morning, chain of custody intact, run by the lab overnight. The cyanobacteria count came back elevated. Within range, below any alarm band — but elevated, and consistent, sample to sample, with what the raw sensors had been saying. The numbers on the screen matched the numbers off the manifold. Whatever was flattening the digital feed had not reached into the vials. The water itself was telling the truth. The colonies in that water were carrying a load they weren't reporting a fight against, and the assay had no opinion about firmware. It only measured what was there.

She read it twice. Then she closed the terminal, picked up the notebook, and went up two floors.

---

Nwosu's window looked out over the full inlet, which meant she saw Station Seven every working day.

Yara knocked and was admitted. The office was the same functional era as her own, scaled up: two monitor arrays on the desk, physical trays of paper stacked with a precision that meant someone actually worked through them. On the far wall a status display showed the whole network — twenty-three stations laid out in their geography, each one a small steady light. Yara's eye went to it before she could stop it. Stations Seven, Eight, and Nine showed the same calm green as all the others. From here, the inlet was nominal. Nwosu watched the entire system, and the entire system was telling her nothing was wrong.

Nwosu looked up from her work with the attentiveness of someone already braced for the conversation, though she gave nothing away that Yara could have named as foreknowledge. Focused. Slightly overextended, the way the head of a technical division running on twenty years was always slightly overextended. She gestured Yara to the chair across the desk.

"Director." Yara sat. She said it once, plainly. "I filed a report yesterday afternoon. Eastern inlet, stations seven through nine — a discrepancy between the raw station data and the summary feed. The system gave me a confirmation number and a forty-eight-hour review window." She set the notebook on the desk, open, and turned it toward Nwosu. "EI-2091-447. The report has been deleted. Not archived, not reviewed and closed — deleted. There's no record I filed it. And the intake receipt is gone too."

Nwosu looked at the notebook. She didn't reach for it. She didn't dismiss it, either, and she didn't perform surprise. "I'm aware there are anomalous readings in the eastern inlet," she said. "The technical division is reviewing it internally. I appreciate you bringing this to me directly."

"What does internally reviewing mean," Yara said, "when the report that documented the anomaly no longer exists?" She kept it level. "I'm not accusing anyone of anything I can't support. But the intake receipt being gone isn't a database error. The intake log doesn't delete receipts. It can't, through any interface I have. Someone with access I don't have went in and took it out."

Nwosu was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, something in her had shifted register — not colder, but moved from the engineer toward the operator, the one who knew which council member to call and how much to tell them. "The Authority takes its integrity very seriously, Yara. An internal process has been initiated. And this is exactly the kind of situation where you should not be investigating on your own. It needs the right oversight, through the right channels. There's protocol here, and the protocol exists to protect everyone. Including you."

Yara reached into her kit and laid the lab results on the desk, beside the notebook. "Three physical assays. Station Seven inlet outflow, drawn yesterday morning, chain of custody intact, run overnight. Elevated cyanobacteria — consistent with the raw sensor data, sample for sample." She kept her finger off the page; she just let it sit there. "This isn't a digital artifact. We have the Authority's raw data, the Accord's independent feed, and now physical samples. Three sources that don't share hardware. They all say the same thing."

Nwosu looked at the results. She read them — Yara watched her read them, the eyes moving the way an engineer's eyes moved, not a manager glancing at a page. The room was very quiet. When she spoke, the warmth hadn't gone out of her, but something had settled into place behind it: a composure that had been decided before Nwosu walked into the room, and was now simply being carried.

"I'm grateful for the thoroughness. I mean that." She squared the lab results gently against the notebook, two pieces of paper, and pushed them an inch back toward Yara. "I'm going to reallocate the eastern inlet monitoring rotation while the review is underway. You'll move to mid-wetlands and inland distribution for the duration." She said it the way you'd note a schedule change, no edge to it. "It's standard. When the person who raised a flag is also the person monitoring the flagged area, we rotate them off. Conflict of interest. It protects the integrity of the review, and it protects you."

Yara understood exactly what it was. She didn't argue. Arguing was for people who hadn't yet understood. She said one thing. "I have a local copy of the deleted report. I'll be keeping it."

"Of course," Nwosu said. "Your own documentation is your own."

Yara picked up the notebook and the lab results, stood, and thanked the director for her time. She meant it in the literal sense — the time had cost Nwosu something, and the courtesy between them had held the whole way through, load-bearing in both directions. Then she left.

Back at her own desk, she transferred the local copy of the report from her terminal to a personal drive — not the Authority's network, not anymore — and locked the drive in her kit bag. She sent Del a message: *Can you come to me, or should I come to you? Either way, today.* She had barely set the tablet down when her assignment list refreshed itself, mid-morning, unprompted. Eastern inlet: reassigned. Her credentials still opened every door in the building. Her assignment no longer pointed at the only doors that mattered.

---

"Forty-seven days," Del said, and wrote it on the notepad in the middle of the desk between them, where they could both see it.

His office in the Upper Terraces was a little better furnished than hers and considerably more lived in — a man who worked here rather than one who used it to write things down. Better coffee, too; she'd taken the cup he handed her and it was actually good. His main screen held the sixty-day Accord feed, three station columns, sixty rows of dates. The notepad on the desk was already half-covered in his diagrams, arrows and boxes and a small column of figures.

"Not three weeks." He tapped the screen. "I pulled the full sixty like we said. The pattern doesn't start at your thirty-day edge. It starts here — forty-seven days back. Same signature the whole way: cyano up, coherence flat, summary clean." He sat back. "I'd been watching it from our side for two weeks before I ever came to you, and I never escalated, because every time I looked at the dashboard it read nominal and I told myself it was a processing artefact. It wasn't until I pulled the raw logs that I saw it had teeth. And then I saw it had been there before I started looking."

Yara took that in. Forty-seven days put the start of it well behind anything on her horizon — behind the first instance she'd caught in passing in the daily summaries, behind all four she'd traced by hand. "Then this wasn't reactive," she said. "Nobody saw a bloom forming and scrambled to hide it. Six weeks back, before the elevation was even subtle enough to notice, the suppression was already running. It was put in place ahead of the thing it's hiding."

"That's where I landed too." Del turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on the desk. "Here's the second thing, and I want to tell it to you in order, because the order is the part that matters." He pulled up a second window. "I escalated yesterday. On my side. A clean technical query to my monitoring supervisor — flagged the discrepancy between my raw feed and the summary, documented, entirely inside my role. Nothing dramatic. Exactly the question I'm paid to ask." He turned the screen toward her. "This morning, the eastern inlet stations are off my dashboard. Not nominal. Not anomalous. Reclassified as scheduled maintenance. A maintenance window."

Yara looked at the entry. *Scheduled maintenance — Eastern Inlet, Stations 7–9.*

"I checked the maintenance record," Del went on. "When it was logged. It went in three hours after I sent my query." He let that sit, then said it the rest of the way. "My query and the reclassification. Nine hours apart, start to finish. Your report was deleted the same day you filed it. Two different mechanisms — yours got pulled out of the system, mine got buried under an administrative category — but the same effect. Both escalation paths cleared. Mine through the Accord, yours through the Authority."

"Within the same window," Yara said. She set down the coffee and laid it out for him in order — the report deletion on day one, the intake receipt gone when she'd dug this morning, the Nwosu meeting, the rotation off monitoring, the lab results confirming the physical samples matched the raw data. He listened going still, something recalibrating behind his eyes, his hands quiet for once.

"So whoever's doing this is watching," Del said. Not dramatically — the flat register of an institutional observation, a man stating a thing about how systems behaved. "Your report. My query. Both of them moved something, almost as soon as they were made. Two responses, coordinated in time, coming out of two different bureaucracies." He spread his hands a few centimeters. "Either someone is sitting on both our trails at once, or there's one thing that reaches into both institutions. I don't know which. But I know it's one of those two."

Yara pulled the deletion timestamp from her morning's work — the last-modified date on the intake log entry that should have held the receipt. Del put up the timestamp on his maintenance reclassification. They lined them up on the notepad, side by side in his handwriting. Day one, evening. Within six hours of each other. The same window had closed over both of them.

---

She didn't need a specialist model. The standard cyanobacterial doubling-time calculation had been in every water systems curriculum since 2064, and she'd run it in her head on worse data than this.

Del had gone quiet. The midday light came in level across the desk. He pushed the notepad toward her, then sat back to watch her work — he was a man who filled silence, and he was letting this one stand.

Yara opened her raw data on the tablet, her thirty-day Authority baseline, and set it next to his forty-seven-day Accord feed. "What we're looking at is consistent with early-stage bloom formation in an estuary," she said, working as she talked, because she thought better explaining and because Del was following. "The cyanobacteria are present and climbing. The biofilter colonies are either not responding to them, or being prevented from reporting that they're responding. For our purposes the two are the same problem. They lead to the same place."

"Walk me to the place," Del said.

"Cyanobacteria, under the right conditions, build a self-sustaining chemical loop." She kept it simple; he was technically literate, not a water systems specialist, and a lecture was its own kind of disrespect. "They excrete compounds that suppress competing organisms and favor their own growth. Below a certain concentration, the biofilter can break that loop — the colonies choke the bloom back down, which is what they're for. Above that concentration, the loop sustains itself chemically. The bloom no longer needs anything external to keep going, and no amount of biofilter activity stops it from the inside." She drew a flat line on the notepad and a threshold above it. "That line is the irreversibility point. Below it, the system can self-correct. Above it, you've left the range where the colonies can do anything at all."

She started the model. The doubling time for cyanobacteria in this estuary, at this water temperature — she checked the temperature log, upper-normal for the wet season, which accelerated growth — applied to the current baseline load, projected forward to the threshold concentration. "This isn't proprietary," she said, half to herself, watching the projection build. "Any graduate student could run it. The only thing I have that they don't is forty-seven days of real baseline instead of a textbook curve."

The number resolved. She didn't announce it. She wrote it on the notepad, under the circle Del had drawn earlier: *~84 hours to chemical irreversibility (±6h).* Then she said it aloud, so he'd have heard it from her and not just read it. "About eighty-four hours. Give or take six."

Del looked at it for a while. "Let me get the distinction right," he said, "because I'm going to have to say it to someone eventually. Is that too late to reverse the bloom, or too late to save the biofilter?"

"It's too late to use the biofilter to arrest the bloom." She was careful with it; the distinction mattered. "After irreversibility, the bloom runs its course no matter what the colonies do. You'd need emergency remediation — a different class of intervention entirely. Public declaration, the full emergency response, hands-on work in the inlet." She paused. "Which is survivable. The eastern inlet has come through blooms before. It would take months to bring back, but it comes back. That isn't the part that should frighten anyone."

"Then what's the part."

"The record." She looked up at him. "The summary feed shows nominal right now and will keep showing nominal, because that's what the suppression does. It'll read nominal up until the moment the bloom is physically visible in the water. So the paper trail won't show an Authority that missed something subtle. It'll show an Authority that monitored the inlet every single day, found nothing anomalous, certified everything clean — and then had a bloom appear out of nowhere."

Del was quiet, and then he put it into his own language, the institutional translation he was better at than anyone she knew. "So the question isn't whether the bloom is preventable. The question is whether the record will show they knew." He turned the notepad a few degrees. "Whoever built this wanted the Authority standing there with a clean monitoring log and a dead inlet behind it. Officially watched. Officially found nothing. Then a bloom from nowhere. In public." He didn't say it with any irony. "In a city that's something of a showcase."

"And the longer it's been running, the worse the record reads," Yara added. "Forty-seven days of nominal reports, all of them the Authority's own, all of them signed and filed. An institution looks exactly as negligent as its data says it does. Right now its data says everything's fine." She wrote a second line under the first: *Anomaly recorded clean: 47 days and counting.* "The clean record is the weapon. They built it out of our own paperwork."

She capped the pen and set it down beside the notepad. Eighty-four hours. The day was already half gone.

---

"So," Del said.

"So," Yara said.

"What do we do."

She noted the phrasing — not *what should we do,* which handed the decision to someone else, but *what do we do,* which assumed the two of them already were the someone. She answered the way she'd start any systems problem: with what they had.

"Inventory," she said. "You have the sixty-day Accord raw feed — your own data, documented and timestamped, on your personal drive and in the Accord's archive. The reclassification only touched the dashboard display. It didn't reach back into the archive." Del nodded. "I have my thirty-day Authority raw data, already saved off the network. The local copy of the deleted report with the full analysis. The lab results. The paper notebook with the confirmation number. And the note I wrote to Station Seven's embedded hardware log last night — which is sitting in the physical station out in the reeds, outside every system either of us has been touching."

"And I still have my credentials," Del said. "Maintenance reclassification took the inlet off my board, but my access to the Accord archive and the certification chain hasn't changed. Nobody's frozen anything." He turned his cup again, slowly. "I've been asked to let the Authority handle it. Obliquely. Nobody's told me to stand down in so many words. There's a difference, and I intend to keep living in it."

"Then here's the shape of it." Yara said it plainly, once. "We keep working this. Off-shift. Not through the Authority reporting system, not through the Accord monitoring chain — they're both closed and they're both watched. We use only what we've already collected and what's already in the archives. Nothing new goes into any feed that something can reach into." She looked at him. "I'm not asking you to spend your secondment on a promise, Del. I'm telling you what I'm going to do. You can be in or you can be clear of it."

Del was quiet for a moment — unusual; he filled silence the way water filled a low place. Outside his window the city did its work in the early afternoon. The living walls on the buildings opposite were a dense, breathing green, the distant glint of the treatment ponds far below at the city's edge, a cooperative delivery rig moving unhurried along the terrace pedestrian way. New Accra, in full midday detail, looking exactly like the thing it told the world it was.

"I've been in since yesterday," Del said at last. "On the staircase landing, when you told me you'd flagged it calibration anomaly instead of suppression, and you held my look while you did it. I just wanted you to say it out loud." He picked the pen back up. "Tonight, then. We work separate, compare in the morning. You take the raw data — there are pattern markers in forty-seven days you haven't mapped yet."

"And you?"

"The certification logs. Stations seven, eight, nine — the Accord's certification records are public, and nobody's reclassified those." He wrote it on the notepad: *Cert logs — 7, 8, 9.* "If something modified those stations, the certification chain is where it had to pass through. It'll be in the paperwork. It always is."

He drew one more thing, off to the side, separate from the bloom clock and the timestamps — a small empty square. "If this is what it looks like," he said, carefully. "If someone built this and installed it six weeks out — there's a third thing we don't have. A why. Who wants a showcase city to have a bloom appear from nowhere, and what does the appearing buy them." He didn't fill the square in. "I don't have a single guess yet. I want to write down that I don't."

Yara looked at the empty square for a moment. Then she picked up her kit bag and stood. At the door she paused — not for effect, only because the thing was true and she'd decided to say it before she stopped saying anything for the day.

"If someone deleted that report, they know what I found. They know I'll find it again."

She left.
