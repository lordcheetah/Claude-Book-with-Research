# Chapter 6: The Open Record

Five came the way it always came, without announcing itself.

Yara sat by the window with the cold cup forgotten on the sill and watched the last of the count fall away. There was no sound to it. The cascade went on stepping the water down weir by weir in the grey, the living walls breathed their faint warmth into the air that was just beginning to lift toward dawn, and on the screen the field that had read *armed* for seven hours simply read something else. A status changed. A confirmation hash resolved, a long clean string of characters she could not read and did not need to, and below it the line she had built the whole night toward: *deposited to public record.* That was all. The thing that could not be un-seen looked, on a screen at five in the morning, exactly like every other confirmed transaction she had run in ten years of correct work.

She sat with the smallness of it. She had braced for a feeling proportional to the size of the act and the size of the act had refused to provide one, the way a valve you have agonized over opens with the same quarter-turn as any other valve. *It's done,* she thought, and the thought was flat and true and carried no triumph in it. The clock under the deposit kept its own count, indifferent to the one that had just ended. Hour ninety-two elapsed. About twenty-two hours to chemical irreversibility, the curve out past the reeds climbing toward the line whether or not a record had just gone public, and now the two clocks ran side by side under the same dawn — the bloom's, which nothing she had done in the night had touched, and the Compact's, which she had just started and could not call back. She had moved one of them. The other was still moving on its own.

She did not look away from either.

---

The first pull came at nineteen minutes past.

She had not expected to watch it happen, but the deposit page held an access log, a plain ledger of who had reached into the record and when, and she found she could not stop reading it. For nineteen minutes it stayed at one — her own deposit, the act of placing the thing there. Then it ticked to two, and beside the two was a name and a credential she did not know: a water-systems researcher, the standing-line reading *cooperative hydrology, Nairobi bioregion.* A stranger. A person on the other side of the continent who had been asleep an hour ago and was now, at the start of their own morning, reaching into the New Accra estuary data because the public record had told them it was there.

Yara watched the line. Two became three. A monitoring cooperative in the delta cities, then a name with a university standing, then two more she could not place. Not a flood — the style guide of the thing was wrong for a flood, this was five in the morning across a sleeping network — but a string of single, real, separate eyes, landing one after another on the firmware delta and the forty-seven days of raw and the photographs of the hardware logs. Each one independent. Each one able to pull the figures and run them and arrive, with no reason to protect anything, at the same wrong number she had arrived at alone in the reeds three days ago.

Something in her chest that had been held tight since the screen first went grey let go, not all at once, but by degrees, the way a colony recovers — slowly, then visibly. The terror of the armed deposit had been a private thing, hers to carry alone in a kitchen at night. This was the opposite of private. *It's out,* she thought. *It's real. It's seen.* And the relief in it was not that she had won anything, because nothing was won yet and the bloom was still climbing, but that the truth was no longer only hers to hold. Strangers were holding it now. They could not be rotated off it. There was no door anyone could shut on a thing being read in Nairobi at the start of the day.

---

Del reached her soon after the light came up properly, by comm, his voice already in motion before she had the connection fully open.

"It's moving," he said. "Yara, it's moving, and it's moving faster than the inside ever moved. The Technical Council has an automated notification logged against the public deposit — automated, which is the word that matters, it means a person didn't have to choose to notice, the mechanism noticed on its own and it can't pretend it didn't." A breath. He was clipped this morning, short on the abundance, working close to the edge of his own words in a way she had not heard from him before. "And my secondment came back. Quietly. No message, no ceremony, I went to check the archive out of habit and the door just opened. Somebody upstairs restored it in the night. Somebody is trying to get ahead of this."

"How far ahead can they get," Yara said.

"Not far enough. That's the whole point of where you put it." She heard him stop, regroup, choose. "There's no statement yet. Altera, Rational Innovation, the Council — nothing public. But the machinery's turning. I can feel it turning from here." He did not go near Strand. The name had done its work in the package and he left it exactly where they had set it, closed and open at once, and she noticed that he left it and was glad. "I'll have hard news by midday. Real news, not the shape of it. Where will you be?"

She was already looking at the clock under the dead deposit, at the twenty-two hours that had not moved, not in nineteen minutes, not because she'd spent them watching strangers read a file. "At the inlet," she said.

A pause on the line, and in it she could hear him understand. "Of course you will."

---

The deposit firing had not moved the bloom one hour. That was the thing the screen could not tell her and the clock could.

She gathered the kit while the city was still deciding to wake, the field coveralls, the manual tools, the override key that was mechanical and stupid and answered to no credential at all. The Council's emergency team would mobilize — the mechanism would see to that, eventually, the way it had seen to the notification. But *eventually* and *twenty-two hours* were two different numbers, and the gap between them was exactly the size of the thing she could do with her own hands. The manual biofilter override needed no permission. It needed access and competence, and the suspension had taken neither. They had taken the credential, not the competence. They had never been able to reach the competence.

She went down through the terraces into the morning. The city was just beginning its ordinary day — the flatbread cart unfolding its awning, a maintenance crew checking a weir on the lower cascade, children moving toward the commons in a loose bright stream, a woman watering the trough plantings outside her door. None of them knew. The record had gone public in the night and the bloom was twenty-two hours from a line it could not come back from and the city went on lifting its shutters and warming its ovens, entirely unaware that something underneath it had changed while it slept. *It still believes in itself,* Yara thought, the way it had at every dawn she'd ever walked out into, and she carried the kit down through it and out toward the reeds, and did not have the heart to wish it knew.

---

The eastern inlet smelled the way it always smelled, green and wet and mineral, alive in the particular way of a wetland doing its enormous patient work, and she went down into it.

The manual suppression sequence was the kind of labor no one wrote songs about. It was a procedure walked by hand, station by station, valve by valve, a thing she knew the way she knew the rhythm of her own breath — the firmware had been masking the colonies' distress and refusing to let the biofilter respond, and so she would do by hand, belatedly and exactly, what the system should have been doing on its own for four months. She keyed the mechanical override at Station Seven and felt the manifold answer through her gloves, the small honest shudder of a gate moving when you tell it to move. She crouched at the intake and read the manual gauges, the analog ones that lied to no one because no one had taught them to.

She worked the sequence into the morning, the sun climbing the reed-tops, the mud taking her to the shins. At each station she walked the same careful steps: isolate the masked range, force the suppression dose the firmware had been suppressing, hold, read the manual gauges, wait for the colonies to take the help. It was slow. It had to be slow — push the suppression too hard and you killed the very organisms you were trying to free to fight. So she went at the pace the living system could bear, which was the only pace that worked, her hands sure on valves she had maintained for ten years, her knees in the cold inlet water, the green-mineral air going in and out of her with the work.

And the system began, very slowly, to respond. At Eight the manual cyano count came down a hair off the climb, then a hair more, the curve she had watched bend the wrong way in her kitchen beginning, under her hands, to bend back. She did not let herself feel it yet. She read it three times, the way she read everything, and it held three times, and only then did she let the smallest part of the satisfaction in — not relief, not victory, but the specific deep quiet of a system responding correctly to correct intervention, the thing she had become a water engineer to feel. She moved to Nine and did it again. The mud, the smell, the weight of the kit on her shoulder, the gauge needles answering. This was the work. This had always been the work. No one was watching her do it, which was, she thought, exactly as it should be.

---

Del came down the wetland path at midday and found her at Station Seven, the sequence walked, the worst of the curve bent, washing inlet mud off her gloves in the channel.

He did not open with a joke. He stood at the edge of the reed line with the sun behind him and set the news down the way a man sets down something he has carried a long way and needs both hands free of. "All right," he said. "Here it is, all of it, because you'll want it once and complete and not in pieces. Altera's certification contracts are frozen — every one, citywide, pending investigation. The certification review opened formally an hour ago, and Strand's signature is part of the record it's examining, exactly the way the package framed it. A name on a signature, in the file, for the Council to look at. Not our accusation. Their evidence now." He let that be precise and bounded and did not push it past where it could go. "Rational Innovation issued a denial. Public, fast, lawyered — no involvement, no knowledge, full cooperation. The investigation continues regardless of the denial, which is the part the denial can't touch."

"Good," Yara said. She kept washing the gloves. "And the rest of it."

"And the rest of it." He came a few steps closer, off the path, onto the boards, and she heard the register change, careful now, watching her face the way he did when the human angle was the one that mattered. "Nwosu resigned. This morning. In the internal process — she stepped down before the review could reach her."

Yara stopped washing the gloves. She did not look up right away.

"They're saying she protected the process and the city's name," Del went on, careful, laying it flat the way Yara herself laid things. "Not that she was part of any sabotage — there's nothing in the record that says that, and I don't think it's true, and neither do you. The story going around inside is that she held a flag back, judged it her call to make, and let the process run instead of the alarm. That's the shape the resignation statement gives it. Nobody's calling it more than that, and nobody can prove it's less." He paused. "Whatever it actually was, it's not one she could stand behind anymore. Not one she could ask anyone else to stand behind. So she stepped down."

Yara straightened. She looked out across the inlet, at the reeds, at the place where the curve had bent back under her hands, and she let the thing land — not as confirmation of anything she could have proved, because Del had been careful and the careful version was the true one. Nwosu had not confessed. She had done a thing that fit the shape of what Yara had inferred in her kitchen, and the shape was all the answer there would ever be, and Yara found she did not need more than the shape. *I told myself,* the woman had said, and not finished it, and this was the end of that unfinished sentence: not the words she would never say, but the office she had just walked out of.

It cost what the call had cost, and more, because the call had been the warning and this was the thing the warning was about. Ten years. A woman who could still read a sensor log faster than half the staff, who had been wrong in a way that trapped her, who was not corrupt and was not coming back. Yara let it be that for a moment, alone with it in the smell of the inlet, the small private grief of an account closed in the only way it could close. Not triumph. Not vindication. Closer to the cost finally being paid by the person who had incurred it, which was its own cold and exact kind of justice, and no comfort at all.

"She read a log faster than anyone I worked under," Yara said, after a while. It was the whole of the eulogy. Del did not add to it, which was right.

---

The Council's emergency team reached the inlet not long after, and they came in late and well, which was the most the clock had allowed.

There were six of them, in proper response gear, hauling the powered equipment she had done without — and they stopped at the reed line when they saw the manual gauges already set and the override keyed and the curve, on their own first read, already off the climb. The lead, a broad woman with a regional Authority patch and the harried competence of someone who had run the whole way, crouched at the Station Seven intake and read the manual sequence Yara had walked, and read it again, the way you read a thing you weren't expecting to find.

"This is done by hand," she said. It was not quite a question.

"It's done as far as Nine," Yara said. "Manual suppression, walked the colonies up off the masked range. I held the dose under the kill threshold, so it's slow, but it's holding."

The woman stood. She looked at the inlet, then at the clock she would have run in her own head on the way out, the same arithmetic Yara had run in the kitchen, and Yara watched her arrive where the arithmetic arrived. "If this hadn't been started this morning," the lead said, plainly, professionally, no ceremony in it, "we'd be standing in a remediation that doesn't work. We're inside the window because somebody put us inside it before we got here. That's the only reason this is recoverable and not a loss." She did not call Yara a hero. She put it in the language of the work — *inside the window, recoverable, not a loss* — and went to brief her team, and that was the truest thing anyone could have said and the only kind of thing Yara would have been able to stand hearing.

The narrowness of it was specific and earned. A few hours later in the morning and the colonies would have crossed the line while the powered gear was still being unloaded. The gap between the deposit firing and the official response arriving had been exactly the size of one engineer with a mechanical key and no permission, and she had stood in the gap, and the gap had closed. That was all. The work had done what it was supposed to do, because she had done it.

---

She and Del walked the wetland path back toward the Upper Terraces in the early afternoon light, and neither of them remarked that they had walked it before.

"So here's what comes next," Del said, and the abundance was coming back into him now, the pressure off enough to let him think out loud again, which she had missed. "The review takes months. Real months — the careful way, applied to the careful way's own failure, which is going to be uncomfortable for everyone and exactly correct. People will be held accountable through a process, not a headline, and the process the Compact triggered is the same process that nearly buried us. That's the thing I keep turning over." He gestured at the city ahead, the terraces catching the afternoon. "Correct process is what almost killed us. A report goes into a queue and a careful person decides it can wait and the careful machinery runs and the bloom climbs. And correct process is also what saved us — a different door, the same founding commitment to doing things right, and it can't be un-filed. Same machine. We just used the part of it they forgot was a machine."

"The part that was never theirs to shut," Yara said.

"That part." He walked a few steps. "Two things we don't get to finish, and I want to say them plainly so we're not pretending. The Altera director — whoever sat on that board and made the contract — that's a name for the Council's investigation now. Subpoena power, financial records, the whole apparatus we don't have and shouldn't try to be. We took it as far as two people on a bench can take a thing honestly. The rest belongs to the process that exists to hold it." He said it cleanly, a handoff and not a surrender. "I'm content to let it. That's new for me. I used to want to chase those all the way down myself."

She glanced at him. There was something underneath the way he said it — Rational Innovation, the review, the reckoning coming for the people whose talking points he had once known by heart — something settled, an account she had never seen the opening of, closing now in the ordinary afternoon. He did not explain it and she did not ask. It was a note in the room, no more, and she let it be one.

"You earned letting it," she said, which was as close as either of them would come.

He almost smiled. "Don't tell anyone."

At the foot of the terraces he peeled off toward the Authority, to begin the long unglamorous business of being reinstated, the paperwork of a door reopening. He left her at the path's mouth without making anything of it, the way he had let her go to Nwosu alone, and she watched him go and understood the leaving as the same thing it had been then: a thing he trusted her to do without him.

---

Later, alone, she went back to Station Seven.

The light had gone long and gold over the reeds, the afternoon settling toward evening, the emergency team finished and gone, the inlet quiet again and doing its own enormous patient work. She crouched at the intake manifold, the way she had crouched at the start of all of it, and ran the sensor check — the same motion, the same hands, the first thing she had done in the first chapter of this and the last thing she would do in it now.

The biofilter at Station Seven smelled right. The particular green-mineral-wet of healthy mycorrhizal colonies doing their work, exactly as it had that first morning. She ran the numbers off the manual gauges, once for shape and once for the figure that mattered, and read them, and ran them again because reading a thing twice was simply who she was.

The smell was right and the numbers were right.

She held that for a moment, the two of them agreeing for the first time in six days, the lie gone out from between them. Then she made the log entry, plain and factual and in her own hand on the station terminal that answered to her again — the date, the station, the manual sequence walked, the readings recovering, the system returned to certified function. Routine. Boring. True. The central object of the whole long week, the thing that had been deleted and falsified and fought over, doing nothing now but its proper ordinary work: recording that a system was running the way a system should.

She closed the terminal and stood. She took a breath of the station's air — green, wet, mineral, alive — and let it fill her all the way, and held it, and let it go. Out past the reeds the colonies went on doing what they had been built to do, converting the world's hurt into more of themselves, certified and steady and unaware of anything that had been risked to keep them so.

Then she shouldered the kit and walked the wetland path back toward the city, the late light on the water beside her, the terraces ahead beginning to warm their lamps against the coming dark. Everything was in order. She had written it down because it was true.
