# Chapter 3: The Pattern

The maintenance path read differently at night.

Yara came along it with her field kit over one shoulder and a hand light she hadn't switched on, letting her eyes do the work. She'd walked this stretch in every kind of light over ten years, and her feet knew it the way they knew the stairs in her own building. The reeds were a black mass on either side, taller than her in the wet season, breathing out the cool damp they held all day. Out in the water the biofilter stations sat as small lit posts, evenly spaced, patient. The same view as two evenings ago, except now she was on the wrong side of her own assignment, off-shift and off-rotation, walking toward stations that were no longer hers to walk toward.

Her credentials still opened the housing at Station Seven. That was the whole geometry of it. The assignment had been lifted; nothing had been revoked. The door read her badge and unlocked without an opinion, the way it had a thousand times.

She did it quietly anyway.

She crouched at the manifold before she touched the kit, and breathed the station in. That was always first. Green, wet, mineral, and under it the faint biological warmth of the colonies turning over substrate — the same warmth she trusted more than any number, because the colonies couldn't be told what to say. At night the station was quieter on the air. The smell was more closed-in, more itself, the way a kitchen smells of the day's cooking after the cooking is done. But it was correct. She noted that, as she always did, before she let herself look at anything.

Then she pulled up the embedded hardware log on her field kit, off the hard line, and went to find her own handwriting.

It was there. Day One evening, exactly where she'd left it, exactly as she'd written it: *Anomaly pattern confirmed across independent sensor networks. Physical samples pending, lab ref EI-S7-2091-0605. Prior report ref EI-2091-447 not appearing in system — investigating.* Sitting in the hardware out in the reeds, in the one log that lived in physical space and answered to no aggregation feed and no incident queue. She read it back twice. Whatever had reached into the incident system and closed over her report had not reached here, because reaching here meant coming to the inlet in person, and nobody had.

She photographed it. The log page, the timestamp, the text in her own phrasing. A small thing, and it steadied her. One thread they hadn't been able to touch.

She moved on to Station Eight.

The path between stations ran on a raised boardwalk over the reed beds, the planks soft underfoot with the day's rain. Station Eight smelled the way Seven had — correct, closed-in, the colonies quiet and working. Its feed would be claiming nominal right now, the same calm lie it had been telling for forty-seven days. She wasn't here for the feed. The feed was the part that had been gotten to. She was here for the part that hadn't.

She opened Station Eight's housing and pulled up the embedded hardware log. Not the sensor stream — the physical log, the one maintenance technicians wrote to by hand at in-person service visits, the record that lived inside the station and got signed and stamped when a real person stood where she was standing now. It went back years. Routine service entries, calibration sign-offs, a filter-medium replacement she remembered authorizing herself two seasons ago. She read down through it the way she read everything, not line by line but as a shape, watching for the place the shape went wrong.

It went wrong four months back.

A firmware synchronization entry. A timestamp, a tag ID, a certification reference number. None of that was strange in itself — stations took firmware updates; updates got logged. What stopped her was the tag.

The format was wrong. Not the Water Authority's tag format, the one she'd been reading her whole career. A third-party tag, a structure she didn't recognize, sitting in the physical log of a Water Authority station. And the date matched nothing in the maintenance schedule she had access to. It was a service visit the operational calendar didn't record. Someone had come to Station Eight four months ago, in person, opened this housing, written to this log — and the calendar said no one had been here at all.

Yara went very still.

She stayed that way for a while, crouched at the open housing, the hand light still off in her pocket, the entry steady on the kit's screen in front of her. People who'd worked with her had learned that this stillness was not absence. She was reading. A real person had stood at this station, out of schedule, off the record, and synchronized firmware to it under a tag that belonged to no part of the Authority she knew. The smell was correct. The feed was nominal. And four months ago someone had been here to make sure it stayed that way.

She photographed it carefully. The log page. The entry. The tag format, close enough to read the structure. Then she took out the paper notebook — the one she'd carried since the Institute, the one whose contents didn't disappear from anyone's queue — and copied the certification reference number out by hand, digit by digit, checking it twice against the screen.

Station Nine was four hundred meters further along the boardwalk. She went.

Nine smelled right too, and Nine's physical log had the same wrong tag. A different date — three days off from Eight's — but the same format, the same off-schedule firmware sync, the same certification reference pattern. Two of the three estuary-adjacent stations carried the trace in physical space. She photographed Nine's entry and wrote its number beneath Eight's, and then she stood up in the reed beds at midnight with images of a thing that should not have been in the hardware log at two of the three affected stations. She let the boardwalk take her weight, and breathed.

On the path back she stopped once.

The Upper Terraces stood lit against the night, solar tiles dark now but the living walls underlit a deep green, terrace stacked over terrace the way they were in the day except softer. The city carried on its work in the dark without knowing what was being done to it down here at its edge. The treatment ponds below the terraces held the lights doubled and still. It was beautiful the way it was always beautiful, and the beauty was not the point and was exactly the point.

*Sixty-six hours,* she thought. She kept walking.

---

Del's office in the Upper Terraces was warmer than hers in every sense — better coffee, a lamp instead of overheads, the lived-in clutter of a man who actually worked where he sat. Yara arrived a little after seven with the night still in her, her photographs and her notebook in the kit bag, and Del already at the desk with a stack of printed certification records squared up beside his screen.

"Sit," he said. "Coffee's real. Tell me you found something, because I found something and I want to go second."

She didn't sit yet. She laid the photographs on the desk one at a time — Seven's hardware log note intact, then Eight's anomalous entry, then Nine's — and put the notebook open beside them, the two reference numbers in her own hand. "Physical logs," she said. "The embedded ones, the ones techs write by hand at service visits. Eight and Nine both have a firmware sync from four months ago under a tag that isn't Authority format. Off-schedule. The operational calendar doesn't show anyone visiting either station." She set her finger by the numbers without quite touching the page. "Those are the certification reference numbers off both entries."

Del looked at the numbers for a moment. Then he turned one of his printed records around so it faced her and laid it beside the notebook, and put his finger on a line near the top.

The number matched.

"Here's the thing," he said, and sat back. "I'm going to tell you what I found in order, because the order is the part that matters." His hands had started moving, sketching the shape of it in the air over the desk. "First thing. Stations seven, eight, and nine all went through a full biotech-node recertification four months ago. Recent, but not strange — you recertify when firmware changes or when hardware gets modified, that's the rule, that's what it's for. It passed. Clean sign-off at the Accord level, a senior certifying analyst's name on it." A small pause there, a half-beat she noticed and filed; he moved past it. "All three stations, one certification cycle."

"Same firm?" Yara said.

"Same firm. Second thing." He tapped the printed record. "Altera Technical Services. Certified technical contractor, current Accord standing, all the right boxes. I'd never heard of them, which means nothing — there are dozens of contractors, I can't name half of them either. So I pulled their file." His hand drew a flat horizontal line in the air, then a short one. "Third thing, and this is the one that made me sit up. The recertification was *fast.* All three stations, one site visit, a single day. That's at the outer edge of feasible for one team. Not impossible. But fast."

Yara looked from his printed record to her photographs. "The tag in the physical log is a four-month-old firmware sync. Your certification record is a four-month-old recertification. Same reference number." She let it settle into place out loud, the way she did when she was certain. "Altera physically came to the stations. They opened the housings. They wrote to the hardware logs. The certification is the cover for a site visit that actually happened."

"That's where I landed at about three this morning." Del turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on the desk. "The cert record has a firmware version number on it — the version Altera certified as installed. So I have what they say went in." He looked at her. "What I don't have is what's actually running now. That needs your side."

"That's a passive read." Yara sat down at last and pulled her tablet from the kit. "I can pull the current firmware signature off the nodes through my credentials without touching anything. It's a read, not a write." She was already laying it out as she talked, the comparison she'd want, the two versions side by side. "You give me the certified version. I pull the running version. We diff them."

It took her a few minutes. Del watched her work and, unusually, didn't fill the time with talk; he turned his cup, and waited, and let her have the silence she worked in. She pulled the running firmware signature from each of the three nodes in turn, the passive read returning clean, and set the certified version against the running version, and mapped the delta.

They differed. Not everywhere. In one narrow, specific place.

"It's not disabled," she said. She kept her voice level, the way she kept it level at a manifold when the numbers argued with the smell. "The sensors aren't off. Almost all of it passes through exactly as it should — flow, residuals, colony activity, all of it, untouched, accurate." She moved her finger down the delta on the screen. "There's one thing it does. Cyanobacteria load. Below a set concentration, the reading passes through normally — that's why the wet-season fluctuations look real, because they are real, they're under the threshold. Above the threshold, the firmware replaces the actual reading with a synthesized nominal value before the data leaves the node. It doesn't suppress the sensor. It substitutes a number for the one specific number it doesn't want anyone to see."

Del had gone quiet. His hands had stopped.

"And the coherence signal," Yara went on. "The colonies are responding to the bloom, Del. The suppression response is there — they're fighting it, doing exactly what they're for. The firmware is masking that too, by the same layer, before it reports. It takes the colonies' actual response and substitutes a nominal coherence value on its way out." She sat back. "That's the flat coherence. It was never flat. We were reading the lie."

The silence held a beat longer than Del's silences held. She had learned that about him — he was a man who filled a low place the way water did — and he didn't.

"It's not broken," he said finally. "It's been taught to lie about one thing."

"One thing, precisely." Yara turned the tablet so he could see the delta. The modification was precise where it needed to be and nowhere else — a blind spot built into a specific reporting range, a specific signal, everything around it left honest so the station read alive and well. *A surgeon's modification, not a vandal's.*

That precision answered the first question she'd carried since the morning she caught it.

The aggregation. She'd traced the anomaly through the aggregation pipeline that first day, by hand, step by step — a raw reading going in elevated and coming out flat. She'd thought the bug lived there, in the normalization or the threshold logic, a blind spot folding the cyano signature into the baseline. The simplest explanation that fit.

It had fit because the aggregation looked normal, and it looked normal because it *was* — doing its job correctly on data that had already been sanitized before it ever arrived. The lie was upstream of everything she'd checked. The node was substituting the number at the source; the aggregation had only faithfully processed a clean-looking input into a clean-looking output. No aggregation error. She'd been auditing the wrong end of the pipe.

"It wasn't the aggregation," she said. "It was never the aggregation. The data came to it already clean."

"So whoever built this," Del said slowly, working it into his own language, "expected the bloom to come on slowly enough that the masking window would hold until it was too late to do anything. The station keeps reading healthy right up to the point where healthy isn't recoverable." He drew the threshold in the air, a flat hand, then lifted it. "And it passed an Accord certification. Which means either the certifying analyst never tested the specific signature range that got modified —" He stopped there. He didn't finish it. The alternative didn't need saying.

---

Yara was already looking at something else. She had the station specifications open beside the firmware delta, scrolling, and the small frown that meant she'd noticed before she could say why.

The firmware was the same in all three — same masking layer, same threshold, seven, eight, nine identical. The hardware was not. She read the spec sheets the way a musician reads a score, and the discrepancy had jumped off the page at her. Stations seven and eight ran nodes from one manufacturer, Cascavel Biotech, standard Covenant-certified kit from a procurement cycle six years back. Station nine ran hardware from Northbrook Biosystems — a different year, different specs at the component level. Same job, different machine underneath.

"That's a question," she said, and turned the sheets toward Del. "Seven and eight on one supplier's hardware, nine on another. But the modification is identical across all three." If someone had gotten in by exploiting a flaw in a particular supplier's equipment, the modification would be shaped around what each platform allowed. Seven and eight would look like one thing, nine like another. They didn't. "Two different hardware lines, one identical modification. I want to know how that's possible before I trust any of it."

"Then let's know how." Del pulled his keyboard over. "I've got the Accord's certified hardware registry. I can check both manufacturers — flagged firmware vulnerabilities, irregular supply-chain reports, anything in the last two years." His hands moved while he talked. "Because if Cascavel or Northbrook shipped compromised kit, that's a different story entirely. That's a supply-chain problem, that's the modification arriving inside the equipment before it ever reached the inlet, and that's — frankly that's almost worse, that's a hundred stations we don't know about."

Yara pulled the full hardware specifications for both suppliers through her own credentials while Del worked the registry. They went quiet for a few minutes, the two of them reading in parallel.

"Cascavel's clean," Del said. "Certified, inspected, no anomaly flags, no irregular reports. Two years back, nothing."

"Northbrook the same?"

"Northbrook the same. Boring. Both of them boring." He looked up. "Which leaves your question standing. How does one modification fit two machines?"

Yara had the answer by then; she'd run the precise check while he read the registry. She set the firmware delta against the hardware specs for both manufacturers, side by side, and looked for the layer where the modification lived — whether it reached into anything specific to Cascavel's architecture, or to Northbrook's. It didn't. It sat above the hardware, at the monitoring-software level, indifferent to the machine beneath it. Which was exactly why it ran identically on both. The substrate was irrelevant to it.

"It fits two machines because it doesn't care about the machine," she said. "It would run on any certified monitoring hardware in the network. The supplier difference is procurement history. It's not the vector."

Del sat back, and she watched him arrive where she'd arrived. "So it wasn't opportunistic," he said. "They didn't find a hole in one supplier's equipment and work with what the hole let them do. There was no hole. They brought the modification with them and installed it on whatever hardware was already standing in the inlet, and it didn't matter which hardware that was."

"Deliberate," Yara said. "Specific. They knew what they were putting in and where they were putting it, and they didn't need the equipment to cooperate. The site visit was the vector. Nothing upstream of it." She closed the comparison. The dead end had sharpened the picture rather than muddying it — the kind that ruled out a whole class of explanation and left the remaining one standing clearer than before. "It came through Altera's door at the stations. Not through the supply chain. Through the certification."

---

Del had been pulling something else while she finished the hardware check — she'd half-noticed the corporate-record header on his screen and let him work. Now he stopped, and read, and went quiet in the particular way that meant he'd found the bottom of something.

"Here's a name you haven't heard yet," he said.

He didn't editorialize. He turned the screen toward her and let her read it.

Altera Technical Services' corporate registration. A standard public business record, plain as anything — the simplicity of it almost striking, after everything else. A single listed director. The director's name. And below it, the line that had stopped him: the director's other listed directorships. One entry.

A board seat at Rational Innovation — Infrastructure Advisory Group.

Yara read it twice. Then she looked at Del. "Rational Innovation."

"I've heard of them," Del said. "Distantly." He didn't go on. It was precise, that restraint — Del who filled every silence was choosing to let this one land before he poured language over it. He picked up the pen instead.

On the notepad in the middle of the desk, the empty square he'd drawn yesterday afternoon was still there, off to the side from the bloom clock and the timestamps. The *why* he'd wanted to write down that he didn't have. He didn't fill it in now. He wrote *Rational Innovation* a little apart from it, in clear letters, and drew a single line from the square to the name.

"Is that good or bad?" Yara asked.

"I don't know yet." Del looked at the line he'd drawn. "But it's a board seat. And a board seat is a choice — nobody ends up on one by accident, somebody sat down and decided to be in that room." He turned the cup once on the desk, slow. "A one-director firm does a fast certification on three estuary stations and installs a modification that teaches them to lie about exactly one number. And the one director sits on the board of something called an Infrastructure Advisory Group."

They had the vector now, the firmware installed by Altera under cover of a certification visit four months ago. They had the clock, sixty hours and running. They had a name, and the certainty that it meant something, and no idea yet what it meant or anyone left to take it to who hadn't already closed the door.

"Let me find out what they actually do," Del said.

The square on the notepad wasn't empty anymore. It had a line drawn out of it to a name that hadn't been explained — the first shape the *why* had taken.
