# Chapter 1: Station Seven

The biofilter at Station Seven smelled right, which was the first thing wrong.

Yara Osei came up the maintenance path on foot, field kit over one shoulder, the reeds still black against a sky that hadn't decided to be morning yet. The wet season air sat on her skin like a second coverall. She'd walked this path a few hundred times, and she still watched where she put her feet, still read the inlet on the way in — the set of the water, the lean of the reed beds, the small constant sounds of a wetland doing its work. Reading a station started before you reached it.

She crouched at the intake manifold and breathed it in before she touched anything.

Green. Wet. Mineral, under that, the clean cold of treated water. And beneath all of it the faint biological warmth that meant the mycorrhizal colonies were alive and busy — the particular smell of fungal networks breaking down whatever the inlet brought them. The smell of a healthy station. Always her first reading, and the one she trusted most, because the colonies couldn't lie the way a sensor could.

The smell was correct. She noted that, and ran the sweep anyway.

Her field kit talked to the station through the manifold port. Flow rate: nominal. Chemical residuals: within band. Fungal colony activity index: nominal, the colonies turning over substrate at the rate they should for the hour and the season. Cyanobacteria load — she watched the number resolve — slightly up. Not alarm-level. Below the notification threshold, the kind of reading the system logged and didn't bother anyone about. A few points of elevation in the cyano count, the sort of thing the wet season pushed around all the time.

Except it shouldn't have been up. Not now, not at this point in the season, not at this station. And there was the other number, sitting right beside it, calm as anything.

*Mycorrhizal signal coherence: nominal.*

Yara stayed very still, crouched at the manifold with the kit balanced on her knee, and looked at the two readings together.

They couldn't both be right. If the cyanobacteria load was genuinely climbing, the colonies would be answering it — the coherence index would show the suppression response, the network tightening as the fungal mat moved to choke the bloom back down. That was what the colonies were *for*. A rising cyano load and a flat, untroubled coherence reading was a sentence with no verb. One of those numbers was describing a station that didn't exist.

She ran the sweep again.

Same result. Cyano up, coherence nominal, the two of them lying side by side and pretending not to know each other.

She unclipped the manual diagnostic port cover — the small one most people forgot was there — and ran it a third time off the hard line instead of the wireless interface, sensor to kit, nothing in between to garble it. The numbers came back the same. So it wasn't the wireless. It was either the station or the truth, and the station smelled fine.

*The smell is right and the numbers are wrong,* she thought, *and that means the smell is lying, or the numbers are.* She didn't like either option. She liked the third one even less, the one she wasn't articulating yet — that something was telling the station what to say.

She took physical samples. Always took physical samples when a reading argued with itself; you couldn't run a lab assay on a number. She drew three from the inlet outflow, capped them, labeled each one by hand — site, station, date — because the day she trusted an auto-label was the day a sample went missing in the queue and she had nothing. EI-S7-2091-0605. She stowed them in the kit's cold pocket.

Then she added a flag to the station log. Routine phrasing, because routine was what she had: *Anomalous sensor correlation. Manual check required. Samples taken for lab.* She didn't mark it urgent. Two numbers that disagreed and a smell she trusted — that was a thing to look into, not a thing to wake anyone over.

She straightened, capped the manifold, and started back.

The dawn came up over the Upper Terraces while she walked, and for a moment she let herself see it. The solar tiles on the high rooftops caught the first light and threw it back in long sheets, terrace stacked over terrace, the living walls a darker green coming awake between them. Down here at the city's edge the treatment ponds held the same brightening sky, doubled and still. New Accra looked, the way it always looked at this hour, like a city that believed in itself.

She'd grown up believing cities could be like this. Most mornings she forgot to notice. This morning she noticed for about the length of three breaths, filed it where she filed things that mattered, and kept walking.

At the transit stop she sat on the bench and pulled up Station Seven's log on her tablet — the last thirty days, raw. She told herself she was being thorough. She was, but that wasn't the whole of it.

The pattern was there when she went looking. Minor cyano elevation, nominal coherence. The same impossible pairing she'd read off the manifold this morning, appearing four times in the last three weeks. Each one below the alert threshold. Each one logged and not followed up.

She'd seen three of them herself, in passing, in the daily summaries. She hadn't followed up either. Below threshold, and there had always been something else.

The transit pod came whispering up to the platform. She got on and didn't look at the view.

---

The lab queue took the samples without comment — standard intake, results in twenty-four hours, the assay tech nodding at her over the counter the way he nodded at everyone. Yara logged the chain of custody, kept the paper slip, and went down to her terminal.

Her office was on the mid-terrace floor of the Water Authority building, a functional box from the sixties with a window that looked out over the treatment ponds and a chair that had surrendered to use years ago. She didn't spend much time in it. The work was in the field. The office was where the field came to be written down.

She pulled the last thirty days for all eight eastern inlet stations and laid them out as a comparative grid, one column per station, raw readings only. It took the screen a moment to render. Then she sat back and read it the way she'd read a sensor log her whole career — not number by number, but as a shape, hunting for the place where the shape went wrong.

Column seven went wrong. Small intermittent cyano elevations, and the coherence trace running flat beside each one, untroubled, as if the colonies hadn't noticed a thing. She'd known that already. What she wanted now was the rest of the network for comparison.

She brought up the regional summary feed alongside it — the aggregated product, the clean version that senior staff read, that the Authority's reporting dashboard published, that the Accord's monitoring systems pulled for their own records. The summary was how Station Seven looked to anyone who wasn't standing at the manifold.

Station Seven, in the summary, was nominal. Fully nominal, across the whole thirty days.

Not flagged and dismissed. Not suppressed-with-a-note. The elevations weren't in the summary at all. Where her raw grid showed four small spikes, the summary showed a smooth, unbothered baseline — a station with nothing to report.

*That's interesting,* she thought, and the word landed the way it always did when she used it on herself. She paid attention.

She pulled the aggregation algorithm's processing log — the steps the system ran to turn raw station data into the summary product. The process looked standard. Intake, normalization, threshold filtering, regional roll-up. Nothing exotic. She took one of the anomalous readings, the cleanest of the four, and traced it through by hand, step by step, the way she'd trace a leak back along a pipe. Raw value in. Normalization, fine. And then somewhere between the normalized reading and the regional roll-up, the elevated cyano number stopped being elevated. It went into the baseline and came out the other side as nothing at all. Smoothed flat. Not deleted — reclassified, folded into the noise floor as if it had never been a signal.

She did it twice more, two other readings, and watched the same thing happen both times. The anomaly went in. The baseline came out.

She checked the other stations. If this was a processing fault, it might be everywhere. It wasn't everywhere. Stations Seven, Eight, and Nine — the three closest to the outer estuary, the three that met the open sea first — all showed the same thing. Small raw elevations, flat coherence, and a summary feed reporting all three as nominal. The other five eastern stations read clean in both the raw data and the summary, which was the difference between a station with nothing to report and a station being reported as having nothing.

She sat with that for a while. The treatment ponds went on holding the sky in the window.

She did not think the word she would later think. She thought the parsimonious thing, the thing the data actually supported: the aggregation algorithm had an error. Somewhere in the normalization or the threshold logic it was systematically misclassifying a particular chemical signature — the cyano elevation paired with the flat coherence — and rolling it into the baseline. A processing bug with a specific blind spot. It was the simplest explanation that fit, and Yara believed in the simplest explanation that fit, right up until it stopped fitting.

She wrote it down. *Working hypothesis: aggregation error, systematic misclassification of cyano signature, stations 7–9.* Then, because the hypothesis warranted documentation and because writing things down was a form of taking them seriously, she opened a new report in the Authority's incident-management system and started typing.

---

She titled it *Preliminary Anomaly Report: Eastern Inlet Stations 7–9, Recurring Sensor Correlation Discrepancy.* Bureaucratic specificity was a form of care. A vague title got read vaguely.

She wrote it the way she did everything — precise about what she'd seen, disciplined about what she hadn't. Four recurrence instances over three weeks, with timestamps. Three affected stations, all eastern-inlet, all estuary-adjacent. The discrepancy between the raw station logs and the regional summary feed, with the trace she'd done by hand laid out step by step so someone else could repeat it. She noted the two explanations the data would currently bear — possible aggregation error, possible systematic sensor miscalibration — and flagged both for review. She did not write that anyone had done anything. She had no evidence anyone had done anything. She had a feed that didn't match the stations under it, and a request: a calibration audit of stations seven through nine and a review of the aggregation logic that fed the summary.

She read it back once. It was accurate. It didn't over-claim. It would do.

The routing field wanted a recipient. Yara filled in Director Adaeze Nwosu without having to think about it. Nwosu ran the Authority's technical division and had for twenty years, long enough to be two people in one office — the engineer who could still read a sensor log faster than half the staff, and the operator who knew exactly which council member to call and exactly how much to tell them. Careful in a way Yara found occasionally frustrating and never wrong to rely on. If there was a real anomaly in the eastern inlet, Nwosu was who you took it to. She'd know what to do with it, and she'd know what it meant for whom — not the same knowledge, and Nwosu had both. Yara routed it to her queue.

"Yara." Tomas leaned in from the corridor, half a step into the doorway. "You're field-side tomorrow — any chance you sign off the maintenance window on Twelve before you go? Permits are sitting on me."

"Window's fine if the flow's diverted first," Yara said. "Divert, then I sign, then they cut in. Not the other way around."

"That's what I told them."

"Then tell them again. They did it backwards in March." He raised a hand and was gone, and she went back to the report.

She considered the urgency flag. She could move it up. Part of her wanted to — the part that had stood at the manifold this morning and felt the wrongness of a station saying the right thing in the wrong way. But she didn't have enough to justify it. She was precise about what the data supported, and an urgent flag on a medium-priority anomaly was a way of teaching people to ignore your urgent flags. She left it medium and submitted.

The system took it. *Report EI-2091-447 received. Routed to Director Nwosu for review. Estimated review window: 48 hours.*

Yara wrote the confirmation number in her field notebook — the paper one, the one she'd carried since the Institute, because numbers in your own hand didn't get lost in anyone's queue. EI-2091-447. She closed the terminal.

A normal working afternoon waited on the other side of it. Two more sites before end of shift, a flow check at the mid-wetlands node and a sensor swap at Eleven, the ordinary maintenance of a network that mostly ran as it should. The process would work. She'd filed it correctly, to the right person, with the right evidence; in forty-eight hours someone would look, and either find the bug in the aggregation logic or tell her where she'd missed it. That was how it went. The whole point of doing it right.

She gathered her kit and was reaching to close up when a knock at the open office door stopped her.

---

Del Marchetti stood in the doorway with the look of a man who'd been deciding whether to knock for longer than knocking would have taken. Tall, slightly rumpled, his Accord technical-staff jacket creased at the elbow as if he'd been leaning on something while he thought. They knew each other the way colleagues did who shared a brief and not an office — coordination meetings, the occasional overlapping site visit, enough to be glad to see each other and not enough to drop in unannounced. He'd dropped in unannounced.

"Yara. You have a minute? I'd have called, but —" He made a small arc with one hand, a gesture that seemed to indicate the general inadequacy of calling. "It's a looking-at-screens thing."

"I have a minute." She set the kit down.

"Here's the thing." He came in, didn't sit, pulled his tablet from under his arm. "I've been on the inlet monitoring brief for the Accord — the independent feed, our own sensors, separate from your network. Routine. And I keep getting three stations showing minor cyano elevations that don't show up in the Authority summary feed. Which is what our dashboard pulls from, so when I flag it up the chain, someone checks the summary, and the summary says nominal, and I look like a man flagging weather." He turned the tablet over in his hands without showing her the screen yet. "So I pulled your public monitoring data too. Also nominal. And I've been sitting with the question of how a station can be three things at once."

Yara didn't tell him what she'd found. Not yet. "Which three stations?"

"Seven. Eight. Nine."

She nodded, and gave him nothing, and asked the only question that mattered. "What do your raw readings show?"

He woke the tablet and turned it to her. She read it as a shape first. The shape was the same. Small intermittent cyano elevations, the coherence trace flat beside them. The same impossible sentence she'd read off her own grid an hour ago, except this was a different network entirely. The Accord's sensors, independently maintained, independently calibrated, sitting in the same water and seeing the same wrong thing.

"Same pattern," she said.

"Same pattern, different hardware." He watched her face. "Which I'd been telling myself was a coincidence, except I don't believe in that kind of coincidence, and I had a feeling you wouldn't either."

It ruled something out. If her network and his network — separately built, separately maintained — both saw the elevation in the raw data, then a calibration fault in the Authority's equipment couldn't be the whole story. Two independent sensor networks didn't drift the same wrong way by accident. The cyano was real. The colonies' flat response was real. And both feeds, hers and his, were clean by the time they reached anyone who'd read them.

She told him what she'd filed that afternoon. Precise, brief — the four instances, the three stations, the trace she'd done by hand from raw log to summary, the calibration audit she'd requested. He listened the way a certain kind of person listens: going quiet and still, recalibrating something behind his eyes.

"One question," he said. "Did you flag it as possible systematic suppression, or as calibration anomaly?"

"Calibration anomaly." She held his look. "That's what the data supports. So far."

He nodded, slowly, the way you nod at a careful answer you respect and wish were a little less careful. "Fair."

They took it out to the landing. The office had one chair and a screen bolted to a desk; the external staircase had a steel landing with a rail and a view of the inlet, and the light was going gold over the reed beds, and it was easier to think with two tablets and some air. They sat on the top step with their screens propped on their knees, her raw feed and his, and worked the comparison line by line — her instance against his, timestamp against timestamp, the elevations landing close enough together to be the same events seen by two different eyes.

"Here's what I keep coming back to." Del talked through it the way he seemed to think, out loud, his free hand drawing the shape in the air between them. "Your raw data shows the anomaly. Our raw data shows the anomaly. But the summary that goes to the Accord — the thing my dashboard reads — gets generated by an automated roll-up that pulls from the Authority's submission. From your side. So the clean version isn't being made on our end. By the time the data leaves your house, it's already clean." He turned to her. "Whatever's flattening these readings, it's happening before the data leaves the Authority's system."

Yara looked at the inlet, the water darkening, Station Seven a small lit post out among the reeds. "Before it leaves the Authority's system," she repeated. She didn't say the rest. He didn't either. Neither of them said *deliberate*. But the shape of the thing was standing up between them now, and they could both see it had a shape.

"Tomorrow," Del said. "I want to pull the historical feed — see how far back this goes. If it's three weeks, that's one kind of problem. If it's three months —" He let the hand finish the sentence.

"Bring the Accord's full raw feed. All three stations, last sixty days." She was already laying it out, the comparison she'd want, the columns she'd line up. "If your sixty matches my thirty, we've got the same thing twice from two networks, and that's a finding."

"Sixty days, three stations. Done." He stood, unfolding to his full slightly-creased height, and looked out at the city catching the last of the light — the terraces, the living walls going dim and green, the ponds holding the dusk. "You know, if we did escalate this properly, the Technical Council would want a full sensor log, a root-cause analysis, two independent certifications, and a signed declaration from the organisms themselves. The Council, which I love and serve faithfully, was built by people who assumed everyone meant well. Charming, really."

"How long?"

"To get the Council to move? Optimistically — weeks."

"I'll believe it when I see it," Yara said.

He laughed, short, the laugh of a man who'd just been told something true. "That's the right instinct. Tomorrow, then." He went down the stairs two at a time, jacket flapping, and she sat a moment longer on the landing watching the light go.

A good problem, she thought. They were both good at problems. That was the part that felt like crackling air. The other part — *before it leaves the Authority's system* — sat under it, small and cold, and didn't go away when she stood up.

---

She walked home along the public wetland path. She needed to think, and she thought better moving.

If the anomaly was in the Authority's own raw data and never reached the summary, then either the aggregation logic had a blind spot — her morning hypothesis, still the parsimonious one — or something between the raw log and the feed was choosing what not to pass on. She made herself run the second branch all the way, the way she'd run any branch she didn't like. Who had access to reach into that part of the system. Who had a reason to want three estuary stations to read clean when they weren't. No answers. She noted that she didn't have them, and kept the question.

The path came around to the stretch that overlooked the eastern inlet straight on, and she stopped. Station Seven sat out in the reeds, a small lit post, the water around it dark and still and exactly as it should be. From here she couldn't smell anything. The inlet looked fine. She knew the difference between looking fine and being fine; she'd learned it at a manifold this morning. But standing here in the dusk she could see why everyone upstream of the raw data believed the summary. The city looked like its own good news.

At her building she let herself into the courtyard and didn't go inside. She sat on the bench by the herbs she grew — basil and scent-leaf and a stubborn rosemary, their smell rising as the evening cooled — and opened the tablet one more time.

The lab queue first. Her samples, EI-S7-2091-0605, still pending; twenty-four hours standard, and it had been nine. Fine. The raw station feed next — the pattern still there, cyano up, coherence flat, unchanged. Fine. Then she opened the incident-management system to look at the report, EI-2091-447, meaning to attach a note about Del's independent observation while it was fresh.

The report wasn't in her queue.

She searched by number. *No record found.*

She searched by date. By station reference. By her own name as submitter. Nothing came back. She went to her account's submission history, the running list of everything she'd ever filed, and scrolled to today, and there was no today. No record that she had submitted anything at all.

Yara went very still, the way she did when she was thinking and people thought she'd left. She had not left. She was reading the absence the way she read everything else — as a shape, looking for the place the shape had gone wrong — except this time the wrong place was something that should have been there and wasn't.

She had the draft. In her local files; she always kept a local copy. She had the confirmation number in the notebook in her kit, in her own hand. The system had told her, four hours ago, *Report EI-2091-447 received.* And the system now held no record that she had ever filed it.

She didn't panic, because panic was a way of doing nothing energetically, and there was something she could still do. She opened a fresh urgent flag directly on Station Seven's raw data log — the embedded log in the station itself, the one action available to her that needed no submission queue and no aggregation feed and no one's review window. She wrote: *Anomaly pattern confirmed across independent sensor networks. Physical samples pending, lab ref EI-S7-2091-0605. Prior report ref EI-2091-447 not appearing in system — investigating.* She saved it locally. She saved it to the station's embedded log, where it would sit in the hardware out in the reeds, outside whatever had reached into the incident system and closed over her report like water closing over a stone.

She looked at the courtyard plants. The rosemary needed cutting back. She'd do it on the weekend.

She refreshed the incident queue one more time. *No record found.*

She'd keep the confirmation number. She always kept notes.
