# Chapter 5: Outside the System

Coffee, made by her own hands, in the grey before the courtyard woke.

Yara stood at the counter of her own small kitchen with the kettle ticking down beside her and let the morning be what it was — slow, domestic, hers. There was no office at the end of it. No station to walk out to in the dark with the kit over her shoulder. The day after the screen went grey had passed somewhere behind her, badly slept, the way she always slept badly. She had woken before any alarm she no longer had reason to set and made coffee the slow way, the way her mother made it, because it was the one part of the morning the review window could not reach. She poured it black and carried it out into the courtyard.

The herbs were doing what herbs did, which was the right thing without being asked. She crouched at the planter and ran two fingers along the underside of a scent-leaf, checking the new growth, the small careful gesture she'd make at a manifold without thinking. The rosemary had thrown out fresh tips at the cut she'd made the week before, reaching, and the basil had set its first flower spikes, which meant she'd let it go a few days too long. She pinched them off, one at a time, because a basil left to flower stopped putting its energy into leaf. The plants did not know she had been suspended. They went on running their own small certified systems, converting light and water into more of themselves, exactly as designed, and there was a steadiness in tending them that she took the way other people took prayer.

Then she went back inside and opened the data.

The personal drive woke under her hand — her own hardware, never touched by the registry, the 47-day raw feed and the lab results and the firmware delta sitting on it exactly where she'd left them. She'd moved a working copy off every network the night before suspension caught up with her, the same instinct that made her photograph a hardware log before she touched it, the same instinct that kept a confirmation number in paper. *They took the credential,* she thought, *not the competence.* The suspension reached the door. It did not reach the math.

She ran the bloom projection herself, off the local copy, the way she'd run it a dozen times in her career on systems no one was trying to hide. The model resolved on her own screen, unaggregated, unsmoothed, the curve climbing toward the line where the chemistry stopped being something the colonies could fight back from. She read the figure off the projection the way she read everything else, once for shape and once for the number that mattered.

About thirty-six hours to chemical irreversibility.

She held it without flinching. The early estimate — eighty-four, ±6 — had been honest when she made it, but the curve had tightened past the comfortable edge of its own error bars, the way these curves did once a bloom committed. Thirty-six. Not a thing to be alarmed at. A thing to be true about. The window had narrowed faster than the slow institutional clock could ever have moved inside it, which was the entire point of what had been done to the inlet, and which was also, now, no longer her institution's problem to be slow about. They had seen to that.

She set the cup down on the desk beside the drive and was very still for a moment, not because she was deciding anything. The deciding was done. It had finished the instant her credential icon went the flat dead grey of a sensor with nothing behind it, and what was left after a decision like that was not doubt. It was procedure. The how. And the how had a name she'd carried for years without ever needing it, a separate door, a credential that was hers and not theirs, and she would spend the day she had left walking toward it.

---

They met outside, because outside was what they had.

There was a public commons along the lower terrace where the cascade path widened into a flagged square — benches, a row of shade trees worked into the design, a vendor cart selling the morning's flatbread and a coffee that would not be as good as the one she'd made. People sat with tablets and children and each other. It was an ordinary place, exposed to the bright mid-morning, the kind of place anyone met anyone, and that was exactly why Del had named it. They used to meet in offices, behind credentials, in front of screens that belonged to the institution. Now they met on a public bench in the sun like two people with nowhere they were required to be, which, Yara thought, was the literal truth of it.

Del was already there when she came down the steps, a paper folder on his knee — actual paper, his certification records and the notepad with the filled-in square — and a flatbread he was eating with the dedication of a man who'd skipped breakfast to be early. He lifted it in greeting. "I'd offer you some, but you'd judge the bread, and you'd be right, and I'm not strong enough for that this morning."

"Then keep it." She sat. She set her own bag between her feet, the field notebook in it, the paper she did not let out of reach. "Let's inventory."

"Let's." He brushed crumbs off the folder and opened it, and the lightness went out of his voice and into the work, which was how he carried things — talk first, then the steadiness underneath. "Here's what's still ours. Your drive — local report copy, the lab assay, forty-seven days of raw, the firmware delta. All of it off-network, all of it before the freeze." He counted it on the table with two fingers. "The notebook. Your hand, the confirmation number, *not a misfile*, the hardware-log reference numbers from Seven and Eight and Nine. The photographs of the embedded logs — the off-schedule third-party sync entries, the reference numbers matching the cert. My printed certification records. The notepad with the why on it." He turned the page so the square faced her, the map drawn in his clear hand: *Altera → firmware → bloom → the failure is the point.*

"And what's gone," Yara said.

"And what's gone. Every credentialed door in the city. The official channel. The building." He laid his hand flat over the closed folder. "Which is the thing worth saying out loud, because it's the whole shape of where we are. Everything we lost was access. Everything that matters is on paper or on a drive you can hold in one hand. They froze the systems. They didn't freeze the evidence, because the evidence was never inside the systems they control." He looked at her. "Paper beats deletion. It's an old rule. It's holding."

"It's holding." She turned it over and it was true. The thing she'd saved into a station's hardware out in the reeds because the queue would not keep it — that instinct, three days running, had built them a case that lived entirely outside the reach of whatever could make a filing vanish. "So tell me the Compact. Properly this time. The whole mechanism. I want to know exactly what it does before I touch it."

"Right." Del set the flatbread down for good and brought his hands up, and they started moving, drawing the shape of it in the air over the table. "Here's the thing about the Compact, and I'll name it before I complicate it because the naming matters — it's a *right*. Covenant-era, founding-document, never repealed because nobody's ever had the nerve and it's load-bearing besides. Public access to ecological monitoring data. The premise was simple and it was correct: you cannot hold a whole population to a shared responsibility for the environment and then keep the environment's vital signs behind a wall they can't see over." His hand drew the wall and knocked it flat. "So the data carries a public right of access. Anyone with standing can deposit to the public record, and anyone at all can read it."

"Standing," Yara said. "Define it."

"Credentials tied to public-infrastructure work. Not to an assignment, not to an employer's say-so — to the work itself, the fact that you do certified labor on shared environmental systems." His finger came down on the table. "You have exactly that. It's the kind of credential they couldn't pull when they pulled your rotation, because it isn't theirs. It was never issued by the Authority. It was issued by the Covenant, to you, for being a person who does this work."

"I know." She'd checked it that morning, before the coffee was even cold — opened the credential on the personal drive and watched it answer, alive, untouched, the flat grey nowhere on it. "It's mine. It's not theirs to take. I checked it this morning, not as far as I know — I checked it. It's active."

Something eased in Del's shoulders that he didn't comment on. "Then here's the part that matters most." His hands went still, which they almost never did. "The deposit doesn't route the way a filing routes. It doesn't go to the incident queue. It doesn't go to the Technical Council. It doesn't go to anyone who's shown they can make a report disappear in four minutes. It goes to the *public record* — which is not a copy of the official record, Yara, it's a different record, with a different door, and that difference is the entire reason it's load-bearing." He let that sit.

"Once it's there, it's there. Independent parties can pull it and verify it. And —" he weighed how much to say "— a public deposit triggers certain institutional responses that an internal filing can't. Mandatory ones. I don't want to oversell the next part because the next part is exactly the thing we won't know until we do it. But the short version is: a thing in the public record cannot be un-seen the way a thing in a queue can be un-filed."

Yara sat with it, measuring the Compact against the system that had failed her, the way she'd measure a new reading against the one she already trusted. The official channel had received her report and then never heard of it. This was a different channel. Not a workaround, not a desperate hack at the edge of the rules — a designed right, sitting in the founding documents the whole time, built precisely for the morning the door got shut. "It's a right," she said. "Not a —" She stopped before the lazy word, because the lazy word was wrong and the wrongness mattered. "The institutions hold the public's environmental vital signs. The Compact says that when they shut the door, the public record is the door that was never theirs to shut. That's not me going around them. That's me using the part of the system they couldn't reach."

"That's it," Del said. "That's the whole of it. You're not outside the system. You're inside the part of it they forgot was there." He almost smiled. "Don't tell anyone I said something that earnest in a public square."

"Then we use it." She picked her bag up off the ground and set it on her knee. "Here's how we split it. You run your network on the analyst — somebody signed the Altera cert, and we don't have a name. I'll build the deposit package. We set it to go early, before the workday, when there's time to verify before anyone who'd want to stop it is awake." The clock was at thirty-four hours, running under the sunlight like a current under still water. "We don't have the days to do it twice. So we do it once, and we do it right."

---

Del found the name before midday.

He came back along the cascade path with his tablet in one hand and the specific expression of a man carrying something he didn't entirely enjoy holding. He sat back down on the bench, didn't open with a joke, which was itself information.

"I have a name," he said. "And a history, which is the part that's useful. Here's the first thing. The senior analyst who signed the Altera recertification — clean sign-off, three stations, single day, four months back — is a man called Emil Strand. Former Technical Council. Standards division." He turned the tablet to her, then turned it back, because the screen wasn't the point. "Dismissed about two years ago. A standards disagreement that went the wrong way for him."

"You know him," Yara said. Not a question. She'd watched the same thing cross his face in the office two days ago, the recitation that wasn't reading.

"I knew of him." Del turned his cup a quarter-turn, the old tell. "Here's the second thing, and then I'll move on, because it's not the story. The standards disagreement — I remember the shape of it. I was still close enough to those rooms, back then, to hear how it went. It was the kind of disagreement that happened to people who wouldn't sign things." A beat, and he let it be exactly one beat. "I once decided not to be one of the people who signed. So I noticed when it happened to someone who decided differently. That's all." He picked the cup back up, and that was him moving on, the way he did. "Now. The part that goes in the package."

"Tell me the part that goes in the package."

"He either tested the masked signature range and missed it, or he tested it and signed anyway." Del laid the two branches flat on the table, side by side, the way he'd lay any honest fork. "Negligence or complicity. We don't know which. We may never know which — that's a question for an investigation with subpoena power, not for two people on a bench." He looked at her. "And here's why it doesn't matter for us, which I know is going to sit wrong with you: the firmware delta doesn't care what was in his head. The selective-substitution rule is on the device or it isn't, and it is. A negligent sign-off and a complicit one both produce the same clean certificate over the same dirty firmware. His state of mind is the investigation's problem. The signature is ours, and the signature holds either way."

Yara turned it over and made herself resist the thing her own mind kept reaching for — the satisfaction of a culprit, a face to set against three days of damage. "He's not the author," she said, holding the line because the line was the truth and the satisfaction was not. "A fired analyst with a grievance against the certification system is exactly the tool you'd want if you were Rational Innovation. Aggrieved, adjacent, ready to hand. But finding the tool isn't finding the hand. The play is theirs. He's a joint in it, not the maker of it." She heard herself and added the harder part, because Del had taught her to. "If we let this become *we found the man who did it*, we've lost. The whole point is that no single man did it. An institution built an incentive and a man fit into it."

"That's the discipline," Del said. "Hold it all the way to the deposit." He tapped the folder. "But the name *is* good for something, and I want to be clear about what. It's the connective tissue. It's the human link between Altera's clean signature and the firmware that wasn't clean — and it tells us something we didn't have this morning, which is that their network runs through Council connections, not just contracting firms. A fired Council man turning up on an Altera cert isn't a coincidence and it isn't only about him. It's a map of where they reach." He wrote *Strand — former Council standards — signed Altera cert — branch open* in clear letters at the bottom of the page, and drew the small box around it, set apart. "I don't know what he knew. I know what he signed. The package can say exactly that and nothing more, and exactly that is enough."

---

She called Nwosu in the early afternoon, from her own kitchen, with the clock at thirty hours and the deposit half-built on the screen behind her.

She didn't have to call. There was no official reason for it — she had no channel left to file through, no permission to ask for, and she had stopped expecting Nwosu to be the answer to anything the moment her report came back *no record found*. She sat with the question of why for a while before she touched the connection, the way she sat with a reading she didn't like. Not to plead. Not to ask leave. Closer to this: she had respected this woman for ten years, had trusted her caution even when it frustrated her, had routed the report to her without a second thought because Nwosu was who you took a real anomaly to. And she was about to put something in the public record that would change all of that forever, and she found she could not do it without telling Nwosu first that it was real and that it was coming. Not a warning. A courtesy she wasn't sure was deserved and was going to extend anyway, because that was the kind of engineer she had wanted to be in front of, once.

The line connected. "Yara." Nwosu's voice came through and it was tired — not cold, not defended, just worn down to something under the usual competence. The warmth hadn't gone out of her. But something had settled into place behind it, set, the way a thing sets when it's been sat with too long. "I wondered if you'd reach me."

"Adaeze." Yara kept her own voice level. "The bloom is real. It's in the estuary stations and it's masked behind modified firmware and it's about thirty hours from chemical irreversibility. I think you know that. I think you've known it longer than I have."

A silence that wasn't denial. "The review process has protocols," Nwosu said. "It exists for a reason. You know that better than most — you've watched people panic a whole network over a reading that turned out to be condensation on a sensor housing." A breath. "Not every urgent flag is an emergency. Someone has to be the person who decides what rises and what waits. I have spent a career being that person. It is not the comfortable job. It is the necessary one."

"My report didn't wait," Yara said. "It vanished. From an append-only system. That takes Director-level access — there's exactly one tier of credential that can reach into that record, and it isn't mine. And the rotation that pulled me off the eastern inlet came from the same place." She didn't make it an accusation. She laid it down flat, the way she laid evidence, three facts in a row, and left the space after them open.

Nwosu did not fill the space with a denial. She filled it with something worse and more human. "There were considerations you weren't aware of," she said. "There always are, from where you sit. A field tech files a flag and it's the most important thing in the world, and it should be, that's what makes you good at the work. But I sit somewhere else. Escalating publicly before a proper review — before anyone had *confirmed* anything — that's not caution, Yara, that's choosing panic over process. Do you understand what an unconfirmed bloom scare does to a city that the whole Accord points to as proof the careful way works? I told myself —" She stopped. She had said *I told myself* and she did not finish it, and the unfinished sentence hung there with more weight than any finished one could have carried.

Yara recognized the shape of it — the same way she recognized a station's whole failure in two readings that couldn't both be true. *She thinks she managed something that needed managing.* That was the thing underneath the procedural language: a woman who had reached into the record and told herself it was temporary, told herself she would review it properly once the noise settled, told herself a field tech's urgent flag wasn't the correct escalation path. None of those sentences were quite lies. Each one was true as procedure. And each one also fit — too closely, said in this kitchen with the bloom thirty hours out — the shape of the deletion described from the inside, without the word.

Yara didn't think Nwosu had set out to bury anything. She thought Nwosu had been protecting the process, and the city's name, and — somewhere underneath both, in the place a person learns not to look at directly — herself: the woman who had made the call. Three things, collapsing into one. And the way Nwosu's own sentence had stopped mid-air, unfinished, suggested that some part of her knew it too — at least for the half-second before she caught herself.

She didn't say any of it. There was nothing in saying it. A confession she dragged out of this tired woman would not move the bloom one hour, and an accusation would only give her something to defend, and Yara had learned a long time ago not to repeat herself to people who were deciding not to listen. So she said the one thing she had called to say, flat, once.

"I need you to know that what I found is real," Yara said. "And it's going to be seen by someone who can act on it."

Nwosu did not answer. The silence came back down the line and stayed, and the not-answering was the loudest thing the woman had said in ten years, and Yara let it be the period at the end. Then she ended the call.

She set the tablet down on the counter, carefully, beside the cooling second cup of coffee she hadn't drunk. The kitchen was very quiet. She stood in it and let the thing land — not a system this time, not a grey screen, but a person she had respected, a door closing on ten years that would not open again, the cost of it real and not softened by anything she could tell herself. It was small and cold and precise, and it cost more than the suspension had cost. The suspension had been a machine. This was Adaeze, who could still read a sensor log faster than half the staff, who had been wrong in a way that had trapped her, who was not corrupt and was not coming back from it. Yara let it cost what it cost. She let it cost that for longer than one beat, this time, because a person was owed more than a system.

Then she filed it where she filed things that mattered, and turned back to the work, and the filing hurt going down in a way the work would have to carry for her now.

---

She built the deposit through the evening, into the dark, the clock falling to twenty-two hours and the city outside her window doing its ordinary work.

She assembled it the way she assembled everything, in order, each piece named. The firmware delta — certified version against running version, the selective-substitution rule isolated. The forty-seven days of raw feed, unaggregated, the cyano elevations and the flat coherence sitting side by side as the sentence with no verb. The lab assay. The photographs of the hardware logs from Seven and Eight and Nine, the off-schedule third-party sync entries with their reference numbers matching the cert. Del's certification records and the notepad diagram, the map of the why. And the notebook — she photographed every page that mattered, the confirmation number in her own hand, *not a misfile*, the reference numbers, the paper made permanent at last by becoming part of the public record. The discipline of photographing a thing before she touched it, three days and ten years of it, all of it folding now into one object that could not be un-filed.

The inside-actor line she wrote last, and slowest, deciding each word the way she'd decide a valve sequence. Del's rule was law here: a guess in the package was a way to get the whole package thrown out. So she wrote no guess. She wrote the facts and only the facts — that the originating report had been removed from an append-only system; that such removal required Director-level append-only access; that the Director of the technical division had, when contacted, acknowledged ownership of the review process for the affected reports. Three statements, each sourced, each true, none of them a leap a hostile reader could pull on to unravel the rest. She did not write Nwosu's name as the actor. She wrote the access vector, and let the access vector stand in the record where the hole had been, and the inference sat there for anyone competent to make and impossible for anyone to dismiss. The hole the package had carried — the place where the inside actor should be — closed, carefully, without a single word she'd have to defend.

Then she read the public-deposit procedure. All of it, start to finish, the way she read a manual for a system she was going to trust her name to. It was clean, designed, exact — the standing requirement, the verification window, the chain-of-custody attestation, the irrevocability clause that made a deposit a deposit and not a draft. There was nothing furtive in it. Reading it carefully only made it more legitimate, not less: she was doing this by the book, the founding book, the one the Authority's book could not override. The same small good feeling rose in her that had risen the evening she'd filed the report correctly to the right person — the quiet pleasure of doing a hard thing exactly right. Except this time it was pointed at the door that couldn't be shut, and it sat differently for that, steadier, with something underneath it she didn't name.

She confirmed the credential one last time. Active. Hers. Separate. She set the upload to execute at five.

Five, because system traffic ran lowest in the hour before dawn, and because she wanted the record public and being pulled by strangers — independent parties, anyone at all — before anyone who could think to stop it was awake to try. It was tactical and it was characteristic and it was also, underneath, the simplest thing: she wanted the truth checked by people who had no reason to protect anything, while the city slept.

The deposit sat armed on the screen, the clock at twenty-two hours under it, a single action between the truth and the public record.

She did not sleep. She sat by the window with the cold coffee and watched the lower terraces, the cascade stepping the water down weir by weir in the dark, the living walls breathing their faint warmth into the night. Every certified system performed exactly as designed. The good city did its ordinary work, not knowing yet what was coming for it, or what was about to be done on its behalf. Somewhere out past the reeds the bloom was building behind the mask, and somewhere on a server she could not see, a fired analyst's signature still certified it clean, and somewhere a tired woman was not sleeping either. Yara sat with all of it and did not look away. The one door that had never been theirs to shut was about to open, at five, by her hand, and she stayed awake to keep watch over the hours until it did.
