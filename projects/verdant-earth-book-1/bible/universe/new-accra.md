# New Accra

## Overview
New Accra is a coastal West African city of approximately 1.4 million people, built over and around the original Accra on land that was selectively elevated, restored, or surrendered to the sea between 2047 and 2065. It is, by 2091, one of the most cited examples of successful post-Collapse urban adaptation — a city that looked at what it lost and built something different rather than reconstructing what was there.

It is genuinely beautiful. This is important: New Accra is not beautiful in spite of its ecological infrastructure, but because of it. The living walls, the biofilter wetlands, the mycelial networks under the streets — these are what the city looks like, not what it's hidden inside.

---

## Geography

### Physical layout
- Coastal; the Gulf of Guinea is visible from most elevated terraces
- The Lower City: near sea level, built on raised foundations with flood margins designed in; the oldest continuous habitation
- The Upper Terraces: stepped residential and working districts built into the hillside above the old city center; the newer, more architecturally coherent section
- The Biofilter Wetlands: a belt of managed wetland running along the former shoreline; part water treatment, part ecological restoration zone, partly accessible to the public
- The Eastern Inlet: the estuary zone where the biofilter network meets the sea; monitoring stations along the whole length

### Climate
Tropical coastal, modified. The urban heat island effect is managed by extensive green cover. Wet season (May–October) delivers significant rainfall processed through the biofilter cascade. The sea is warmer than it was in 2040; managed cooling technology keeps the inlets functional.

## Infrastructure

### Water systems
The New Accra Water Authority manages a four-stage treatment cascade:
1. **Intake wetlands**: initial filtration through managed reed beds and biofilter colonies
2. **Mycorrhizal processing nodes**: biotech stations where fungal networks break down chemical residuals (the technology Yara maintains)
3. **UV cascade treatment**: backup purification using certified solar-powered UV arrays
4. **Distribution network**: pressurized ceramic pipes with embedded biofilm sensors throughout

The network has 23 active monitoring stations (Station 1–23). Stations 1–9 handle the eastern inlet (estuary-adjacent); 10–15 cover the mid-wetlands; 16–23 are inland distribution. Stations 7, 8, and 9 are the innermost estuary stations, closest to the open sea.

### Energy
Decentralized solar-biogas hybrid. Upper Terraces have high solar coverage; Lower City relies more on district biogas (waste-processing bioreactors). No grid-dependent single point of failure by design.

### The mycelial data network
New Accra's infrastructure is partially connected through a mycorrhizal sensor network — biological sensing embedded in the urban subsoil that reports water chemistry, soil health, and structural integrity. This is the technology that makes New Accra's monitoring uniquely capable, and uniquely vulnerable to firmware manipulation.

---

## Governance
New Accra operates under a cooperative municipal council structure. The Water Authority is a semi-autonomous technical body under the council, with Accord Technical Council certification oversight. In practice: the Authority runs the water; the council funds and politically shields it; the Technical Council certifies that the biotech remains within Covenant parameters.

The chain of accountability becomes the source of obstruction in the story — each body can point to another.

## Sensory profile

### Sight
Solar tiles on upper-terrace rooftops catch light at different angles throughout the day. Living walls — vertical gardens maintained by building cooperatives — cover most vertical surfaces in the Upper Terraces, less in the Lower City. The wetlands are a haze of green at the city's edge. Water is visible everywhere: channels, treatment ponds, the inlet reflecting sky.

### Sound
The city has a particular sound at dawn: water moving through the treatment cascade, birds in the living walls, the low hum of biogas compressors cycling. By mid-morning, human traffic noise dominates. The biofilter stations themselves are quiet — you hear the water, not machinery.

### Smell
This is what Yara uses to assess station health. A healthy biofilter station smells: green (living plant matter), wet (saturated mycorrhizal substrate), mineral (the treated water), faintly biological (the particular warmth of active fungal colonies). An unhealthy station smells off in ways that are hard to describe but immediately recognizable to someone who knows.

### Temperature
Warm, coastal, humid in the wet season. The living infrastructure moderates it slightly — biofilter wetlands create local cooling. The elevated terraces catch the sea breeze.

## Cultural character

### Identity
New Accra self-consciously presents as a post-Collapse success story, which creates a particular pressure: the city's administrators are acutely aware of what they represent and protective of its image. This institutional pride becomes, in the story, part of why an anomaly gets suppressed — it doesn't fit the narrative.

### The cooperative texture
The social world is cooperative in the formal sense: building cooperatives, work cooperatives, water user cooperatives. This is not utopia — cooperatives have politics, and New Accra's are as complex as anywhere. But the baseline assumption is that people hold resources in common and make decisions collectively. Yara grew up in this; it's simply how things work.

### The Before Time
Older residents sometimes reference the pre-Covenant era — flooded neighborhoods, the year the power went out for ten days, the period when it was uncertain whether the city would survive. For Yara's generation, this is history: vivid family history in some cases (her parents relocated from coastal flooding), but not personal memory.

## Key locations in the story

### New Accra Water Authority headquarters
Mid-terrace, functional modernist building from the 2060s. Yara's office. Director Nwosu's office is two floors up. The data center is in the basement.

### Station Seven
The monitoring station where Yara first notices the anomaly. Eastern inlet, accessible via a maintenance path through the wetlands. Has the smell of a healthy station — which is why the wrong numbers are so alarming.

### Del's temporary office
Accord Technical Council secondment space in a shared government building in the Upper Terraces. Slightly better furnished than the Water Authority; neither is impressive.

### The public wetland path
A walking path along the biofilter wetlands accessible to residents. Yara uses it to think. The city is visible from here — the thing worth protecting.

## Notes for writer
- New Accra should feel lived-in and specific, not like a concept
- Its beauty is structural, not decorative — the ecological infrastructure IS the aesthetic
- The city believes in itself; this is not naivety, it's earned
- Yara's relationship to the city should echo her relationship to the biofilter network: care expressed as competence
