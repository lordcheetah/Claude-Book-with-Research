# Query Letter Opening — Thinking Machines: How AI Actually Works

Most people who use AI tools every day have been handed exactly two explanations for what's happening when they do, and both are bad: "it's just statistics" (true, and somehow still useless), or "it's basically magic" (comforting, and a polite way of giving up on the question). *Thinking Machines: How AI Actually Works* is a ~25,500-word work of narrative nonfiction that takes a third path — it opens the mechanism up, piece by piece, in plain language, and trusts the reader with both what is now genuinely understood and what honestly isn't.

The book follows one continuous question across eight chapters: not "is AI good or bad," but "how does this thing actually work, and how would I know if someone were telling me the truth about it?" It starts on the most ordinary ground possible — a spam filter nobody designed by hand — and builds outward through next-token prediction, representation, attention, and scale, before turning, in its second half, to the territory most books about AI get wrong: why these systems confidently say false things, why the people who build them still can't fully explain what's happening inside them, and what the field's most careful researchers are still honestly arguing about — alignment, machine consciousness, where this is all heading — without either side winning. The book never adjudicates these open questions for the reader. What it does instead is name, explicitly, in its closing pages, the tool it has spent seven chapters quietly building in them: a working habit for telling the difference between a claim that's settled, one that's genuinely contested, and one that's just a guess dressed up with confidence. That tool, the book argues, is more durable than any specific fact about AI — which it's honest enough to expect will be out of date before the ink dries.

For readers of Brian Christian's *The Alignment Problem* and Anil Ananthaswamy's *Why Machines Learn* — both of which trust general readers with real mechanism rather than reaching for metaphor in its place — *Thinking Machines* offers a shorter, more conversational entry point into the same territory: less a comprehensive technical history, more a single sustained argument that understanding the machine doesn't shrink the wonder of it. It sharpens it. Where Carl Bergstrom and Jevin West's *Calling Bullshit* built a general-purpose toolkit for spotting bad statistical reasoning in the wild, this book builds the AI-specific version of that same instinct — and, like that book, bets that readers would rather be equipped than reassured.

*Note on comp titles*: Both primary comps (Christian, 2020; Ananthaswamy, 2024) are well-reviewed, backlist-strong titles in the same "trust the general reader with real mechanism" lane — recent enough to signal market relevance, established enough to signal the lane has an audience, neither a runaway #1 bestseller that would make the comparison feel presumptuous. *Calling Bullshit* (Bergstrom & West, 2020) is offered as a secondary comp specifically for the book's most distinctive feature — that its real payload is a transferable *skill* (the sorting habit) rather than a stockpile of facts. If the agent is known to represent narrower science-explainer or tech-criticism lists, swap in titles from that specific list; these three were chosen for general appeal to agents working broadly in popular-science/tech nonfiction.
