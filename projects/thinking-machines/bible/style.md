# Style Guide — Thinking Machines

## Book type
Nonfiction — accessible technology explainer
Target audience: curious non-engineers with no CS background; people who use AI tools and want to understand what's actually happening

---

## Voice

### Narrator tone
First person plural ("we") as a device to put reader and writer alongside each other investigating the subject. Not "I'll explain" or "you should know" — rather "let's look at" and "here's what we find." Intellectually honest about uncertainty: where consensus exists, say so; where it doesn't, say so.

Conversational authority: the tone of someone who finds this genuinely interesting and has thought about how to explain it clearly, not the tone of someone performing expertise. Never condescending. Never oversimplified to the point of becoming wrong.

### Analogies
Central to the book's approach. Good analogies here are:
- Mechanical/physical (weights on a scale, water flowing, sorting through a pile)
- Everyday decision-making (recognizing a face, guessing the next word)
- Organizational/social (departments reporting to a manager, learning from feedback)

Bad analogies: anything that anthropomorphizes AI beyond what's accurate; anything that implies sentience or desire.

---

## Structure

### Chapter structure
Each chapter follows a rough arc:
1. **Concrete hook**: a thing the reader has experienced or can immediately picture
2. **Deepen the question**: what would it take to do that? What's surprising about how hard/easy it is?
3. **The mechanism**: how it actually works (the main explanatory content)
4. **Implications**: what this tells us about the system's capabilities and limits
5. **Bridge**: connects to the next chapter's question

### Section breaks
Use subheadings. Chapters have 3–6 named sections. Section names should be phrases, not labels.

---

## Language & vocabulary

### Register
Accessible prose. Technical terms are introduced once with a clear explanation in plain language, then used normally. No jargon without definition. No pretense that the topic is simpler than it is.

### Terms to introduce carefully
- Parameter / weight
- Training / inference
- Gradient descent
- Token / tokenization
- Context window
- Hallucination / confabulation
- Emergent behavior
- Benchmark

### Forbidden
- "Magic" or "magical" (unless immediately followed by "which is another word for 'we don't fully understand why'")
- Implying AI "wants," "decides," or "thinks" without qualification
- Presenting any open research question as settled

---

## Tone & mood

### Overall
Curious, precise, honest about uncertainty. The book's argument is not that AI is good or bad — it's that understanding it matters, and it's more understandable than it looks.

### On capabilities
Accurate about what current systems can and cannot do. Neither dismissive ("it's just statistics") nor mystifying ("it's already smarter than us"). The honest middle: remarkable in specific ways, limited in specific ways.

### On risks and futures
The book doesn't take a position on AI development policy. It presents the landscape fairly so readers can form their own views. The final chapter acknowledges major open questions without pretending to answer them.

## Chapter length
- Target: 2,500–3,500 words per chapter
- 8 chapters total
- Introduction + Conclusion (shorter: ~1,500 words each)
