## Where we are

Chapter 5 picks up directly inside Chapter 4's closing question — no re-grounding, opening cold with "So: what actually changes?" — and spends its length answering it on two tracks at once: the track where the answer is settled, and the track where it genuinely is not.

The chapter opens by pulling apart the word "bigger" itself: parameters, data, and compute are three distinct dimensions that must scale together (stated as architecture-level fact, not hedge), introduced through the returning million-dials image from Chapter 1, now multiplied by a thousand. From there it builds its central organizing image — a hand-drawn coastline sketch versus a satellite photograph of the same coastline — and uses it to frame the chapter's actual question: does scaling up a system like this reveal a *clearer version* of the same picture, or does it reveal things that were never representable at the smaller scale at all? That question turns out to have two competing, evidence-backed answers, and the chapter spends the rest of its length walking both.

**Track one — settled ground.** Scaling laws (the empirically robust, half-decade-old finding that performance improves predictably and graphably as parameters/data/compute scale together) are established with full, unhedged confidence — explicitly calibrated to the same tier as Chapter 2's "next-token prediction is the entire training task." The Chinchilla finding (DeepMind, 2022) refines rather than contradicts this: model size and training-data size must grow in matched proportion, or the larger model actually underperforms. A real-world economic complication follows immediately — companies like Meta deliberately *ignore* the Chinchilla-optimal ratio for inference-cost reasons (the LLaMA example), which the chapter uses to show the theory and the spreadsheet both surviving contact with each other.

**Track two — actively disputed ground.** The chapter pivots explicitly from *degree* to *kind*: at certain scales, models don't just get better at things smaller models could already sort-of do — they start doing things smaller models reliably could not do at all (in-context learning, multi-step reasoning, with a concrete ~100B-parameter threshold example). This is **emergence**, and the chapter names it as the place where "the field itself stops walking on settled ground." It then presents, with genuinely matched specificity and procedural respect, the two live camps disputing what this means: Schaeffer/Miranda/Koyejo (2023), who argue the apparent "cliffs" are measurement artifacts manufactured by coarse, all-or-nothing scoring (illustrated via a thermometer-resolution analogy), and Du and colleagues (2025), who argue the sharpness of these jumps tracks a real, measurable internal threshold and persists even under the continuous scoring the skeptics recommend. The chapter explicitly declines to pick a winner — "nobody knows... the disagreement itself has *moved*, rather than resolved" — and forecloses every common false-balance failure mode (no "but experts now lean toward," no last-word effect, no recency bias toward the more recent paper).

A misconception-correction beat follows immediately: "emergent" does not mean something mystical switched on — the same ordinary training process produced these capabilities, exactly as it produced everything else in the book; what's disputed is only *how they became visible*, not *whether* ordinary training produced them. A brief, explicitly-fenced kinship is drawn to the stochastic-parrots debate from Chapters 2–3 (named as "cousins," not the same argument). A small, separate illustration of contestation — the Kosinski theory-of-mind controversy — closes the section, now explicitly marked as "a different, smaller kind of dispute" so it cannot be mistaken for an extension of the emergence debate itself.

The chapter then delivers its central payoff: the fourth and *final* appearance of "novelty is scale, not kind," explicitly earned (both the settled and the strange phenomena are produced by the exact same machinery, scaled up — the one thing both warring camps agree on) and explicitly retired on the page: "You will not hear it again after this page... Consider it retired." It closes on cost economics, the honestly-labeled-as-speculation question of whether future scaling will produce more surprises, and a clean bridge to Chapter 6: the gap between what a system *can* do and what it actually *does*, "where hallucination lives."
