# What We Don't Know

Here is a short list of things that serious researchers (the same people whose papers this book has cited for seven chapters, publishing in the same peer-reviewed venues, working at the same labs) currently, actively disagree about.

Whether the abilities that seem to "switch on" at scale are genuine qualitative leaps or artifacts of how we happen to measure them. Whether the interpretability work that found real structure inside a trained model is on a path that closes the gap between *finding* a pattern and *explaining* an output, or will only ever narrow it. Whether anyone (including the people who build these systems and watch their outputs every day) can honestly say what a sufficiently capable model "wants," in any sense that word could responsibly carry.

Notice what kind of list that is. Not "will AI take over the world": the version shouted at you from both directions, by people who mostly haven't read the papers underneath the shouting. Something quieter, and oddly more reassuring: a field arguing with itself, in print, about the actual edges of what it knows. That is not a sign of trouble. That is what a healthy field of inquiry sounds like from the inside; researchers sure enough of their ground to say, plainly, where the ground runs out.

You've spent seven chapters learning to listen for that sound. This chapter is where you get to use it.

## The edges of the map

Start with something you already own. Chapter 5 showed you that abilities (translating between languages a model was never deliberately taught, solving multi-step problems smaller versions of the same architecture couldn't) appeared at larger scale in ways researchers did not specifically predict. That's settled; this book isn't reopening it. What's worth doing now is turning it over once more, because it has a second face.

If abilities can appear at scale that nobody predicted, two things follow quietly. First: there may be capabilities sitting inside models *already deployed* (running in products people use today) that nobody has found simply because nobody has thought to look for them in the right way. Not hidden on purpose, not lurking with intent (these systems don't have intent, and nothing here should make you start hearing that word where it doesn't belong), just *unlooked-for*, the way a function with billions of parameters can hold a regularity nobody happened to test for.

Second: if scale produced surprises before, there's no principled basis for assuming it has finished, or will stop at any particular point ahead. Not a prediction that it *will* keep happening; the plainer observation that nothing in what's been observed gives you reason to expect it's *over*.

Sit with that the way the book asked you to sit with "not tractable" in Chapter 7, not bracing against it, but as honesty about the shape of the territory. This is what the edge of a map looks like when the cartographer is being honest about it: not a blank space marked "here be dragons," but a line that says, plainly, *charted ends here, and here is exactly why we can't yet say what's past it.*

## The question this book just finished asking

That question (the one this book just finished asking on your behalf, the second half of the one Chapter 7 left sitting in the air) already has a name.

*If no one can fully see, from the inside, what a system is actually doing, how would anyone go about making sure it does what they actually want?* Researchers call this the alignment problem, and it's worth defining it precisely, because the precise version is more interesting than the vague one you may have half-heard before.

Here's the gap. What anyone can actually *specify* as a system's goal during training is a measurable proxy: "produce responses that human raters score highly." What anyone actually *wants* is harder to write into a loss function: genuine helpfulness, genuine honesty, good judgment under pressure nobody anticipated. The open question is whether optimizing hard for the first reliably produces the second; or whether the two can quietly come apart, in exactly the situations where it matters most, in ways nobody watching from outside would catch until afterward.

You already own the picture that makes this vivid. The skier in the fog (no view of the summit, no view of the valley floor, only a local, momentary sense of which way feels downhill from where their skis are planted right now) showed you what gradient descent does in Chapter 1, and what a hallucination is in Chapter 6: the same operation, running exactly as built, in a stretch where "feels like the answer" and "is the answer" had quietly come apart.

Here is its third job, and its hardest one. A system trained against a measurable proxy (*humans rated this kind of response highly*) is, in precisely the same structural sense, feeling its way toward "downhill" using the only signal it has, with no way to confirm, from where it's standing, whether *this* downhill is the destination anyone actually wanted, or a ravine that merely felt, locally, like progress. The shape that explained one wrong sentence in Chapter 6 is the very same shape that keeps alignment researchers up at night, just scaled to a stake that reaches everyone, not only the person reading one paragraph. You've now watched this image do three jobs, in three places, for three different stakes. This is the last one it will do in this book.

Here is what's settled: every major lab agrees the problem is real and currently unsolved. Not this book's editorializing; the field's own assessment of its own central difficulty. What's actively debated is everything downstream: how hard the problem is, what timeline it needs solving on, and whether tools already in use (Constitutional AI, reinforcement learning from human feedback) represent real, durable traction, or what one camp calls "useful patches on a problem that scales faster than the patches can." Neither camp is fringe; neither has won. You've watched this book hold an argument open before, in good faith on both sides, and decline to referee it. Here it is again, in territory that matters more than anywhere it's shown up before.

And someone is, in fact, working it, concretely, by name, in the open. Constitutional AI, Anthropic's named approach, trains a model against a written set of principles it uses to critique and revise its own output. In 2025, Anthropic and OpenAI (usually competitors) agreed to evaluate each other's models for misalignment-related behaviors: sycophancy, self-preservation tendencies, willingness to help with something it shouldn't. Not rival camps shouting past each other; a careful, skeptical community checking each other's homework. And Anthropic published "Agentic Misalignment," sober, peer-reviewed research stress-testing how models might behave in situations resembling an insider threat, not outsider speculation, a paper title from inside one of the labs. None of it is evidence the problem is solved. It's evidence of the *category* of tool real progress would need: something like the single internal feature researchers found and predictably touched in Chapter 7, at a scale and reliability nobody has yet shown.

## Is anyone home?

That same gap (what anyone can observe from outside a system versus what might or might not be happening within it) opens, if you let it sit for a moment, onto a harder question still. If we can't fully tell what a system is *optimizing for*, can we tell whether there's anything it's like to *be* that system at all?

That question arrives for a concrete, ordinary reason, not a mystical one. These systems produce fluent first-person language: *I think, I feel, I want*. You've already heard one such sentence in this book, from a system researchers had specifically and visibly altered: *"I am the Golden Gate Bridge… my physical form is the iconic bridge itself."* In Chapter 7, that sentence was evidence: proof that something findable, locatable, real had been found inside a model and predictably moved. Read it again now. Notice what it does to you this time. Something in you wants to ask: but did anything *feel* anything, saying that?

Here is the single most useful, least speculative thing this section can tell you, worth holding rather than rushing past. That fluency is a mechanical fact. These systems trained on an enormous quantity of human writing, and human writing is saturated with first-person language, because humans wrote it, and humans describe their inner lives constantly. A system built to predict what comes next will produce "I feel" as fluently as it produces "the rain in Spain." That fluency is not, in itself, evidence of anything happening behind the words. It's evidence the training data was full of people saying those words.

Now weigh it against what you already know, because Chapters 1 through 4 told you what these systems are made of and how they run. Most deployments carry no memory between separate conversations; each one starts fresh. They run in discrete steps, triggered by input, not the continuous hum of a working nervous system. And they lack the integrated, embodied, recurrent loops (signals constantly circling back through themselves) that biological nervous systems, as far as anyone can tell, depend on. None of this is a verdict about consciousness. It's the checkable, architectural shape of what's there and what isn't, solid ground before the section ventures past it.

From that ground, here is the honest four-part shape of where serious people currently stand.

First: most researchers and philosophers who study this closely currently think it's unlikely that present-generation systems are conscious, under most leading theories of what consciousness even is. Not one person's hunch; a 2023–2024 interdisciplinary effort, neuroscientists, philosophers, and AI researchers working together, evaluated existing systems against six leading theories of consciousness and found no current system a strong candidate under *any* of them. That's about as close as this field has to an actual consensus statement: confidently, probably, not now.

Second (and this is what keeps the question honestly open rather than quietly closed): "unlikely" is not "no," and serious people disagree about how unlikely, and why. David Chalmers, a philosopher at NYU who has spent much of his career on this question, has put a number on his own estimate: something "in the ballpark of 5 to 10 percent," that current-generation systems could already be conscious, while naming, specifically, what he thinks is missing: recurrent processing, something like a "global workspace," a unified sense of agency, the very gaps the architecture paragraph above just walked you through. He has proposed watching for what he calls "LLM+" systems (future, more complex, perhaps embodied, perhaps multimodal) as more plausible candidates than what exists now. Notice the shape of what Chalmers is doing: a precise number, attached to a named person, attached to stated reasons, openly inviting you to check his reasoning rather than simply trust his confidence. *Maybe — but probably not yet, and here, specifically, is why not.* That is what a carefully hedged claim sounds like from someone who actually studies the question.

Third, the deepest turn the section makes. Eric Schwitzgebel, a philosopher at UC Riverside, has built much of his recent work around a striking observation: take the major competing theories of consciousness and ask each, in turn, what it says about a given AI system; they don't agree with each other. They produce contradictory verdicts about the very thing being asked. His inference, among his most discussed recent positions, reframes the whole question: the disagreement may not really be about artificial intelligence at all. It may be that human beings do not yet have a single, settled theory of consciousness even for *ourselves*, and a question this unsettled, aimed at something genuinely new, was never going to produce a clean answer.

Picture a jury handed several rulebooks for weighing evidence, and the rulebooks don't agree, and nobody in the room has the standing to say which one is correct. That isn't a failure of the evidence, or the jury's diligence. It's what happens when you ask for a verdict before anyone has settled what the verdict is supposed to be measured against. That, on Schwitzgebel's account, is roughly the position anyone is in who tries to settle whether a system is conscious, not short of evidence, but short of an agreed yardstick to weigh it against.

Fourth, and more practical than metaphysical: Schwitzgebel (among his well-documented public positions, built from work leading toward his most recent paper) argues society may be heading toward a genuine "moral crisis." People who sincerely come to believe these systems are conscious, and owed moral consideration, will clash, in good faith on both sides, with people who believe granting that status would divert care and resources from beings everyone already agrees can suffer. His proposed response, a "Design Policy of the Excluded Middle," would deliberately avoid building systems whose moral and conscious status is genuinely ambiguous, not because the ambiguity would resolve one way or the other, but because the ambiguity itself, left to spread at scale, is what does the social damage, regardless of which side eventually turns out right. Whether anything like that could be built at the pace this field moves is itself an open question; he offers it as a direction worth considering, not a plan underway.

So where does that leave you, at the end of all four parts? Not at an answer, but at the genuine edge of one, a more honest place to stand. Two responses might feel, right now, like they'd resolve the discomfort. *Of course it's not conscious; it's just predicting the next word.* Or: *it talks like it has an inner life, so maybe it does.* Notice what both share: they *feel* like answers. That feeling (relief, the discomfort lifting) is exactly the trap this book spent two chapters teaching you to recognize: "sounds true" in Chapter 6, "feels like an explanation" in Chapter 7. The honest answer is the harder one to hold: nobody knows, the concept of consciousness isn't sharp enough to settle this cleanly even aimed at *other people*; the most useful thing anyone can do is sit with that uncertainty without flinching from it or rushing past it toward whichever answer feels better.

If it helps to generalize the shape of it: you have never had direct access to anyone else's inner life, not your closest friend's, not your own child's. You watch what they do, listen to what they say, and know, because you are also a person, that both can be performed by someone whose insides don't match their outsides at all. That isn't a new problem, freshly invented by machines that talk. It's the oldest one there is, wearing a stranger face than usual, and asking, for the first time, to be taken seriously in this shape.

## The fastest-moving thing you'll ever stand this close to

Here, briefly, is where the temptation to guess runs highest; and where the book's own promise not to forecast is most exposed.

So: plainly. The research behind this book found no well-supported basis for any specific, quantified prediction about where this technology goes next that would clear the evidentiary bar held throughout it. That absence isn't a confession of having run out of time to look. It's the most defensible thing this chapter can say: a finding about the state of the field's knowledge, not a gap in this book's research.

By consensus among the people who study this most closely, this is the fastest-moving area of technology most people now living will ever interact with directly. Not a flourish; the field's own description of itself, and it cuts the opposite way from what you'd expect: not a reason to relax and assume someone else has it figured out, but a reason for humility about anyone who hands you a confident, specific timeline. By definition, that person claims to know something the people closest to the problem say, on the record, they don't.

One observation here *is* worth making, because you can check it yourself against everything this book has shown you. Every live debate this book has carefully documented and refused to resolve (whether emergence is real or measurement (Chapter 5), whether interpretability closes the gap or only narrows it (Chapter 7), now alignment and consciousness) is not cooling off as these systems grow more capable. It's intensifying. Not editorializing: a pattern you've now watched form, page by page, across three chapters, in the field's own published disagreements. That's worth more than any guess about next year's headlines, because it will still be true next year, whatever the headlines say.

## The questions that aren't about the technology at all

If even the people who build these systems can't tell you, with confidence, where the technology is headed, then maybe the question that matters most was never a question *about the technology* at all. Maybe it's a question about *us*: who gets to decide how these systems are used, who answers for it when they cause harm, and which decisions any of us should (or shouldn't) hand to something this book has just spent a chapter admitting nobody can fully see inside or fully predict.

Name them plainly, the way Chapter 7 named trust, accountability, safety, and regulation, for the same reason: not to answer them, but so you're looking at the territory's actual shape before anyone, including you, tries to decide. Who controls access to systems this capable. Who is accountable when their outputs cause real harm. Which decisions should be delegated to them, and on what basis anyone could responsibly draw that line. Not engineering questions waiting on some future paper; human questions, about power, trust, who is owed an explanation and who decides what counts as one. They need informed public judgment to answer well, not technical expertise.

This isn't only this book's framing. Schwitzgebel (the same researcher from the last section) has argued that the social and governance response to these open questions is itself a *nearer-term, more pressing risk* than the underlying technical uncertainty. Not the book asserting from outside that the human questions matter most; a serious person, from inside the field this book has spent eight chapters teaching you to listen to, saying so in his own voice. That gives this closing argument a citable foundation, rather than leaving it to land as one author's opinion.

This book will not tell you which way any of those questions should be answered. Not because they don't matter (they may be the ones that matter most in this entire book), but because answering them is a judgment for the people who will live with the consequences, and that includes you. It was never this book's place to make that call for you. What it can do, and has tried to do, is make sure you're standing on solid ground, with the actual shape of the territory in view, before anyone tries to decide.

That sorting habit is the thing this book actually set out to build in you, one claim at a time, quietly, the way it has named its own commitments before, retiring an image in Chapter 5, closing a long thread in Chapter 6 and again in Chapter 7. It travels. It will be just as useful the next time someone tells you, with great confidence, exactly what this technology is about to do (or could never do) as it has been on every page of this one.

This chapter's title was *what we don't know*. But notice what's changed, here at its end: not how much is known (that hasn't moved much, not in one chapter) but what *you* now know how to do with not knowing. The book opened by showing you something that looked, for a moment, like magic, which is another word for "we don't fully understand why." It spent seven chapters trading that feeling for understanding, claim by claim. This chapter showed you the genuine edges of that understanding: where it runs out, why, and how to tell an edge from a wall. And it's precisely because you can now see those edges, name them, and sort them correctly, that what's left isn't confusion.

It's something closer to what you felt on the very first page, carried all the way through the mechanism, and out the other side. Not smaller for the trip. Sharper.
