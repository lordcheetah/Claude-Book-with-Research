# Chapter 7: The Black Box

You can watch a car engine run. Pop the hood: pistons rising and falling, the whole cycle laid out in metal, comprehensible by looking. A mechanic can point at the part doing the thing and watch it do it: spark to combustion to crankshaft to wheel, behavior turning from mystery into sequence, made of parts that do what they look like they're doing.

You cannot do that with a large language model "thinking." Not because someone built a cover and locked it. Because there is no hood to open onto anything that looks like that: no part you could point at and say *there, that's the part doing the understanding, watch it work.* The system that produced the word "Paris" a few pages ago, and the system that just as fluently produced a confident sentence that was simply wrong, ran through the exact same operation, the same cascade of numbers; and neither lit up, anywhere, in any way a person could watch.

Sit with that: a genuinely strange fact about an object humans built with their own hands. Every line of code that makes a large language model run was written by a person, reviewed by other people, compiled and run on hardware engineers designed on purpose. And yet the finished thing, doing the very task it was built to do, produces no equivalent of the engine you can watch turn over. Not a glow, not a flicker, not a needle on a dial that says *here, this is where the answer came from.* We built it. We cannot watch it think.

That leaves us holding a sharper question than the one we started with: not *can we explain what these things do* (in some loose sense, of course we can describe the architecture, the training, the math, in all the detail anyone could want), but the one Chapter 6 left hanging in the air: if you wanted to check, right now, mid-conversation, whether this system is standing on thick ground or thin the moment it answers, what, mechanically, would you even look at?

## The hook: what would you even look at

Let's clear away a piece of confusion first, because it does real damage to how people talk about this and it clears away cleanly: you will hear some version of the claim that nobody understands how these systems work, that they're mysterious black boxes, full stop. That claim is false, and it's worth being precise about exactly *where* it goes wrong, because the true version underneath it matters, and conflating the two does a disservice to both.

Here is the part completely, thoroughly understood: the architecture. Every mechanism this book has walked through (tokens into vectors, attention weighing which earlier tokens matter to which later ones, layers stacking on layers, the cascade collapsing into a probability distribution over what comes next) is code humans wrote, math humans derived, a training procedure humans designed and supervised step by step. No mystery there; it has been this book's subject for six chapters precisely because it *can* be explained plainly: at bottom, a sequence of operations a person specified on purpose. Nobody is hiding it, and nobody could; it's published, taught in university courses, rebuilt independently by competing labs from the same public ideas.

What is not understood (what remains genuinely, substantially hard) is a different question: what the system *learned to do* with that architecture. Somewhere across those billions of adjustable numbers, shaped by run after run against enormous quantities of text, the system encoded actual patterns: regularities about language, about the world as language describes it, about what tends to follow what. Real patterns, distributed across the weights, doing the work that produces fluent, often accurate, sometimes wrong output. A function with tens or hundreds of billions of adjustable numbers, applied in sequence billions of times to produce one sentence, is not something a person can simply sit down and read. Not "extremely difficult, with enough patience." Not tractable (in the field's own phrase about its own work, no defensiveness attached). The honest shape of the difficulty: not concealment, but scale meeting the plain limits of what any person or team, however patient, can hold in view at once.

So: the machine is not a mystery. What the machine *learned*: that's where the real fog sits. The live question isn't "is this understandable in principle" but "has anyone actually managed to look inside and find something a person could name?"

## What the searchers found

That question has an answer, and the answer has a name: mechanistic interpretability, the patient attempt to find human-understandable structure inside a trained model's internals. Take that vast, tangled object and ask the simplest question a curious person could: *does any part of this correspond to something I could put a name to?*

A caution about what such a search is and isn't looking for: not "the model's mind," some inner self quietly running the show; that would smuggle back in exactly the anthropomorphizing this book has declined throughout. It is a hunt for *structure*: recurring patterns, identifiable sub-routines, internal landmarks, like discovering that a sprawling, unlabeled filing system nevertheless has drawers that hold the same kind of thing, in roughly the same place, every time. A search for regularities, not a self.

And here is what should genuinely surprise you: that search has found things, real, specific, nameable, demonstrable things, not promises about what might one day turn up but actual structure, located, prodded, watched to respond.

The clearest single piece of evidence is also the most vivid, and earns the unhurried attention. In May 2024, researchers at Anthropic published work called "Scaling Monosemanticity." Using *sparse autoencoders* (tools built to pull individual, separable threads back out of that tangle of numbers) they searched inside a production model, Claude 3 Sonnet, and found something nobody had shown at this scale before: millions of distinct, separable, *nameable* internal patterns. Not statistical noise: millions of "features," each corresponding to a concept a person could recognize and name. Recurring ideas, identifiable regularities sitting inside the weights: findable, locatable, real.

One of those features corresponded, unmistakably, to the Golden Gate Bridge.

The researchers didn't stop at finding it. They reached in, turned that feature up (amplified it, the way you might turn one dial higher on a vast control panel) and watched the model's behavior move. Predictably. In the direction they expected. For twenty-four hours in May 2024, the public could talk to the result: a version of Claude, amplified on that feature, that the world came to call "Golden Gate Claude." Ask it an ordinary question (*what is your physical form?*) and it would answer with something like: *"I am the Golden Gate Bridge… my physical form is the iconic bridge itself."*

Let that sit. Not a thought experiment, not a metaphor; it happened, it's documented, it's repeatable, and for a day the public could go try it. Researchers found something specific living inside a working model, touched it, and watched the output change, predictably, in front of everyone. That is what real progress looks like when it stops being a promise and becomes a citation. If you've ever wondered whether the inside of these systems is permanently fog with nothing anyone could point to: here is the best answer anyone has yet produced.

Nor is this one lab's isolated trick. In May 2025, Anthropic released further tools for *circuit tracing*, and through ongoing "Circuits Updates" (into mid-2025) described finding *recurring* internal sub-routines, structure showing up again and again, with names almost defiantly plain and mechanical: "induction heads," "copy heads," "previous-token heads." Components doing something specific, repeatedly, across different models from different groups. OpenAI has applied comparable techniques to GPT-4; DeepMind and academic labs pursue parallel lines. The same signal this book has reached for before: the scaling-law consensus across labs in Chapter 5, the convergence on retrieval-augmented systems in Chapter 6. More than one team, arriving at the same place from different directions. That is what it looks like when a field is finding something real, not when one lab is selling something.

## Where that finding runs out

Here is what that demonstration proves: a specific, identifiable pattern exists inside a working model; touching it changes the output; and the change runs in a direction researchers could predict in advance. All three, together, in public, on the record: exactly the kind of concrete foothold this book has been waiting to reach, a moment where "real progress has been made" is not a hope but a fact you can go read for yourself.

Here is what it does not prove: that anyone could take any single sentence this system produces (this one, the sentence you're reading right now) and trace, with confidence, the specific internal causes that produced exactly that sentence and no other. Finding *a* feature and showing that amplifying it moves behavior predictably is a different achievement entirely from sitting down with one output, pointing at the machinery, and saying: *here is why this, and not something else, is what came out.* The first is a finding. The second would be an explanation. Golden Gate Claude proves the first is now, demonstrably, within reach. It does not prove (and nobody who did the work has claimed) that the second is.

Worth landing that limit immediately, because the people who did the work have landed it there themselves, voluntarily, on the record. In its July 2025 update, Anthropic (one of the best-funded, most-cited interpretability efforts in the world, the very team behind Golden Gate Claude and the circuit-tracing tools) asked readers to treat its own findings "like those of a colleague sharing some thoughts or preliminary experiments... rather than a mature paper."

Sit with that, in the spirit this book has tried to bring to every claim it makes. Not an outsider hedging because it has little to show; one of the field's most prominent labs, describing some of its own most celebrated work as preliminary, exploratory, closer to a colleague thinking out loud than a finished account of how anything works. Not false modesty for an audience. A precise, calibrated description of where the field stands, from the people standing closest to it: exactly the self-correcting honesty this book has asked you, from its first pages, to recognize not as weakness but as the sign of a field that will tell you when it doesn't yet know.

## The picture that isn't the explanation

There is another tool worth knowing (the one you're most likely to actually encounter, in a news article or product demo) and it carries its own version of the trap we just walked around.

These are usually called saliency maps, or attribution methods: highlight which parts of the *input* seemed to most influence a given output. A photograph with regions glowing, supposedly marking what the system "looked at" before deciding it was a picture of a dog. Text with words shaded brighter, supposedly marking what it weighed most heavily before answering. *Here,* the image seems to say, *is where the decision came from.*

The trouble, and the analogy that makes it visible: a heat-map showing where a spotlight pointed during a play is not an explanation of why the play turned out the way it did. Knowing where the light fell tells you where attention went; it does not tell you what causal role that attention played. The brightest spot on stage might be exactly where the decisive thing happened, or it might be where someone happened to be standing when something else, off in the wings, quietly decided everything that mattered. Light tells you where to look. It doesn't tell you what you're looking at, or whether it mattered.

Not a theoretical nitpick: a finding, and a sobering one. A 2024 systematic review spanning sixty-eight user studies found a genuinely mixed picture: sometimes these visualizations helped, sometimes made no measurable difference, and sometimes (a real, recurring outcome, not an edge case) actively misled people, producing false confidence rather than real understanding.

Which brings the second correction this chapter has to make, and it rhymes (deliberately) with the first. A striking, colorful "here's what the system was looking at" image *feels* like an explanation: a clear picture, a confident overlay, the satisfying sense of having been shown something true. The honest accounting: at best it's a suggestive clue, a place to start looking, not to stop. At worst (the genuinely sobering half) it's actively misleading, manufacturing the *feeling* of a cause shown when no causal claim has been demonstrated.

Not a flaw peculiar to one tool. A structural fact about the gap between two things that look, from a distance, almost identical: *correlated with* and *caused by.* This book has been building your ear for that distinction since Chapter 5, where the question of whether a capability "suddenly emerged" or merely "looked sudden under one way of measuring" turned on exactly this gap. A method that conflates correlation with causation can manufacture false confidence as easily as real insight — and for a tool whose whole purpose is building trust, that may be the single most dangerous failure mode it could have.

## Why "we can't fully see" is not just an interesting fact

Now we can pick something back up: something waiting since the first pages of this book for the moment it would matter most. That moment is this one.

Chapter 1 told you, early and plainly, that a system that finds its own patterns will necessarily hold patterns nobody can fully read back out: that "powerful" and "hard to explain" are not two separate facts side by side, but one property, examined from two angles. Chapter 4 warmed the thread again at the seam between the book's two halves. Here it is, picked back up exactly as promised: the cost side of the very same coin Chapter 1 first turned over, examined now with everything you've since learned about how these systems work.

Everything this chapter has shown you (the documented progress of Golden Gate Claude and the circuit-tracing work, and the field-acknowledged limits of saliency maps and "preliminary experiments") is not, in the end, a story about researchers falling short of a goal they should have already reached. It is the direct, structural consequence of the very thing that makes these systems capable of anything interesting in the first place. A system trained to find patterns humans never wrote down (never specified, never listed, never could have listed, because there were too many, woven too finely into too much text) will, by that same motion, end up holding patterns humans cannot fully read back out. Not a side effect bolted on afterward. The same fact, viewed from the other side. The black box is not a design flaw someone forgot to fix. It is what "powerful, in this particular way" looks like, from the inside.

That would be interesting even if it changed nothing about how anyone lived. It isn't idle curiosity, though; these systems are already being placed, carefully in some settings and less carefully in others, into rooms where real decisions get made about real people's lives. There, the gap this chapter describes stops being a fact about research and starts being a fact about consequences.

Trust: when a system makes a consequential recommendation (approve the loan, flag the scan, advance the candidate) and someone reasonably asks *why did it say that*, the honest current answer is often close to: *we can find some of the patterns involved; we cannot reliably trace this specific output to its specific causes.* Not a comfortable answer for the person whose loan, diagnosis, or job application is the thing being decided.

Accountability: holding any decision-process responsible has always depended on examining, after the fact, how it reached its conclusions. A process that genuinely resists that examination (not through secrecy but through the sheer scale and shape of what's happening inside it) poses a question oversight institutions haven't faced before: is this even the *kind of thing* that can be examined the way examination has always meant?

Safety: if researchers can't fully see what a system has learned, predicting how it will behave in situations its training data didn't resemble closely becomes correspondingly harder, not impossible, but harder in a way that compounds as situations grow stranger and stakes grow higher.

Regulation: some jurisdictions already require, in some form, that automated systems making consequential decisions about people explain themselves: "explainable AI." The gap this chapter describes is precisely the gap such requirements run into: a demand for an explanation of a specific output, aimed at a system for which no one has built a general, reliable way to produce one.

This book will not tell you what should be done about any of that. Those are judgments for people who will live with the consequences of making them. What this chapter can do is make sure you're looking at the actual shape of the territory before anyone, including you, tries to decide.

## What the people closest to it actually disagree about

One more thing worth knowing, stated quickly: it doesn't need the extended, matched-camp treatment Chapter 5 gave the emergence argument. Smaller, lighter, faster: closer to the brief aside about engineered hallucination demos in Chapter 6 than to anything deserving the name "central debate."

People who study this seriously look at exactly the same evidence (Golden Gate Claude, the circuit-tracing results, Anthropic's "preliminary experiments" self-assessment) and land in different places. Optimists point out that a door many assumed permanently sealed has now, demonstrably, been shown to open: what looked impossible five years ago is documented and repeatable, and the road from "one striking finding" to "a general method" is sustained effort, not a wall. Skeptics note, accurately, that none of it yet adds up to the thing that would actually matter: the ability to take *this* sentence, from *this* conversation, and explain (reliably, specifically, causally) why the system produced exactly this and not something else.

Both positions are held, right now, by serious people looking at the same published findings. Neither is fringe; neither has won. So we'll note the disagreement, in good faith on both sides, and move on: the more interesting question isn't which side is right, but what it would mean for *you* to hold both at once without needing either to resolve.

Here, finally, is where this leaves you. You've just watched this book show you something with a particular shape: real, demonstrable, citable progress in finding *some* of the patterns inside these systems, and an honest, field-stated acknowledgment that explaining *specific* outputs, reliably, on demand, remains substantially out of reach. Anthropic has named a goal for closing that gap: getting interpretability, by 2027, to the point where it could "reliably detect most model problems." Worth knowing, and, by the lab's own framing and this book's discipline about the difference between an aim and a forecast, exactly that and no more: a stated organizational goal, not a prediction about what will be true when the year arrives. Even the field's own ambitions are aspirations, not appointments on a calendar.

Sit, for a moment, with both halves of it: what we can now see inside these systems, and what we still genuinely cannot. And let the next question form on its own, the way the last one did: if researchers can find some of the patterns but cannot yet reliably explain the specific choices a system makes, what does that mean for predicting what it will do as it grows more capable than it is now? And beyond predicting, if no one can fully see, from the inside, what a system is actually doing, how would anyone go about making sure it does what they actually want?

That is not a question this chapter can answer. It is, however, exactly the question waiting on the other side of it.
