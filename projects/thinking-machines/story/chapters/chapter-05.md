# Chapter 5 — Scale

So: what actually changes?

Start with the word itself, because "bigger" is doing more work than it looks like it's doing. When people say a model got bigger, they usually mean one of three different things, and the three are not the same thing at all. There's **parameters** — the adjustable settings, the dials on that control panel from Chapter 1, except where Chapter 1 asked you to picture a wall covered in them, today's largest systems carry hundreds of billions. There's **data** — how much text the system learned the pattern of, the size of the pile it was shown during training. And there's **compute** — how much actual computation was spent doing the learning, how many passes through how much material, on how much hardware, for how long. Three different dimensions. Three different things you could turn up. And — this matters immediately — turning up only one of them doesn't simply give you "more of whatever turning it up gives you." Pile on compute without giving the system anything new to learn from, and you hit diminishing returns fast. Add parameters without adding data to fill them, and you get a system with room to learn that has nothing left to learn from. These three things move together, or they don't move well at all. That's not speculation or a rule of thumb — it's architecture-level fact, the kind of foundation the rest of this chapter can simply stand on.

Picture that wall of dials again, the one Chapter 1 asked you to imagine — a million unlabeled settings, each nudged fractionally, over and over, until something useful came out the other side. Now multiply it by a thousand. Not because the picture changes — it doesn't, not in kind — but because the *scale* of it is the entire subject of this chapter, and you should be holding the image at its true size before we go any further.

## The Same Picture, At a Different Resolution

Take a hand-drawn sketch of a coastline — a few pencil strokes suggesting where the land meets the water — and a satellite photograph of that same coastline. Both are, in a real sense, "the same picture." Both represent the same stretch of geography. Both are recognizably maps of the same place, and a person looking at either one would correctly say, "yes, that's the coast." But the satellite image carries something the sketch structurally cannot: not just a clearer version of what the sketch shows, but things the sketch has no way to show *at all* — individual buildings, the wake of a single passing boat, the pattern of roads threading inland. These aren't details the sketch got wrong or left out by accident. They're not present in the sketch as representable features, at any resolution the sketch is capable of. You'd need a different instrument — more resolution — before those things could even appear on the page.

Something like that is what happens when you take a system like the one this book has been describing and simply build it larger. And the rest of this chapter is going to spend its time asking exactly how literally to take that "something like" — because it turns out that question doesn't have one answer. It has two serious, competing answers, held by careful people who have looked at the same evidence and reached different conclusions. We'll get there. First, the part where the answer is not in dispute at all.

## The Curve You Can Draw in Advance, and What It Took Us a While to Get Right

Here is something that *is* settled — as solid as anything in this book, on the same tier of confidence as Chapter 2's claim that predicting the next word is the entire training task. As you scale parameters, data, and compute together, performance improves. Not roughly. Not "often, in the studies we happen to have." *Predictably* — on a measurable curve, plotted in advance, that has held up since researchers first documented it in 2020 and has survived years of subsequent attempts to break it. Feed a system more of all three, in the right proportion, and you can draw — before you've spent a dollar training it — roughly how much better it will get. That is an extraordinary thing to be able to say about a system this complicated, and it is simply true. This is what the field calls **scaling laws**, and it is among the most robustly established empirical findings anywhere in this domain.

This is the satellite-image frame, doing its first piece of real work: here, the resolution genuinely *does* increase, in a way you can graph, in a way you can predict, in a way that has kept predicting correctly for half a decade running. Bigger, fed properly, reliably means better. That part of "bigger is better" turns out to be true — measurably, repeatably true.

But "fed properly" was carrying weight in that last sentence, and it's worth pausing on exactly how much.

In 2022, a research team at DeepMind published a finding that didn't contradict scaling laws so much as sharpen what "scale" actually means — the same kind of "here's the more precise version of the thing you just learned" move you watched Chapter 3 make when it corrected its own king-and-queen demonstration live on the page. The finding, now generally known by the name of the model that demonstrated it — Chinchilla — showed this: for a *fixed* compute budget, model size and training-data size need to grow *together*, in roughly matched proportion. A model stuffed with parameters but starved of data — all those dials, nothing much to turn them with — actually *underperforms* a smaller model that was fed proportionally more to learn from. More settings is not, by itself, more capability. More settings *fed enough material to fill them* is. This isn't a wrinkle in the scaling-laws finding; it's the refined version of it — current work, years on, continues to confirm that this compute-optimal balance holds up as a broadly reliable way to think about spending a training budget.

And here, before the stranger material arrives, is the first quiet correction of something you might be carrying in unprompted: "bigger is always better" was already too simple — and we haven't even reached the part of this chapter that raises the real stakes on that sentence.

Here's a small complication the clean version leaves out, though: companies training the large models you've actually used routinely — and *deliberately* — ignore the Chinchilla-optimal ratio. They train smaller models on far more data than the formula says is efficient. Meta's LLaMA models are a documented, public example. It isn't that the formula is wrong. It's that training cost is only one line on the bill. A model that's going to be run by millions of people, billions of times, pays back its training cost many times over through *use* — through inference, the actual day-to-day running of the thing once it's built. So a smaller model — cheaper to run, cheaper to serve at that scale — can be the better overall deal even if it cost a bit more, by the textbook formula, to get there in the first place. The theory is correct. The theory is also not the only line on the spreadsheet, and that's worth knowing if you've ever wondered why the systems you actually encounter don't always match the ones the papers describe as "optimal."

That's the first correction of "bigger is simply better" — gentle, almost a curiosity, the theory meeting the spreadsheet and both surviving the encounter. Now here's the part that surprised the researchers themselves.

## When "More" Stops Meaning "More of the Same"

Everything so far has been about *degree*. More parameters, more data, more compute — yielding more performance, on a curve smooth enough to graph and predictable enough to budget against. More, better, on a slope you could see coming.

What researchers actually noticed, watching these systems get larger, was something considerably stranger than "the slope kept climbing." At certain scales, models didn't just get *better* at things smaller models could already sort of manage. They started doing things smaller models seemingly could not do *at all* — not badly, not occasionally, not "with enough prompting you could coax a flicker of it out" — but not noticeably, not at any size below a certain point, and then fairly reliably above it.

Two concrete examples, because the abstraction is easier to hold once it has hands and feet. **In-context learning**: a model picking up a brand-new pattern from just a few examples placed directly in the prompt — no retraining, no adjustment to a single one of its underlying settings, just demonstrating the pattern a few times in the moment and watching the system run with it. And multi-step reasoning — chains of "first this follows, then that follows from it, then the answer follows from both" — which models below roughly a hundred billion parameters reliably fail at, and models above that rough mark start reliably succeeding at.

This is the word **emergence**. And this is where the chapter — and, candidly, the field itself — stops walking on settled ground.

## Two Camps, One Question, No Verdict

Here's the question, stated as plainly as it can be stated: when a capability appears to switch on, abruptly, at a certain scale — is something *actually* changing, structurally, inside the model at that threshold? Or does it only *look* that way because of how researchers chose to measure it?

Both of these are live, serious, peer-reviewed positions, held by careful people who have spent careers looking at exactly this evidence. Neither has won. Here is each one, given the same room, the same respect, and the same specificity.

**The skeptical position** — laid out by Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda, and Sanmi Koyejo in a 2023 paper that asked, in its own title, whether emergent abilities might be a mirage — argues that the appearance of a sudden jump often comes from *how the test was scored*, not from anything sudden happening inside the system. Many emergence claims rest on all-or-nothing scoring: the model either nails the entire answer, or it gets zero credit, nothing in between. And that kind of scoring can *manufacture* a cliff out of what is, underneath, a perfectly smooth slope.

Here's the cleanest way to see how that happens, and it doesn't require a single statistic. Imagine a thermometer marked only every ten degrees — a line at sixty, a line at seventy, a line at eighty, nothing between them. Now watch a smooth, gradual rise from sixty-nine degrees to seventy-one degrees through that instrument. What you *see*, reading only the marks, is: nothing. Nothing. Nothing. *Suddenly — over seventy!* It looks, from the marks alone, like the temperature lurched. It didn't. The rise was smooth the whole time; your instrument simply lacked the resolution to show you that, and so it handed you a cliff where there was actually a slope. Switch to an actual numerical thermometer — one that reads continuously, in tenths of a degree — and the "jump" turns out to have been there all along, just smooth, just gradual, just invisible to the coarser instrument. Schaeffer and his colleagues showed that swapping binary scoring for continuous scoring does something close to exactly this to a number of "emergence" curves: the cliff softens into a slope that had been there the entire time.

**The "it's at least partly real" position** — represented by more recent work, including a 2025 paper by Du and colleagues — turns to that argument and answers, point for point: the measurement critique is real, and it doesn't fully close the case. Their work finds that the *sharpness* of these jumps tends to line up with the model crossing a measurable internal threshold — a specific point in how well the system has learned to predict its own training data overall — which suggests that *something* does shift, internally, in a way that isn't purely a property of the instrument doing the measuring. And, importantly, fairly: even when you switch to continuous, ungenerous scoring — exactly the fix the skeptics recommend — some of these transitions still show up as genuinely consequential jumps. A model crossing from "reliably wrong" on some task that matters to "reliably useful" on it, in a way that isn't just smoother on the page, but is still, functionally, a cliff in the place where someone is actually trying to use the thing. This camp isn't waving away the measurement critique. It's arguing that the critique, even where it's correct, doesn't fully explain away what's being watched.

A coarse thermometer that manufactures cliffs out of slopes, on one side. A genuine internal threshold the coarse thermometer happens to be standing near, on the other. Both groups have looked hard at the same curves and don't agree on what the curves are showing them.

And here is the strange, quietly thrilling part: nobody knows. Not in the sense of "the answer hasn't been published yet, but it's coming." In the sense that the most careful people working on this question, right now, looking at the same evidence, are reaching different conclusions about what it even *means*. As of this writing, neither side has settled the argument — and the disagreement itself has *moved*, rather than resolved. It's no longer simply "is this curve a cliff or a slope." It's "how do you read a smooth curve against a real-world baseline that actually matters," and "how much of what looks like emergence is something else entirely — in-context learning, for instance, doing quiet work that gets misread as a threshold being crossed." The argument got more precise. It did not get settled. Those are different things, and the difference between them is, itself, informative.

Here is what this is not: an awkward loose end this book has been forced, reluctantly, to admit it can't tie off. It's the clearest demonstration in miniature of the thing this book has been trying to model from its very first page. Researchers who have devoted their careers to these systems — who can describe, in formal and exacting detail, exactly what computation runs when a model produces an answer — cannot agree on whether something genuinely new is happening inside that computation at scale, or whether it only *looks* that way from where we're standing. That disagreement is worth taking seriously on its own terms, because it shows how hard it remains even to *describe*, cleanly, what's happening in there — let alone explain it. (If this has a familiar shape, that's not an accident worth chasing too far: it rhymes, at the root, with the stochastic-parrots argument from Chapters 2 and 3 — both are, underneath, disagreements about what's actually happening behind something we can only watch from outside. They're not the same debate, and shouldn't be folded into each other. But they are, recognizably, cousins — two places this book has had to say, plainly: we can watch the behavior with total clarity, and still not agree on what's producing it.)

Before that word does to you what "attention" nearly did back in Chapter 4 — before it quietly starts carrying meaning nobody intended it to carry — it's worth taking it apart in the open.

"Emergent" does not mean the system spontaneously grew something its creators didn't put there. It does not mean some switch flipped on, unbidden, inside the machinery. Nobody serious is claiming that, and it's worth being direct about why: the same ordinary training process from Chapter 1 — the same blind, repeated, "which way is downhill from here" adjustment — produced these capabilities, exactly the way it produced everything else this book has shown you. The same next-token objective from Chapter 2. No new ingredient. No different process. What's contested is not *whether* ordinary training produced these abilities — it did, plainly, the same way it produces all the rest — but *how they became visible to the people watching*: suddenly, at a real threshold, or gradually-and-invisibly until the measuring stick happened to cross a line. That is a measurement-and-prediction problem. It is not a séance, and "emergent" is not a polite word for "we don't understand how this happened." We understand, with full confidence, *how* it happened. What's argued over is how to read the shape of its arrival.

Here's a small, vivid example of exactly how contested even the *existence* of a claimed capability can get, at ground level. In 2024, a researcher named Michal Kosinski reported that GPT-4 was approaching human-level performance on hundreds of "theory of mind" scenarios — tests of whether a system can correctly model what *another* mind would believe, want, or expect, given limited information. Other researchers immediately countered that the tests themselves can be passed through surface pattern-matching, without anything resembling the thing they were meant to measure. Neither side has the last word here, and this book isn't going to try to give them one — this is a different, smaller kind of dispute than the emergence question above (this one is about whether the capability is even present at all), but it's a useful close-up of what active contestation looks like, on a single specific claim, while the larger argument about emergence plays out around it.

## What This Strangeness Actually Is — and the Last Time This Book Will Say So

Here, gathered into one place, is everything this chapter has put in front of you: the curve you can graph in advance, the correction about data and parameters needing to grow together, the strange jumps, the two camps who've looked at the same evidence and still don't agree on what they're looking at.

All of it — every single piece — is the same machinery this book opened with. The same dials from Chapter 1. The same next-word guess from Chapter 2. The same attention, computing relevance scores fresh, every time, that Chapter 4 just finished walking you through. Nothing new was smuggled in at scale. Nothing different was switched on. The strangeness you're sitting with right now — strange enough that the people who study these systems for a living can't agree on how to even describe it — is not evidence that some other ingredient crept into the mixture somewhere along the way. It is, if anything, the most dramatic demonstration this book has to offer of the oldest thing it has told you: novelty, here, is scale, not kind.

You've heard that sentence three times now — as a flat statement in Chapter 1, as a payoff in Chapter 2, wearing a new coat in Chapter 3. You will not hear it again after this page. It has done the work it was built to do — said the same true thing three times, in three different lights, until you could recognize it on sight, in whatever clothing it happened to be wearing — and it has earned the right to stop being said out loud. What it points at will keep mattering, all the way through to the end of this book. The phrase itself, though, can rest now. Consider it retired.

## What This Means, and Where It Points

So: what does any of this actually change, for someone using one of these systems?

It means "just make it bigger" was never going to be a simple answer to anything. Bigger costs more to run — the training-versus-inference economics from a few pages back, returning here in miniature: a larger model is a larger bill, every time someone sends it a question, multiplied by however many millions of people are asking. And bigger can mean *qualitatively* different, in ways nobody fully predicted in advance and the people who study these systems most closely still argue about how to characterize after the fact. That's precisely why the people who think hardest about how these systems get built and deployed watch the curves of scale-up the way meteorologists watch a pressure system forming over open water — not with alarm, and not with confidence that they already know what it will do, but with the specific, steady attentiveness that genuine unpredictability deserves. Whether scaling further will produce more surprises like the ones this chapter just walked you through is not something anyone can responsibly tell you. It follows from the fact that the *last* round of scaling produced surprises nobody saw coming — and that fact, on its own, says nothing certain about what the next round will do. That's not a hedge. That's the honest shape of the question, left exactly as open as it actually is.

But notice what every single thing in this chapter has been about: capability — what these systems, at whatever scale they've reached, are *able* to do. And here is the seam this book is about to walk you straight into: there is a difference between what a system *can* do and what it actually *does* — output by output, in the specific moment you are sitting in front of it, asking it something that matters to you. "Capable of" and "does, right now, in this answer" are not the same claim, and the gap between them is not a footnote. It is a structural fact about how these systems work — and you have, in all likelihood, already run straight into it without having a name for what you hit.

That gap — between what a model can do and what it actually does, this time, for you — is where the next chapter goes.

It's where hallucination lives.
