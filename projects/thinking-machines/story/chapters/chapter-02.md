# Chapter 2: Reading as Prediction

You've felt this one before. Midway through a text ("Running a few minutes," maybe, or "Can you pick up"), your phone jumps ahead and guesses your next word before you've finished thinking it. Sometimes the guess lands, and you tap it without a second thought. Sometimes it's so far off you wonder what could possibly have produced it. Either way: you've watched a machine try to finish your sentence. And somewhere along the way, without ever putting it into words, you built a rough theory of how it's doing that: it looks at what you've typed, and it guesses what comes next.

That, at the level of mechanism, is the answer to the question Chapter 1 left hanging. *What is a system doing, moment to moment, when it produces the next word of a sentence?* The same basic thing your keyboard does: guessing what comes next, from what came before. The difference is scale, and not a small one. Picture a child's crayon drawing of a coastline next to a satellite photograph of that same coast. Same idea. Entirely different universe. Chapter 1 already prepared you for this move: when the *amount* of something changes by enough orders of magnitude, what it can do changes right along with it. Not because the underlying operation got stranger. Because there's so much more pattern to draw on that the results stop resembling the small version at all.

So: there's the headline answer, delivered as promised. What follows is the unpacking of it, because "guessing the next word" turns out to be close, but not quite right. And the gap between close and right is exactly where this chapter wants to spend its time.

## Words as Tokens

Here's the first complication, and it arrives early, because it changes everything downstream. The system isn't predicting the next *word*. Not quite. What it's actually predicting is the next **token**. A token and a word overlap often enough that you could go a long time without noticing they're different things. They aren't.

Picture a box of building blocks. Not finished toys (no pre-assembled little house, car, dog) but a kit of interlocking pieces in a range of sizes. Some pieces are big enough to be a whole, recognizable word on their own: "the," "dog," "running." Some are fragments that only make sense bolted onto something else: "-ing," "un-," "-tion." Some are smaller still: a single letter, a punctuation mark, a bare space. Whatever you're building, sentence or paragraph or entire book, you build it the same way: snap the pieces together, in order, one at a time. Nothing shows up pre-assembled. Everything gets built from the kit, piece by piece.

That's roughly the picture for how text gets to a language model, and how it gets text back out. Before the system ever "sees" a sentence, that sentence has already gone through a process called **tokenization**: chopped into a sequence of these smaller pieces, tokens, drawn from a fixed kit the system was built with. A token might be a whole common word. Or a fragment of a rarer one. A single character. A digit. A punctuation mark. Even a chunk that looks like a word in an entirely different language. Take "strawberry." The system never gets it as a word. What it gets is whatever pieces its kit happens to break that word into, with no native sense, none at all, of what letters built it before it arrived already broken apart.

None of this is a technical footnote you can skip past. It's the ground floor: the level everything else gets built on. Reading what you wrote, producing what it writes back: all of it happens in tokens. Not words. Certainly not letters. And once that's in view, something that used to look like a strange little glitch starts making an entirely different kind of sense.

## The Strawberry Problem

You may have run into this one already. Ask a language model how many letters are in "strawberry," and there's a real chance it gets the answer wrong, confidently, fluently, wrong. People find this baffling. A little funny, even. Some treat it as proof the whole thing is hollow underneath: *if it can write an essay on the French Revolution, how can it not count to eight?*

Here's the answer, and it falls straight out of the picture we just built. The system may never encounter "strawberry" as eight individual letters at all, depending on how its kit is built; it might receive the whole word as a single chunk. The linguistic equivalent of pulling a finished four-stud brick out of the bin without ever touching, or counting, the studs that make it up. Ask it to count letters, and you're asking it to look inside something it has no way to look inside: a piece that, as far as its processing is concerned, is one solid, indivisible unit.

None of that makes the task impossible. It makes it *awkward*, in a precise, explainable way: a mismatch between the level the question lives at (letters) and the level the system actually operates on (tokens). What feels trivially easy to a person (*just count them, they're right there*) turns out to be strange, unfamiliar territory for something that was never handed letters in the first place. Not broken. Not a fraud. Just a small, visible, oddly satisfying consequence of how the input gets built, the kind of thing you can't quite unsee, once you've spotted the mechanism behind it. *Oh*, you might think. *That's why.*

## The Prediction Task

So let's name plainly (no hedging needed here, because this part of the picture is about as settled as anything in this book gets) what the system is actually doing with all those tokens. Every large language model behind the headlines of the last several years was trained to do exactly one thing, over and over, an almost incomprehensible number of times: look at everything that comes before, and predict which token comes next.

That's the entire job description. Not "understand the sentence." Not "know what's true." Not "be helpful," "accurate," or "careful." Just: look at the tokens so far, guess which one comes next, then do it again, one token further along, and again, and again. No second task running quietly underneath. No hidden fact-checking module, no separate process cross-referencing reality. Start to finish, the whole pretraining objective comes down to one instruction: guess the next piece, and see how close you got.

Sit with that, because it's worth letting the size of the gap register: the systems that opened this book (the ones that write working code, hold up their end of a conversation, explain an idea you've never encountered before) all of that traces back to a task this simple. Not "simple" in the sense of unimpressive. Simple in the sense of singular: one job, stated in one sentence, nothing else mixed in. Everything else, every capability that looks, from the outside, like reasoning or knowledge or judgment, emerged from doing *that one thing*, over and over, at a scale that's about to get considerably stranger to picture.

## Training on Almost Everything

Here's where the autocomplete comparison stops being cute and starts being genuinely vertiginous. Your phone's keyboard learned its habits from a relatively small, personal pool: your own messages, maybe some general patterns layered on top. A large language model's training pool is closer to "a meaningful fraction of everything humans have ever digitized and written down." Books, fiction and nonfiction, contemporary and centuries old. Encyclopedias. News archives stretching back decades. Forums, comment sections, product reviews, recipe blogs. Scientific papers. Legal filings. Transcripts of speeches and lectures. Repositories of computer code, written by millions of programmers across decades, in dozens of languages. Not a curated highlight reel. An ocean.

Now bring back the loop you already know cold from Chapter 1: guess, measure, adjust, repeat. Nothing about it changes here. The system looks at a stretch of tokens. Guesses what comes next. Finds out how far off it was (by checking its guess against the text that actually follows) and nudges its internal settings, its weights, fractionally, in whatever direction would have made that one guess a little less wrong. Then it does the entire thing again, on the next stretch. And the next: not across two piles of labeled email, not across thousands of examples, but across that entire mountain of text, with the cycle repeating an almost unfathomable number of times.

Picture, again, that control panel from Chapter 1, the one covering an entire wall, dense with unlabeled dials, each nudged just slightly after every pass. Now multiply it. The panels behind today's large language models hold not thousands of dials, not millions, but **billions**; and each of those billions gets adjusted, fractionally, every time the system guesses and finds out how close it came. Same mechanism. Same loop. Same blind, mechanical, "which way is downhill from here" correction the skier-in-fog picture gave you. Nothing about the *operation* has changed from what you already understand. What's changed is the *amount*: the size of the panel, the size of the pile, the number of times the cycle runs, and as Chapter 1 already prepared you to expect, when amount changes by that many orders of magnitude, what becomes possible changes right along with it. This is "novelty is scale, not kind," paying off exactly the way it promised to.

## What "Paris" Actually Is

Let's make all of this concrete with a single, small example, because this is the chapter's central move, the one worth slowing all the way down for.

Take a sentence with its last word missing: "The capital of France is ___." Hand it to a well-trained model, and almost without fail, out comes: "Paris."

Now ask the question that actually matters: *why*?

The tempting answer: the system "knows" this the way you do, the fact sitting stored somewhere and, on request, pulled out. The way a search engine returns a hit. The way a database fetches a record from a labeled field. That answer is intuitive, and it is not what's happening. There is no entry anywhere inside the system that says *capital of France: Paris*. No labeled cell. No lookup. Nothing to "go and check."

What's actually happening is this: somewhere across that staggering volume of text (news articles and travel guides and history books and trivia forums and a thousand other kinds of writing, produced by thousands of independent people who never coordinated with one another) the sequence "the capital of France is" was followed, overwhelmingly, by the word "Paris." Not occasionally. Not more often than not. *Overwhelmingly*, densely, consistently, from source after independent source, to a degree that swamps every alternative the system might otherwise have guessed. The system isn't retrieving a fact from storage. It's reproducing the statistical shape of a pattern it has encountered, in slightly different clothing, an enormous number of times. "Paris" isn't an answer it looked up. It's the single most likely next piece, given everything that came before; and here the margin between "most likely" and "everything else" is enormous, because the real world has, in effect, written that same sentence in ten thousand different ways across two centuries of recorded language.

That's the correction worth sitting with: this is not "looking it up," in the sense that a search engine or a database looks something up. It is predicting, purely, mechanically predicting, which token comes next, based on everything that came before, the exact same operation Chapter 1 walked you through and this chapter has been deepening ever since. "Paris" simply *is*, by a wide margin, the statistically likely next piece in that context. The system produced it for the same reason it produces any token: because, given the pattern of everything it has seen, that was the best guess available.

## The Question That Sits Underneath

And now, here's where we have to stop and actually look at what we just watched, because something genuinely strange just happened, and walking past it would be a kind of dishonesty.

If all the system is doing is guessing the statistically likely next piece (not "knowing" anything, not in any sense a person would recognize that word) then why does the *result* so often look exactly like knowing? Why does "Paris" arrive in the right place, in the right context, phrased correctly, fitting smoothly into a hundred different sentence shapes that could have asked for it? Why does this happen not just for the capital of France, but for an enormous range of topics: history, chemistry, software, etiquette, grammar, geography, cooking, each one producing fluent, plausible, frequently *correct* continuations, over and over, at a scale no list of memorized facts could plausibly cover?

Here is the honest middle position, the one this book has, by now, earned the standing to state plainly: producing fluent, accurate-sounding continuations across that enormous a range of subject matter requires the system to have absorbed *something* about the structure of how those subjects get discussed. Not "facts," in the sense of discrete items filed away. Something more like (and we're reaching for the least loaded phrase available) the shape of what tends to be said, and how, when a given thing is true, or a given idea is being explained, or a given kind of question is being answered. What that "something" amounts to, underneath, is exactly what the next several chapters are going to spend their time on. We are not yet claiming to know.

And here we have to slow down, because this is the part of the chapter that needs the most care. There's a real disagreement to name. Not the lazy kind, between people who've thought it through and people who haven't. The serious kind: two camps, both full of people who've spent careers on exactly this question.

The first position has a name: the "stochastic parrots" view, after an influential 2021 paper by linguist Emily Bender and her co-authors, including AI researcher Margaret Mitchell. Its claim is blunt: fluency is not understanding, full stop. What looks like knowledge, on this view, is sophisticated pattern-reproduction and nothing more. The resemblance to understanding is exactly that: a resemblance, and treating it as anything closer is a category error wearing a flattering disguise. A parrot can produce a phrase that sounds exactly like a greeting without anything behind it resembling the *concept* of greeting somebody. That, on this view, is what's happening here too. Just with a vastly bigger vocabulary, and a much better performance.

Another serious position argues that framing is starting to strain under the weight of what these systems visibly do: holding multi-step conversations that track context across long stretches, producing chains of reasoning that land on correct answers to problems that weren't in any training set, modeling what another speaker does or doesn't know well enough to communicate with them effectively. On this view, dismissing all of that by definitional fiat (*it can't be understanding, because we've decided in advance that prediction can't produce understanding*) is itself a move that needs defending, not a neutral default.

Both are live positions, held by people who have thought about this longer and harder than most of us ever will, and this book is not going to resolve their disagreement, because it is not resolved. A chapter that pretended otherwise, that picked a side and presented it as settled truth, would be doing exactly the kind of overclaiming this book exists to push back against. So here is the formulation we're going to leave you with, because it's the most honest one available: the result *looks like* knowing. Whether that is the same thing as knowing, a cousin of it, or something that genuinely needs a new word: nobody currently knows. And saying that plainly, instead of picking a side to sound more confident than the evidence allows, is the more respectful thing to do with your attention.

## Sounding Right and Being Right

There's one more thread to pull from everything we've just walked through, and it's the sharpest one, sharp enough that the book is going to need it again, in full, several chapters from now.

A system trained, exclusively, to produce the most statistically likely continuation of a piece of text has been optimized for exactly one thing: *plausibility*. Sounding right. Fitting the pattern that the overwhelming weight of its training data suggests should come next. Not in the guessing. Not in the measuring. Not in the adjusting. Nowhere in the loop you now understand in mechanical detail was the system ever asked to tell "this sounds like it could be true" apart from "this is, in fact, true."

In the world as it happens to be arranged, those two things overlap to an enormous degree. True statements tend to get repeated, cross-checked, restated in textbooks and articles and conversations, written down again and again by people who independently arrived at or verified the same fact, which is precisely *why* "Paris" works so cleanly as an example. The pattern is dense because the truth, in that case, generated the density. But "tends to overlap, heavily" is not the same statement as "is the same thing as," and a system trained only to track the first of those has no built-in way of noticing when the second one quietly comes apart from it.

This is worth stating in the same level, unhurried register Chapter 1 used for the transparency problem, because it deserves the same kind of honesty: this is the direct, structural, *necessary* consequence of training a system to do exactly one thing (predict the next piece) and nothing else. The very same root that produces the fluency this chapter has just spent its length explaining also produces, by the same mechanism, the failure mode this book will return to directly when it reaches Chapter 6. Powerful, and fallible in this particular way, turn out, once again, to be the same fact, looked at from two different angles.

## What Comes Next

So: stand back, and take stock of what this chapter has actually given you. We now know, in real mechanical detail, what a system is doing when it produces the next word of a sentence. It isn't working with words at all; it's working with tokens, pieces from a fixed kit, assembled and predicted one at a time. And the entire task it was ever trained to do is to guess, given everything so far, which piece comes next, repeated not thousands of times, but billions upon billions of times, across very nearly the whole of what humanity has ever written down.

But there's something this chapter has been quietly setting aside, and it's worth naming before moving on. Correct token prediction is a different thing from meaning. The system put "Paris" in the right place without anything inside it holding the concept of Paris: a city, a dot on a map, a three-dimensional place with weather and history and eight million people moving through their days in it. The system has never seen a map. Never stood on a street. Never held a concept the way you hold one; and yet it produces the right word, in the right place, an enormous proportion of the time.

So here is the question this chapter is handing forward, deliberately left open: if prediction is the *task* (and we now know, in detail, that it is) then what is actually sitting underneath it that makes the predictions this good? Not what the system is *doing*. We have that part now, mechanically, in full. The question is what it must be *holding*, internally, in order to be able to do it: what shape "the capital of France is Paris" takes inside something that has never seen a capital, or France, or a city of any kind. That is exactly where Chapter 3 picks up: not with the task anymore, but with what's underneath it.
