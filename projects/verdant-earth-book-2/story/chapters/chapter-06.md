# Chapter 6: The Record

The Watching Wall held the last of the night's cold in its stones, and Maialèn sat on it anyway, satchel still over her shoulder from the records room. She hadn't decided to bring it. She'd simply stood up from the table at four-thirty, when sitting still had stopped being possible, and it had come with her because it always did.

Below, the pass road was a grey thread, the valley still holding its night shape. Frost on the lower terrace steps caught the first thin color before anything else did — a pale gold edge along each stone course, climbing ahead of the actual sun. The Vault House roof lit next, its solar sheeting going from black to bronze in a few breaths. Maialèn watched it the way she'd watched the citation counter three nights ago — telling herself she wouldn't, then not looking away.

She had not slept, but she didn't feel it the way she had two nights running before the filing — that had been a debt accumulating. This felt lighter. The document was filed. Whatever happened next would happen to it, not to her.

The tablet chimed.

She had it out before she'd finished registering the sound — the same reflex that had pulled her hand toward the Trust's portal a week ago, except this wasn't the Trust's tone. Flatter. More clipped — the sound that had confirmed her submission at four in the morning.

*Rights Tribunal — Notice of Acceptance and Procedural Update*

She read it standing, then again, slower — the second pass the one that counted.

*Reference: your petition (Ref. RT-2094-11847, filed 04:00, Day 12) has been accepted for expedited review under Section 9 of the Tribunal's procedural code. A case officer will be assigned within five working days.*

*In connection with the claims raised regarding the Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel's pending recommendations, the Tribunal has issued an administrative hold (Notice AH-2094-0231). The hold applies to the GDPC Technical Council's session item: "Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel — presentation of recommendations following 2093-94 framework review." This presentation is postponed pending independent review of the methodology's scoring criteria. No date for rescheduling has been set.*

"Bixente," she said, to the empty stones, then heard footsteps on the path below — unhurried, familiar — and didn't need to say it again.

He came up the last steps with two cups, steam rising off both, and held one out to her without a word. She took it. The warmth went straight through her gloves, telling her how cold her hands had gotten.

"You didn't sleep," he said. Not a question.

"No. Read this." She turned the tablet toward him, the way she'd turned it a week ago with a much heavier letter. "*Demasiado limpio* — too clean, except this one isn't trying to be clean. It's just procedural."

She read it through again, translating as she went — not the language, but the shape of it. "*Accepted for expedited review.* They're not sitting on it. *Case officer within five working days* — fast, for a body like this. And then —" her voice slowed without her meaning it to — "*an administrative hold on the Subpanel's session.* Bixente. The presentation, the codification vote — all of it, *postponed pending independent review*."

He set his cup down on the wall, carefully, the way he set down anything he wanted to give his full attention to. "The session that was nine days out."

"The session that was nine days out." She found the line again, quieter this time. "*No date for rescheduling has been set.* No date."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The sun had reached the second tier of terraces, and the cold was beginning to lose its edge.

"I've been doing arithmetic all week," Maialèn said. "Nine days, then eight — I knew exactly how many mornings were left." She turned the tablet face-down against her knee. "There's no number now. Not zero — zero would mean it happened, or it's today. There's just no countdown. The clock didn't run out. It stopped."

"Is that good?"

"Not the same as good. Good would be a new date, openly scheduled, three months out. This is — the room went quiet, and nobody's said yet what happens next." She watched her breath go pale in the cold. "But for the first time in nine days, the thing I've been afraid of isn't happening on a schedule I can count down to. That's a different kind of not-knowing. A better one."

Bixente drank his coffee, then said, in the dry register she'd learned to listen for: "Three people at the archive terminal already, when I came up. Before breakfast. I checked the citation count while the coffee was going. Fourteen, last I looked. It was three, last night."

"Fourteen." Maialèn opened the tablet and found the archive reference from memory — *QW-GERM-2079-2093* — the counter still ticking, though this time she didn't feel the need to wait for it to settle. She scrolled the metadata, looking for the pattern underneath the number. "Atlantic — that's the group Yenny mentioned. Mediterranean. And an Andean group, querying their own bioregion's data against ours. Three bioregions. Independently. This has nothing to do with the Tribunal — it's just people finding data that was locked up yesterday and isn't today, and doing what people in this line of work do when that happens."

"The world doing what it does," Bixente said, and she recognized Yenny's phrase coming back in his voice, dry and exact.

"The world doing what it does." Maialèn sat with that, the cup warm in her hands, the sun reaching the Vault House's lower wall now. Then, because it was a habit she trusted more than almost anything else about herself, she said it the way she always said it.

"Let me say it out loud. One: the petition's accepted, case officer within the week. Two: the Subpanel's session is on hold, no new date — the codification vote isn't happening on the schedule that's been frightening me for nine days. Three: the citation count is fourteen and climbing, three bioregions, and that part was never waiting on us at all."

She stopped. The count was complete — three items, nothing left over, nothing missing.

"That's the first time," she said, half to herself, "the count's had three things in it and none of them frightened me. I think that's just what it feels like when the count finishes and everything in it is — fine. Not solved. Fine."

Below, in the courtyard, someone struck the hand bell three times — the planting-day signal. It carried up the slope clean and bright, answered a moment later, faintly, from further down the valley.

"That's the start of it," Bixente said, getting up and offering her a hand, which she didn't strictly need but took anyway. "Inés said to expect us at the records room first. Then the terraces."

Maialèn looked once more at the valley, full daylight reaching almost to the pass road, and slung the satchel back over her shoulder.

"Then let's go down," she said.

---

The records room held the morning's cold less stubbornly than the morning the closure letter had arrived — or maybe she was simply less braced for what might be waiting. She opened the satchel notebook to a fresh page, the way she did every morning, and was halfway through writing the Tribunal notice's reference number when the portal chimed again.

This was the Trust's tone. She knew it the way she knew her own name.

She finished writing the reference number first — not defiance, exactly, more that the habit of finishing one thing before starting the next was older than any of this. Then she opened the message.

*Re: Registration Status — AST-7734*

*It has come to the Trust's attention that a Rights Tribunal petition was filed on Day 12 under your registered credentials (AST-7734), naming methodology frameworks referenced in your now-closed field assignment (MEE-2094-QW-04). Your continued registration status is under review pending consultation with the Compact's standards office. No action is required from you at this time. You will be contacted if further information is needed.*

She read it once, then again, slower, the way she read anything she was going to have to live with. The room was very quiet — the climate system's low hum, the same sound it had made at four in the morning, now in daylight instead of lamplight.

*Under review.* Not *resolved*. Not *outside the scope* — the Trust hadn't even reached for that phrase this time, and she noticed the absence the way she noticed a reading that was missing a decimal it should have had. *Pending consultation with the Compact's standards office.* A different kind of sentence. One with an open end.

Bixente had come in behind her and set down a stack of forms, unhurried. He didn't ask. She turned the tablet so he could read it, and watched his face do the thing it rarely did: not change exactly, but go still for a beat longer than usual.

"This is the one Tomás was talking about," Maialèn said. "The closure was the Trust shutting a door politely. This is the Trust opening a different one, just a crack, without telling me what's behind it." She set the tablet down flat. "It's not termination. Not even suspension — it doesn't say I can't work tomorrow. It says there's a question now, with my registration number attached, where yesterday there wasn't one."

"Could it become something?"

"It could." She said it plainly, the way she'd say a number that hadn't balanced. "A formal review of my conduct — whether filing under my own credentials, naming a framework my employer has an interest in, was improper. Outside *my* scope, this time. Or it could sit in a queue for months and quietly become nothing, the way Tomás said things sometimes do, and I'll never get a clean answer either way."

She looked at the message a moment longer. Then she closed the tablet and put it back in the satchel, alongside the Tribunal notice — the two of them sitting together in the same canvas pocket the way the two institutions now sat together in her week.

Eighteen years, she thought, of a registration number that had never had a question mark next to it. And now there was one. She found that she could hold it without it canceling out everything else the morning had brought. The Tribunal's acceptance didn't make this go away. This didn't make the Tribunal's acceptance smaller. Both were true at once.

She pulled out the satchel notebook, found the next clean line, and wrote a note to a future version of herself who might need it stated plainly:

*AST-7734: under review. Filed anyway. Would file again.*

She read it back once, decided it didn't need anything added, and closed the notebook.

"Maialèn?"

Inés stood in the doorway, sleeves rolled, a basket of seed potatoes balanced on one hip with the ease of someone who'd carried heavier things up worse stairs. She didn't ask what the tablets had said — Maialèn realized, a beat later, that Inés might not even know about the Trust's message yet, and that this morning, that was exactly as it should be.

"The Long Terraces are starting," Inés said. "Are you coming down?"

Maialèn looked at the satchel — closed now, both messages inside it, sitting together where she could carry them without either needing to be solved before she moved. Then she looked at Inés, and at the basket, and felt something in her chest settle that had nothing to do with either institution.

"Yes," she said. "Let's go plant something."

---

The Long Terraces in full morning light were a different place than Maialèn had seen before — not the hushed, frost-edged rows of the germination count, but loud with people, baskets passing hand to hand. The whole slope moved with the unhurried purpose of a community doing something it had done every season for as long as anyone present could remember.

Someone put a foot-plow into her hands before she'd properly arrived — a long wooden implement with a metal-shod blade, worn smooth by generations of hands. "You push, then you lever back," said the young man who'd handed it to her, miming the motion, the blade sinking in with one clean fold of soil.

Maialèn tried it. The blade went in at an angle, turned half the soil intended, and she nearly lost her balance recovering from the lever.

"Again," the young man said, not unkindly, and walked off to the next row — already done with her, which she understood was its own kind of vote of confidence.

"You're leaning too far forward," said a voice beside her — Rosalía, setting down her own basket with the dry amusement Maialèn remembered from the harvest day. "You audit records like you've done it eighteen years. You plant like you've read about it in a book." She put one hand briefly on Maialèn's shoulder and pushed, gently, until her weight shifted back onto her heels. "There. Now push."

Maialèn pushed. The blade went in clean, and she felt the difference — the angle that made the tool do the work instead of her shoulders.

"Better," Rosalía said, already moving on, "for someone who's read about it in a book," and Maialèn found herself laughing — the first real laugh in days.

She fell into a rhythm after that, row after row, the cold morning giving way to the warmth of working outdoors in altitude sun. Baskets of seed tubers came past at intervals — the redistributed backup stock from three days ago, finding their way into ground across three terraces instead of the one they'd been meant for.

Doña Encarnación was working the row beside her. Maialèn hadn't seen her arrive — the old woman was simply there, matching her pace at first, then easing ahead. She didn't say anything about the filing, the Trust, or any of the last twelve days. None of it changed what this row needed: tubers spaced a hand's width apart, soil folded back over them, the next row started before the sun moved too far.

"This line goes deeper than you're putting it," Doña Encarnación said, after a while, not looking up. "Two knuckles. The frost gets the shallow ones first."

"Two knuckles." Maialèn adjusted, and felt the tuber settle properly for the first time.

"Good," Doña Encarnación said, and that was all — but Maialèn worked the rest of the row with the small, warm certainty of someone corrected by exactly the right person.

Inés found her an hour later, mid-distribution, a basket of tubers against her hip while she waited for the next row to be marked out. "How's the back," she asked, with the dryness of someone who'd watched a lot of auditors discover what their backs were for.

"Manageable. Doña Encarnación fixed my depth." Maialèn straightened, working a kink out of one shoulder. "This is the redistributed stock, from three days ago?"

"Some of it. The rest is going to a cooperative two valleys over — they sent a message yesterday, asking if we had anything to spare." Inés handed off a basket and reached for the next. "They're not as lucky as we were. Their D-score suppression went on longer — a heritage oca line they've kept for longer than anyone there can say. Their backup stock came back under thirty percent last month."

Inés said it the way she'd said the seventy-eight, three days ago — accurately, no decoration. "Not enough to replant a full terrace. They're outcrossing what's left with a related variety, to save what genetics they can. But the line itself — the one their grandmothers kept, unbroken — that one's gone. What grows from the cross will be related. It won't be the same plant."

Maialèn stood with that a moment, the basket heavy against her hip, the sun warm on her neck. "Thirteen points felt like a lot, three days ago."

"Thirteen points was a lot. It still is — it's just not the worst version of this." Inés didn't soften it. "We had backup stock. They didn't, not enough, not in time. That's the difference between a loss and an ending, and it isn't a difference either of us did anything to deserve. We're sending what we can spare. It won't replace what they lost, but it'll help the cross take. That's something."

"That's something," Maialèn agreed, and turned back to her row, the morning's enumeration habit already running quietly underneath the work, the way it sometimes did when her hands were busy and her mind had room.

*One.* The methodology review was open, a case officer within the week. *Two.* The codification session was delayed, not cancelled — no new date, but no date wasn't the same as proceeding. *Three.* The citation count, fourteen and climbing, three bioregions — public now, independent of anything the Tribunal did next.

*Four.* The lot two valleys over — the line that wouldn't come back, the cross that would grow into something related but not the same. Naming the difference between thirteen points and an ending was part of the job too. *Five.* The Subpanel's composition hadn't changed today, and the Tribunal's full review was months away, more likely years — a timeline that didn't fit inside a planting day, or a single chapter of anyone's working life.

She set the last tuber of the row, folded the soil over it, and straightened.

*That,* she thought, *is what a partial win looks like.* A row of new plantings in good soil. A basket going to a cooperative that needed it more than this one did. A registration number with a question mark beside it that wouldn't resolve for a long time. All of it true at once, none of it canceling out the rest.

By early afternoon the terraces had gone quiet again — not empty, but settled, people drifting toward other tasks with the loose-limbed tiredness of a morning's good work. Maialèn straightened from the last row and felt the ache in her back and shoulders, and found, to her mild surprise, that it felt nothing like the exhaustion of three nights ago. That had been the kind of tired that came from holding something too long. This was just tired — clean, earned.

---

The records room at late afternoon held a different light than Maialèn had worked in before — long and low, coming in sideways through the narrow window. It caught the dust the way it had on her first afternoon here, before any of this had started.

Inés brought tea and settled into the second chair with the ease of someone who had nowhere else she needed to be. Not supervising — the four days of drafting had been focused, exacting, two people building something against a clock. This was companionable. The climate system hummed its low, familiar note, the light moving a fraction across the table as Maialèn worked.

"My interim report's still due to the Trust. Three days from now." She didn't look up from the tablet. "It's going to look strange, filing a routine closing report the same week they've opened a registration review into the document I filed instead of it. But that's what you do."

"Will it say anything different than it would have a week ago?"

"Everything's different and nothing's different. The facts are the same facts — Lot 441, the monitoring gap, the trial result. I'll write it the way I'd have written it regardless. It's just overtaken. By the time anyone reads it, it'll be a record of where things stood before everything else happened. That's what closing reports are for."

She turned, then, to the document she'd actually sat down to write — not the Tribunal filing, already gone, already out of her hands. This was her own audit report, the formal closing record of Field Assignment MEE-2094-QW-04, written now with everything that had followed it available to reference.

*Finding 1: The original anomaly identified in Lot 441's chain-of-custody record (four identical viability readings across eleven months and three independent testing events) was the result of an automated monitoring-cycle suspension mechanism (Protocol 4.7(c)). This mechanism was triggered by a D-score below the established threshold (2.1 against a threshold of 2.5), and operated as designed. No falsification of records by any individual has been identified, at any stage of the chain.*

She read it back. True, fair, naming nothing that could be argued with — which was the whole point of a finding like this. The system had done what it was built to do. That was the finding, and it was also the problem.

*Finding 2: The D-score itself is calculated against a comparison-set methodology, established under the GDPC's 2079 framework, which classifies high-altitude Andean varietals — including Lot 441's variety — against a comparison set composed entirely of lowland commercial cultivars. This classification has not been revisited since the framework's adoption. The resulting D-scores for Q'asa Wasi-origin lots (range: 2.0-3.5) are systematically lower than scores for comparable varietals assessed under more appropriate comparison sets (e.g., 4.6 for the same andigenum varietal under Mediterranean-circuit assessment).*

"The second one's the real one," she said, half to Inés, half to the page. "The first says the system worked. The second says the system was built wrong, fifteen years ago, by people with no reason to notice — and nobody's looked at it since, because the same flaw that misclassified the lot also reduced how often anyone checked. It's the same finding the document already made. But this is the version that goes in *my* file, under *my* name."

"That matters to you," Inés said.

"It matters to me."

*Finding 3: This methodological gap produced a measurable, material loss. Germination trial results for Lot 441's archived backup stock (Day 12, Q'asa Wasi) returned 78% viability against the certified 91%, a confirmed degradation of thirteen percentage points over an eleven-month transit period during which no re-testing occurred. This represents direct evidence that the comparison-set methodology's effect is not merely statistical, but has real-world consequences for the genetic stock under its jurisdiction.*

"Thirteen points," Inés said, reading over her shoulder. "Written down properly, finally."

"There's a lot two valleys from here that didn't get a thirteen-point finding. They got an ending." Maialèn paused. "That's not my finding to make. But this report ends with a basket going somewhere and a row getting replanted. Theirs doesn't end that way. Not yet."

Inés didn't answer that directly, and after a moment Maialèn wrote the last section — the one that needed, more than any other, to be exactly as honest as it could be.

*Open question: The independent review triggered by this petition may examine only the Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel's current composition. Or it may extend to the broader pattern of geographic bias this case suggests, affecting other high-altitude and heritage-variety collections across the network. Which of these will occur is not yet known, and is not within this report's scope to determine.*

She read it through once, start to finish — three findings, one open question, nothing overstated, nothing missing. Then she signed it: name, registration number, date. The same gesture she'd made on hundreds of documents, and yet this time she paused over it a moment longer than usual.

"It's a quieter signature than the one at four in the morning," she said.

"It doesn't need to be loud," Inés said. "It's just true."

"The council will keep doing what it's always done," Inés said eventually, not as a conclusion to anything, just a thought arriving in its own time. "Whatever the Tribunal decides — a year, five years — the planting still happens every season. The records still get kept. We were keeping them long before anyone outside this valley cared what they said, and we'll keep keeping them no matter what the GDPC does with ours."

"Keeper, not innovator," Maialèn said, half to herself — something Inés had said once, weeks ago.

"Keeper, not innovator. Though today, for a few hours, we innovated a little — new rows, on land that's never grown that varietal before." Inés refilled both cups. "Tomás heard, by the way, before we'd even come down from the Wall. He said to tell you he's glad to have been wrong about the timing, if not the principle — he still thinks the cost is real, he just didn't expect the response this fast."

Maialèn thought of the old man with his stick, *I wanted to see the face that goes with the question.* "Tell him I'm glad he said it plainly, in the room, before the vote. The council heard it and chose anyway. That's not nothing either."

"I'll tell him." Inés drank her tea, and the light kept moving, and for a while neither of them said anything else — exactly the right way to end an afternoon like this one.

---

By the time they walked up to the Watching Wall, the sun was gone behind the western ridge, and the valley had gone the deep blue-grey it went at this hour. The cold arrived fast and total — no twilight to soften it, just day and then, abruptly, not-day.

The sky above them was already doing the thing Maialèn had read about and not quite believed: clearing completely, edge to edge, the first stars visible while the western horizon still held a thin band of color. The same thin air that made the cold this honest made the sky arrive early and arrive whole — nothing softened, nothing smudged.

"There," Inés said, pointing down the pass road, where two small lights had appeared, impossibly far off, climbing a long grade in low gear. "The supply truck. Tuesday."

They watched it come for a long time — the lights growing from pinpricks to two distinct beams, the engine sound arriving only after the lights had been visible.

"My grandmother kept the cooperative's ledgers for forty years," Inés said into the quiet, her eyes still on the climbing lights. "Before that, her mother did. The first entry in my grandmother's hand — it's still in the cabinet, one of the pages that went into the archive last week — is from 1991. The worst years. She wrote down a germination count for one of the tuber lines, in pencil, in a notebook that wasn't even the cooperative's official one yet, because the official one had been lost the year before, in the resettlement."

The truck's lights swung briefly out of sight behind a switchback, then reappeared, closer.

"Nobody asked her to write it down," Inés went on. "There was no archive then, nothing public, nothing anyone outside this valley would ever see. But she wrote it down anyway. One line. A number, a date, the variety's name. I asked her once, when I was young, why she bothered. She said — someone has to remember what grew here. I didn't ask again, because even then I understood it wasn't really an explanation. It was just true, the way some things are true and don't need defending."

Maialèn didn't say anything. The truck's engine was audible now, low and steady, and the first stars overhead had been joined by dozens more, the sky filling in all at once, completely.

She reached into the satchel, found the notebook by feel, and opened it to the next clean page. By the last grey light in the west, and then by the truck's headlights as it took the final curve below, she wrote a few lines. Not many. She didn't read them aloud, and Inés didn't ask to see them. The truck climbed the last stretch and pulled into the receiving yard, its lights washing across the terrace wall and settling as the engine cut out.

Down in the yard, voices started up — people unloading crates, someone laughing, the cooperative's evening continuing as it had every Tuesday for longer than anyone could easily say.

Maialèn closed the notebook.

---

Three weeks later, the light in the Donostia office was doing the thing it did in October — low and amber and honest about the season. It came in off the Bay of Biscay at an angle that made the water look older than it was. Maialèn had forgotten how much she liked this hour. Altitude light had been beautiful in its own way, but this was softer, slower — the kind that didn't ask you to notice it.

The office was nearly empty, the way it usually was at this hour. Her satchel was open on the desk, though what was in it had changed since the last time she'd sat here.

The Tribunal review was still in its early stages — a case officer assigned within the week, as promised, and a request for supplementary documentation answered the same day it arrived. Nothing resembling a finding, and not for a long time. The Subpanel's session remained undated. The citation count on QW-GERM-2079-2093 had climbed past forty — not because she'd lost interest, but because it had stopped needing watching. Her registration remained, as the Trust's single follow-up message had put it, *under continued review, no further action required at this time.* She had filed her interim report on schedule, and it had been received without comment. For now, she'd decided to take that as exactly what it was: nothing, yet.

Bixente came in with two cups, set one beside her elbow without being asked, and glanced at what she had open on the desk.

"That one again," he said. Not a question, and not quite an observation either.

"That one again," Maialèn agreed.

"Some files don't really close," he said, settling into the chair across from her. "They just stop needing daily attention. That's its own kind of finished, I think." He said it lightly, and went back to sorting forms — the same unhurried thoroughness he'd brought to the first time she'd opened this file, five weeks ago.

She looked down at what was open in front of her: the original transfer record for Lot 441. Departure, Donostia: 91.00%. Lisbon retest: 91.00%. Guayaquil retest: 91.00%. Arrival, Q'asa Wasi: 91.00%. The same four numbers, the same too-tidy, *demasiado limpio* row of identical readings that had started the whole of it.

She read it through one more time.

She knew now what the number had actually been. Not a measurement — or not only a measurement. It was a claim, made by an automated process, inherited from a fifteen-year-old framework that had stopped being checked because checking it had been deprioritized by that same framework. A closed loop nobody had built on purpose. Everyone, including her, had trusted it because it looked clean.

The claim hadn't really been *this lot is at ninety-one percent.* The claim had been *this lot does not need to be looked at again* — made, in the end, on behalf of a framework that benefited from nothing being looked at again either. Not a person's claim. The framework's own claim, about itself, repeated four times until it looked, to anyone reading it cold, exactly like the truth.

*Whose claim is this?*

She'd asked herself that question, in different words, on this exact page, five weeks ago. *Someone is telling the truth and someone is telling a polite lie, and I would like, please, to find out which is which before I decide who.* She'd wanted a person. A name.

There hadn't been one. There had been a system, making a claim on its own behalf. It had taken someone willing to ask the question, four times over, to notice that the thing making the claim and the thing the claim protected were the same entity.

She found her pen and turned to the margin beside the arrival figure — the same margin she'd left blank five weeks ago, not yet knowing what to write there.

She wrote: *Whose claim? Mine now too. I signed it forward. — M.E.G.*

Short. In her own hand, in her own voice. At the end sat a small habit she hadn't planned and didn't take out: *signed it forward*, half in one language and half in another, the way her thoughts sometimes did. Rereading it, she decided it was exactly right and didn't need translating, even for herself.

For eighteen years, her signature on a document had meant one thing: *I checked this, and it is accurate.* A statement about the record. But she understood now that it had always meant something else too: *I am the one who checked, and I am answerable for what I found.* A statement about her. Win partial or not — and this win was partial, she had no illusions about that — the statement held regardless. She had looked. She had said so, under her own name. That part was true, and would stay true, the way the best entries in her grandmother's ledgers had stayed true for a hundred years.

She closed the notebook, set it back in the satchel beside the transfer record, and closed the satchel too.

Outside the window, the Bay of Biscay went on doing what it always did at this hour — the amber light thinning toward grey, the water settling back into being just water at the end of an ordinary day.

Maialèn sat a moment longer in the last light, then reached for her cup, and drank her coffee while it was still warm.
