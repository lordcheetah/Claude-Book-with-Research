# Chapter 5: Outside the Scope

The records room held the cold of the night long after the rest of the Vault House had started to warm. Maialèn had been at the table since before first light, not because she'd planned to be, but because she'd woken at four and found that lying still was worse than sitting up.

She heard them before she saw them — Inés's footsteps, and a second set, slower, with the particular hesitation of someone older navigating a familiar floor in poor light.

"You're awake," Inés said, stepping inside. Behind her came an old man Maialèn hadn't met, wrapped in a wool coat the color of the terraces in shadow, leaning on a stick he didn't seem to need so much as trust.

"I didn't sleep. There's a difference." Maialèn closed her notebook and stood. "Good morning."

"This is Tomás," Inés said. "He sat on the council for thirty years before he stepped back. He asked to come."

Tomás inclined his head, unhurried, and lowered himself into the third chair without being asked. "I wanted to see the face that goes with the question," he said. "Eleven years is a long time to wonder about a face."

"The council called everyone in last night," Inés said. "Not just the five of us — the terrace representatives too, and the elders who've served before. For something that touches every family's stake, that's how it's done. It passed."

Maialèn felt something in her chest loosen — not relief, exactly, more like a held breath she hadn't noticed holding. "Thank you."

"It wasn't unanimous." Inés sat across from her, and there was no apology in how she said it, only accuracy. "Eight in favor, three against. Tomás was one of the three."

"Then I should hear why," Maialèn said, and meant it.

The old man studied her for a moment — not unkindly, the way Inés studied things, as though deciding how much weight a statement could bear before it needed support. "I've watched cooperatives pick fights with bodies larger than themselves," he said. "Sometimes the cooperative is right, and loses anyway, and the body doesn't punish them for it — not directly. No one writes a letter saying *because you challenged us, your replacement parts will come slower now.* But they do come slower. The next monitoring visit gets rescheduled. A grant application that used to take four months takes seven. Nothing you could ever point to and call retaliation, because it isn't shaped like retaliation. It's shaped like an institution that has, very quietly, stopped being in a hurry about you."

"That's a real risk," Maialèn said. "I won't tell you it isn't."

"I know it's real. I've watched it happen twice, to cooperatives I knew well." Tomás shifted his grip on the stick. "I voted against it because someone needed to say it plainly, in the room, before the vote — not after, when it would only sound like complaining. I said it. They voted anyway. That's how it should work."

"He's not wrong, and the council didn't pretend he was," Inés said. "We talked about it for two hours. Nobody argued the risk away. We named it as the cost of doing this — eyes open, not eyes closed. That's the difference between this vote and the one eleven years ago, when the council filed comments and hoped. This time we know what we might be choosing, and we're choosing it anyway."

"Q'asa Wasi co-files," Maialèn said, half to herself, testing the shape of the sentence.

"Q'asa Wasi co-files. By name. Alongside you." Inés set a folded paper on the table — handwritten, the council's seal pressed into wax at the corner, old-fashioned in a way that felt, to Maialèn, exactly right. "The authorization. You'll need it for the filing."

Maialèn picked it up, read it once, and set it down with the careful precision she gave to documents that mattered. "Gracias," she said, and then, catching herself the way she always did, half a beat later: "Thank you. Both of you."

Tomás rose, declining Inés's hand on his arm with a small shake of his head. "I came to see the face," he said again, at the door. "Now I have. Good luck with it, whatever it turns out to be." He was gone before Maialèn could find the right answer, which was, she thought, probably the point.

---

Her tablet chimed twenty minutes later.

She almost didn't look at it — the council's decision was still settling into her, warm and solid, and she wanted another minute with it before anything else arrived. But the chime had the particular tone the Trust's portal used, and eighteen years of habit had her reaching for it before she'd decided to.

*Re: Field Assignment MEE-2094-QW-04, Status Update*

*Thank you for your continued reporting throughout this assignment. Following review of your interim findings, the Atlantic Seed Trust confirms that the monitoring-cycle gap in Lot 441's chain of custody has been adequately explained. No evidence of fraudulent record-keeping has been identified at the Q'asa Wasi facility.*

*With respect to the broader methodology questions raised in your reporting: the D-score comparison-set framework is maintained by the Genetic Diversity Preservation Council under its own charter. Questions regarding its composition or scope are a policy matter properly addressed through the Council's annual submissions process — outside the scope of routine audit review.*

*In light of the above, your current field assignment is considered complete, effective immediately. We appreciate your thoroughness throughout this assignment. Please file your interim report, in its current form, within seven days as a formality closing the file.*

She read it once. Then she read it again, more slowly, the way she'd read the escalation response three nights ago — except this letter wasn't angry-making the way that one had been. This one was gentler. Closing, not refusing. There was nothing in it she could write down as wrong.

"Bixente," she said. He looked up from the far end of the table, where he'd been sorting transfer forms into a stack with the unhurried thoroughness he brought to everything. "The assignment's closed."

"Closed how?"

"Not suspended. Closed." She turned the tablet so he could read it. "No fraud found. Monitoring gap explained. The methodology question is — " she found the phrase without needing to look back at it — "*a policy matter properly addressed through the Council's annual submissions process.* Same words as the escalation response. They've decided I asked the wrong office, twice now, and they've decided it very politely."

He read it. His face didn't change much — it rarely did — but he set the form he was holding down flat on the table, which she'd learned, across eight years, was its own kind of attention. "What does that mean for you? Practically."

"Practically, it means I'm not the Trust's auditor at Q'asa Wasi anymore, as of right now. It means I file the interim report within the week, and the file closes, and someone in an office in Donostia marks MEE-2094-QW-04 as resolved, and that's the end of it." She set the tablet down. "Let me say it out loud and see how it sounds. One: the Trust says the assignment is closed. Two: the council says yes." She stopped.

The third item didn't come.

She sat with that for a moment, turning it over the way she'd turn over a record that didn't balance — except this wasn't a record. *Three* was supposed to be the place where a pattern resolved itself, where the gap she'd been circling finally showed its shape. But there was no gap here. There was a decision, and decisions didn't arrive the way findings did, in a sequence that completed itself if you counted long enough. A decision was something you made, not something you found.

"Three," she said again, quietly, almost amused at herself. "There isn't a three. That's not — " She shook her head. "Eighteen years, and the habit just walked up to the edge of something and stopped, because there was nothing on the other side to count. I think that's the first time that's happened."

"What's on the other side, if it isn't a finding?"

"Me," she said. "Just — me. Deciding what I do next, with no one telling me whether it's in scope."

Bixente didn't say anything for a moment. Then he picked the transfer form back up, squared it against the others, and said: "Inés is bringing the records from the cabinet down to the archive terminal this morning. I told her we'd help carry them. After that — what do you want to start with first, the methodology write-up or the circuit data?"

It was, Maialèn thought, exactly the right question, and exactly the right size. "The methodology write-up," she said. "I want the Subpanel named by name before I do anything else today."

"Then let's start." He stood, and the day, whatever else it was going to be, had a shape.

---

The four days that followed had a shape too — not frantic, not slow, just full, the way the weeks before a planting deadline were always full. Maialèn drafted in the mornings, when her head was clearest. Bixente fact-checked behind her, cross-referencing every figure against the notebook and the archive twice before it went into the document. Inés moved between the records room and the council chamber, carrying questions one way and answers the other. Twice she brought younger council members to read the draft and tell Maialèn, plainly, when a sentence sounded like an outsider's rather than the cooperative's own.

The document took shape in four parts, the way Maialèn had described it to Bixente that first morning. The first part was the scoring methodology itself — the comparison-set tables, the "too tidy" mismatch between andigenum varietals and lowland cultivars, written up now with citations to the 2079 charter and the founding survey's bioregional list. Anyone reading it could check the claim against the source in under a minute. The second part was the monitoring-frequency mechanism: Protocol 4.7(c) and the automated suspension triggered at a D-score of 2.1. It traced a chain running from a flawed comparison set to a real reduction in oversight, without a single human decision along the way. The third part was the ledger cross-reference — fifteen lots, nine years, Q'asa Wasi's own germination records against the official D-scores for the same lots. The "too narrow" geographic gap sat beside it, in a table Bixente rebuilt until the columns lined up. The fourth part was the timeline — and the fourth part was where Maialèn finally went looking for the thing she'd been putting off.

"The Technical Council session," she said, on the second morning, tapping the tablet. "Next month. I never actually looked at what it is."

She looked now. It took less time than she expected, once she searched by committee rather than by date: *GDPC Technical Council, regular session — Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel: presentation of recommendations following 2093–94 framework review.*

"Eleven days," she said, reading the date against the calendar pinned by the door. "Not next month. From where we're sitting now, it's eleven days."

"What's the recommendation?"

She read further — the session's agenda, posted with the dry administrative thoroughness the GDPC applied to everything, including, it seemed, the quiet machinery of its own permanence. "The Subpanel's recommendation is to codify the current comparison-set framework as binding Covenant standard. The comment period closed six weeks ago. This session is the last step before the Council votes." She found the comparison in her own field. "After that it's not a working document anymore. It's the standard — the difference between correcting an entry in a ledger and reopening one that's already archived and certified. Not impossible. Much harder."

"Eleven days," Bixente repeated.

"Eleven days until the room that built this gets to make it permanent." She set the tablet down, and felt something settle into place — not quite urgency, not quite calm. The third thing: the steadiness she recognized from every planting deadline she'd ever worked, the gear she shifted into when a window narrowed. "Then we file before that. Not after. Before."

She turned to a fresh section of the document and began to write.

*This challenge names, specifically, the Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel of the GDPC Technical Council, established 2080, as the body whose current composition and ongoing maintenance of the 2079 framework are at issue. Of the Subpanel's six current members, four hold professional or institutional ties to commercial-crop development programs; the remaining two are methodology specialists without agricultural affiliation. One member — appointed via the framework's original advisory subcommittee — represents fourteen years of continuous institutional involvement in the methodology under review.*

She read it back to herself, once, the way she read everything important. "There," she said. "It isn't 'the GDPC' anymore. It's a body, with a name, six people in it, and the document says exactly who they are and how long they've been there." She looked up at Bixente. "An incentive structure made visible. That's still all this is. But now it has an address."

One name on the Subpanel list — the Mediterranean-basin consortium affiliation that had tugged at her memory days ago — went into the document beside the others, same typeface, same one-line description as the rest. Bixente glanced at it as she typed. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing more to say; it was simply part of the record now.

---

The ledger deposit happened on the third afternoon, and it was, by design, almost nothing to look at.

Inés had asked one of the younger council members — a woman named Yenny, who ran most of the cooperative's archive interface — to handle the actual upload. The pre-Covenant ledgers had already been scanned, page by page, over the past two days. What remained was submitting them to the Compact's open agricultural archive, the same network any bioregion's researchers could query.

"This part doesn't need me," Inés said, standing back from the terminal with her arms loosely folded. "Yenny's done this for the cooperative's other records. It's routine."

"It doesn't feel routine," Maialèn said.

"It isn't, for what it's about. It is, for how it's done." Inés nodded at the screen, where a progress bar moved with the unhurried steadiness of a process built to be reliable rather than fast. "Fifteen lots, nine years, scanned and transcribed. Once it's in, it's in. Anyone can pull it."

Yenny pressed the final confirmation, and the bar filled, and a reference number appeared — *QW-GERM-2079-2093, archived* — with a small counter beside it that read, simply, *0 references*.

Maialèn watched the counter. She told herself she wasn't going to — that this would take days, that real change happened slowly, in the background. She watched it anyway. Watching things was most of what she'd done for eighteen years, and old habits didn't excuse themselves just because the moment felt larger.

Nineteen minutes later, the counter read *1*.

"Someone's already pulled it," she said, and heard, in her own voice, the same flat note of a number that had finally balanced.

"Someone's always already pulling things," Yenny said, not looking up from the screen — for her this was simply how the world worked, and had never worked any other way. "There's a research group in the Atlantic bioregion that flags new high-altitude germination data automatically. They've been trying to build a better comparator set for years. They'll have a query running." She gestured at the counter, which read *2* now, then *3*. "This is the world doing what it does when it's seen this kind of gap before. It's not surprised. It's just been waiting for the data."

Maialèn thought of the design history — *the survey went where its authors already had contacts, and none had contacts at thirty-eight hundred meters* — and thought, too, of how different this felt. Not an absence being noticed for the first time. A presence finally becoming available to people who'd been looking for it, for years, without knowing where it was.

"That's the rest of it, then," she said. "Not a cascade. Just — a door that was locked, now isn't, and people who'd been waiting on the other side walk through at their own pace."

"That's the document's fourth part, more or less," Bixente said. "The regulatory timeline. The ledger's public now, citable, independent of anything we file. Whatever the Subpanel decides in eleven days, this exists — and it didn't come from us standing on a table shouting. It came from a cooperative depositing its own records, the way cooperatives do."

By evening the document was complete in every section but one — four parts, each citing its sources, the Subpanel named, the timeline set against the session ten days out. One placeholder remained, where the trial result would go: *[Pending: Lot 441 backup-stock germination trial, scheduled Day 12.]*

"Tomorrow," Maialèn said, looking at the bracket. "Then it's done."

---

The walk up to the Nursery Terraces before dawn was the coldest Maialèn had been since arriving — the kind of cold that got into the back of the throat and made every breath feel deliberate.

The terraces themselves were quiet, the covered rows still dark, frost furring the windbreak fabric in a fine white lace that would burn off within the hour. Inés was already there, with two seed-keepers Maialèn recognized from the harvest day — an older man named Ernesto and a woman whose name she hadn't caught. Both moved along the trial rows with the unhurried care of people who'd done this exact count more times than they could remember.

"This is the last of the backup-stock trays from Lot 441's original archive sample," Inés said. "Eight months in cold storage, planted in trial beds three weeks ago. This morning's the count."

Maialèn crouched at the end of the row, the way she'd watched Inés do that first dawn. The cold pressed against her back from the open terrace. Ahead of her, under the row's low cover, the air was already different — warmer, carrying the green smell of things growing in managed shelter. The contrast was sharp enough that kneeling here felt like a line she'd crossed, half in one register, half in the other.

Ernesto worked methodically, tray by tray — counting germinated seedlings against each tray's planted total, calling each number to the woman beside him. She recorded it in a ledger Maialèn recognized: newer paper, same handwriting conventions, the same family of records she'd spent eight days reading. Maialèn helped where she could, mostly holding trays steady, and once caught one before it tipped — a small thing, the kind of help that didn't need expertise, just another pair of hands.

Tray after tray, the numbers came in close to each other — high seventies, low eighties, the kind of spread that meant something real but not yet a verdict. Maialèn found her hand twitching toward the air, the old instinct to sketch a shape and start counting — *one, two* — and stopped herself. This wasn't a pattern she was discovering. It was a number she'd been afraid of, arriving.

The last tray was the largest — the bulk of the archived sample, the one the others had been a kind of preview for. Ernesto counted it twice, the second time slower, and then sat back on his heels and did the arithmetic the way seed-keepers did, in his head, against the number he already knew.

"Seventy-eight," he said.

The woman beside him wrote it down. Inés didn't say anything. Maialèn did the subtraction without meaning to — ninety-one minus seventy-eight — and the number arrived whole, the way a number does when it's been waiting just behind a door: *thirteen*.

"Thirteen points," Maialèn said quietly. "Below the certificate."

"Below what we sent out, eleven months ago, at ninety-one." Inés's voice was steady, but there was something underneath it Maialèn recognized — not surprise. Grief. The kind that came from a number confirming a fear rather than introducing one. "Real loss. Not a methodology question anymore. A planting season's worth of seed, degraded, while the system that should have caught it was busy not catching it."

"I know," Maialèn said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry yet." Inés was already standing, already turning toward the Vault House, her voice shifting into something brisker — not colder, just faster, the register of someone who'd done this before. "We have backup stock for this. That's what backup stock is for."

What followed was, Maialèn thought afterward, one of the most quietly impressive things she'd seen in eighteen years of cooperative work. Within twenty minutes the nursery was full of people — not panicked, but moving with the brisk economy of a community that had drilled this without ever calling it a drill. Trays of the cooperative's own held-back stock came out of the cold-storage annex on hand-carts, were checked against the planting calendar, and were redistributed across three terraces inside the hour. Someone ran a message down to the Long Terraces. Someone else recalculated the season's planting allocation on a slate by the door, crossing out one set of numbers and writing in another.

On the Long Terraces, where part of the redistributed stock was headed, Maialèn caught sight of Doña Encarnación among the workers — already there, already moving a tray into position before anyone had asked her to. The old woman didn't stop, didn't look over, but lifted one hand briefly as Maialèn passed — not quite a wave, more an acknowledgment that the hand happened to be free.

By midday, the season's planting plan had been revised, the backup stock allocated, and the loss — real, thirteen points of it — absorbed into a system built with exactly this kind of absorption in mind. Not because anyone had expected this specific failure. Because a community that had survived four hundred years of failures had learned, long before the Covenant, that the only honest way to plan was to plan for things going wrong.

That evening, back at the records table, Maialèn added the final figure to the document's fourth section.

*Lot 441 backup-stock germination trial, Day 12: 78% viability against certified 91% — a confirmed loss of thirteen percentage points, consistent with the documented suppression of D-scores for Q'asa Wasi-origin lots.*

She read the whole document through, start to finish, for the first time as a single piece. Four parts. Every figure cited. The Subpanel named. The timeline set against a session now nine days out.

"It's done," she said.

---

The Watching Wall at night was a different place than at midday. The wind was quieter, the valley a darkness broken only by the occasional light of the supply road's waystations. The cold was sharper, too — it found its way in through every seam if you stood still long enough.

Maialèn stood still long enough.

The day's adrenaline — the count, the number, the brisk communal hours of moving trays and revising plans — had drained out of her sometime after dinner, leaving something quieter and heavier in its place. Not despair. She turned the word over and set it aside, the way she'd set aside words that didn't fit. Grief, maybe. For a lot that had lost thirteen points of itself while a system designed to notice did not notice, because it had been built, fifteen years ago, by people with no reason to look.

"That's the number I was afraid of," she said, to the dark, to the valley, to no one in particular — the way she'd talked to empty rooms her whole working life. "That's the number the record didn't have."

She heard Bixente's footsteps on the stone behind her — unhurried, familiar — and then he was beside her, lowering himself onto the wall with the careful deliberateness of someone whose knees had opinions about cold stone. He didn't say anything. She didn't either.

The wind moved up from the valley in its slow, steady way, carrying the mineral smell of the high terraces and, faintly, something green from the nursery below. They sat like that for a long time — long enough that Maialèn lost track of it, the way she sometimes lost track of time during a careful count, except this time there was nothing to count.

"You're going to file tonight," Bixente said eventually. Not a question.

"Tonight. Now, if Inés has signed the authorization — she has, it's in my satchel." Maialèn looked out at the dark valley rather than at him, which made it easier, somehow, to say the next part plainly. "Under my own credentials. Not the Trust's. Mine."

"Do you have everything you need? The document's complete — the Subpanel section, the ledger reference, the trial number?"

"All of it. Four parts, every figure sourced, the co-filing authorization with the council's seal." She patted the satchel, an old habit, checking something she already knew was there. "I read it through twice this evening. It's ready."

"Then it's ready," he said. That was, she understood, the whole of his response — not a speech, not a debate, just a confirmation that what she'd built over four days would hold the weight she was about to put on it.

"Thank you for staying," she said.

"Where would I go?" He said it lightly, but he meant it, and she heard both things at once, the way she'd learned to hear most of what he said.

They sat a while longer. Then Maialèn stood, brushed the cold from her coat, and went to get the tablet.

---

The records room at four in the morning was lit by a single lamp, the climate system's low hum the only other sound. Maialèn sat at the table where she'd spent most of the last twelve days, the satchel open beside her, the document loaded on the tablet's screen.

She read through the filing form once — the Rights Tribunal's interface, plainer than the Trust's, built for exactly this kind of submission and nothing else. Auditor of record: a field that asked for her name, her credentials, her registration number with the Atlantic Seed Trust. The registration was hers, she'd checked, independent of any assignment — the way a notary's seal belonged to the notary, not to whatever document happened to be in front of them that day.

"Let me say it out loud," she said to the empty room, quietly, the way she always did when she wanted to be sure of a sequence before she committed to it. "What I'm attaching. The methodology challenge document — four parts, complete. The council's co-filing authorization, Inés's signature and seal. The ledger archive reference, already cited by researchers in three bioregions. And the nine-day window before the Subpanel's session, noted as the basis for requesting expedited review."

She checked each item against the form's fields, one by one, attaching documents as she named them — a literal list this time, nothing to enumerate beyond what was actually there, in the satchel, on the table.

The petitioner field asked for a name. *Maialèn Etxeberria Goikoetxea*, she typed. *Auditor, Atlantic Seed Trust verification circuit (registration AST-7734).* Not *on assignment*. Not *on behalf of*. Just her name, and the credential that was hers regardless of what any closure letter said about scope.

The co-petitioner field asked for a second name. *Q'asa Wasi Cooperative Seed Council*, she typed, *represented by Inés Huanca Quispe, Council Chair* — and beside it, the reference number for the authorization she'd attached.

She read the whole form through one final time. Everything was there. Nothing was missing. The Trust's letter had said her assignment was closed, and it was; this form didn't ask whether she was on assignment. It asked whether she had standing, and credentials, and evidence — and she did, all three, more completely than she had ever had them for anything in eighteen years.

*This is no longer the Trust's question to answer,* she thought, and the thought arrived in the present tense, the way her clearest thoughts always did — not a memory of a decision but the decision itself, happening. *It's mine. It always was, underneath the assignment. The assignment was just the reason I happened to be the one who noticed.*

She pressed submit.

The confirmation came back almost immediately — a reference number, a timestamp, *04:00, Day 12* — and beneath it, the kind of acknowledgment language the Tribunal used for everything: brief, procedural, entirely unlike the Trust's careful, closing courtesy. *Submission received. Under review.*

Maialèn sat with it for a moment — the lamp, the hum of the climate system, the cold room, the small bright rectangle of the tablet. The timestamp was going to matter to people she'd never met, in bioregions she'd never visited, for longer than she'd be able to follow. Outside, the sky over the pass road was still fully dark. Somewhere behind the mountains, morning had already started thinking about itself.

She closed the tablet, and sat a while longer in the quiet she'd made.
