# The Monitoring Gap

Inés turned from the second shelf with a blue folder — standard-weight, fastened with a paper clasp, a Q'asa Wasi lot number written in the top-right corner in a careful hand that was not Inés's. She set it on the table and stepped back without sitting, the way she had been doing since Maialèn arrived: present, available, not hovering.

Maialèn opened it.

The monitoring-cycle record for Lot 441 was four pages, each one logged and timestamped with the precision that Transit Accord documentation required. She read from the beginning. Lot departure — Donostia, autumn close, her own certification number in the outgoing field. First intermediate arrival — Lisbon, standard viability check, documented. Second intermediate — Guayaquil station. And there: a system flag, printed in the standard Accord red-text format used for automated suspensions.

*MONITORING CYCLE: SUSPENDED. Automated trigger. Lot D-score below re-monitoring threshold (2.1 / threshold 2.5). Per Protocol 4.7(c): lots with D-score below threshold are exempt from mandatory re-test at intermediate stations in standard-transit classification. Cycle suspension logged.*

The date on the flag was eight months ago. There was a timestamp and a system identifier and no human signature — it had been generated automatically, by a system operating exactly as it was designed to operate. She read it again. The system hadn't failed. She had audited enough to know the difference between a failure and a design behaving correctly, and this was a design behaving correctly.

She set the page down and picked it up again. Then she looked at Inés.

"What is the D-score for this lot?"

"Two-point-one," Inés said. She said it without looking at any documentation. She already knew.

Maialèn wrote it in the satchel notebook. 2.1. Then she sat with it.

She had been reading D-scores for eighteen years. The diversity-significance scale was not a simple calculation — it attempted to capture, in a single number, how genetically irreplaceable a seed lot was: how narrow its commercial equivalents, how specific its performance range, how absent its genetics were from the wider network. A 2.1 was the language of limited-resource monitoring. It said: this lot is adequately covered by what already exists. It said: if we lose this, we haven't lost much that isn't available elsewhere.

*Solanum tuberosum* subsp. *andigenum* was a cold-tolerant high-altitude potato varietal. It was not adequately covered by what already existed in the lowland-focused commercial register. A 2.1 for this plant was — she searched for the right word and found it: calibration error. The measurement itself was correct if you accepted the parameters. The parameters were wrong.

"The calculation sheet," she said. "The breakdown that shows how the score was derived. Do you have access to that through the standard archive?"

"We have the lot documentation as filed by the Accord's Genetic Diversity Preservation Council," Inés said. "If that includes the scoring breakdown, it would be in the supplemental section at the back."

Maialèn turned to the back of the blue folder and found it: four columns, eight rows, each field filled in precisely. The scoring algorithm was public methodology; she knew it well enough to read the calculation the way she would read someone else's arithmetic. Cold-tolerance range — documented. Altitude-specific performance range — documented. Genetic rarity score — documented, at a value that looked low to her but was technically supportable if the comparison set was narrow. Commercially equivalent varieties weighting: she slowed here.

The "commercially equivalent varieties" field drew from a comparison set listed at the bottom of the sheet. Six entries. She read through them. Kennebec. Yukon Gold. Shepody. Atlantic. Russet Burbank. Innovator.

She read them again.

Every one was a lowland cultivar. Every one was a variety selected for commercial yield and transport stability under temperate to subtropical conditions. Not one was an Andean highland variety. Not one operated above 3,500 meters. Not one had been selected for frost tolerance in the ranges where *andigenum* had been doing its actual work for the last several hundred years.

The comparison set was saying: here are things this lot is equivalent to. And the comparison set had been drawn from a database that contained no equivalent things.

"Correcto," she said, very quietly, in the voice she used when a measurement resolved into something unmistakable. Correct. The calculation was correct. Every field was properly filled. The methodology had been applied exactly as specified. The score was 2.1 because the tool had been calibrated for a different kind of plant, and no one had flagged it. The automated monitoring-suspension protocol had worked exactly as designed. Lot 441 had traveled from Donostia to Q'asa Wasi with two intermediate stations on record and no intermediate testing actually performed.

She wrote it in the satchel notebook in careful columns. Not a conclusion yet. An observation.

---

Inés left her to the records room that evening, at the end of the working day, with the same unhurried directness she brought to everything. "We close the outer door at twenty-one hundred. The climate system runs on its own overnight. If you need anything from the shelving, it's all open." She paused at the door. "There's soup in the common room if you want it."

Maialèn thanked her and stayed.

The records room was quiet after the cooperative day had ended — just the low mechanical breathing of the climate system and the cold outside the stone walls, which she could feel through the floor despite the insulation. She pulled her heavy overshirt from her satchel and put it on, then pulled the chair square to the table and opened the network database on her tablet.

She worked methodically. First: all Q'asa Wasi outgoing lot records for the last five years. This took some time — the cooperative's filing was good, but cross-referencing against the Accord's regional transit database required navigating two separate index systems that didn't always agree on lot identifiers. She printed forty-three lot records and laid them out on the table in order of date.

Then: D-scores. She wrote them in the satchel notebook in a single column, in the order the lots were filed. The column ran down the page like a measurement taken at intervals along a slope.

Most of the scores fell between 2.0 and 3.5. A few touched 4.0. None reached 5.0.

She sat with the column for a moment. Then she opened the Accord's regional averages database and pulled the Atlantic circuit D-score average for the same varietal classes over the same five-year window. She wrote it at the bottom of the column, separated by a line: 5.2.

Then the Mediterranean circuit average for equivalent varietal classes: 5.1.

She set the pen down and looked at the two rows of numbers for a long time. The room was very still. Outside, the mountain had gone entirely dark; through the narrow window she could see a section of sky holding more stars than she was accustomed to, the altitude stripping away the atmospheric scatter that normally made them soft. They looked very precise. She thought, irrelevantly, that the sky at this elevation looked something like a record — everything visible, everything in its exact position, the distance and brightness telling you something about the physics if you knew how to read it.

She raised one finger and traced a shape in the air in front of her — a sketch of geometry, not drawing anything specific, just giving the pattern a form outside her head while she thought through it. She did this when data resolved into something that had a shape.

"That's three things," she said to the empty room. Her voice came back flat from the stone walls. "D-score floor, across the full lot range. Monitoring suspension for Lot four-forty-one on the basis of the D-score. Systematic gap between Q'asa Wasi's lot scores and the circuit average for the same varietal classes." She looked at the column of numbers. "By more than two full points."

She sat with it. Outside, something moved on the terrace — a sound she couldn't identify, the cooperative settling into night.

"Three is not a coincidence."

She wrote it in the notebook. Then she wrote the three items beneath it, numbered, with their specific values. Not a conclusion. Still an observation. But an observation with a shape she recognized.

---

In the morning, Inés came to the records room before breakfast to tell her she was going up to the Nursery Terraces for the daily germination check, and that Maialèn was welcome to come along if she wanted to see the morning routine. It was offered in the tone of a person who had made a decision about something overnight — not quite an invitation, more like a considered extension.

Maialèn pulled on her coat and went.

The terrace complex was two minutes from the Vault House, sheltered from the pre-dawn wind by the stone walls on three sides that she had seen from the receiving yard. The contrast at the gap in the windbreak was as sharp as the day she'd arrived: cold mountain air outside, and then — stepping through — warmth, the concentrated smell of growing things, the particular green density of a working nursery in full season.

The morning check had its own tempo. Two technicians moving along the tray rows without hurrying, each row labeled with a varietal code in a consistent hand. They worked from a standardized germination sheet — tray ID, day-count, percentage emergence, any anomalies. One technician read the count; the other wrote. They did not explain themselves or their methods; they proceeded, and Maialèn walked behind them and watched without asking questions until she had watched long enough to ask useful ones.

"The codes on the tray labels," she said to Inés, after the second row. "Are these the Accord standard varietal identifiers, or the cooperative's own coding?"

"Ours," Inés said. "We maintain our own in addition to the Accord format. For internal cross-reference — some of our lots have Accord identifiers that don't capture the local subvariety distinctions. We use a parallel system for the terraces."

Maialèn wrote it in the satchel notebook, not because it was evidence of anything, but because it was the kind of operational detail she needed to understand the place.

The air in the nursery was alive in a way she did not have a better word for. The seedlings in their trays were various — some already showing the particular dark-green of established potato stock, some still the pale translucent green of recent emergence, their first paired leaves the size of a thumbnail. The light coming over the east wall was cold and flat and turning gold in the way high-altitude mornings turned gold, as if the sun needed a moment at this elevation to remember what it was doing. She stopped at one tray and looked at the seedlings there without touching them.

"*Andigenum* subvariety?" she asked.

"Yes," Inés said, from two steps ahead. "Late-season trial. We're testing germination performance on this batch against our reference stock for the previous five years. The results have been consistent."

Maialèn looked at the seedlings a moment longer. Then, without changing her register, she said: "I looked at the D-score records for Q'asa Wasi's outgoing lots last night. All forty-three from the last five years."

Inés didn't stop moving, but her pace settled into a very slight stillness.

"The lot scores cluster between two-point-zero and three-point-five," Maialèn continued. "The Atlantic circuit average for the same varietal classes is five-point-two. I wanted to ask — does the cooperative have a sense of how the scoring methodology applies to high-altitude varietals? Whether it captures what these plants actually represent, in terms of genetic value?"

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. In those two seconds Inés's attention moved from the seedling tray she'd been examining to the middle distance, and then back — a brief, controlled absence of expression that was itself a form of expression. She looked at the seedlings in the tray in front of her the way she might look at something she was choosing her next words around.

Then she said, precisely: "We are familiar with the scoring system."

Nothing more. The technicians continued their count. The morning continued.

They finished the terrace check and walked back to the Vault House for breakfast, and Maialèn asked about the Q'asa Wasi growing cycle and the current season's objectives and the cooperative's research partnership with the Cusco bioregional office, and Inés answered each question clearly and without apparent evasion. The conversation was technically uninterrupted.

But Maialèn catalogued the pause. She wrote it in the satchel notebook in her shorthand, later, after breakfast: *Terrace. D-score question. Two seconds. Not evasion. Calibration.*

---

That evening, she drafted the escalation.

The records room's flat light was the same at evening as at midday — the narrow window's angle didn't change much. She had the table to herself; Inés had left her the access key. She wrote a first draft by hand in the satchel notebook, then transferred it to the official escalation form on her tablet, going through the language twice for precision before she filed it through the Trust's reporting portal.

The escalation noted: monitoring-cycle suspension in Lot 441's chain of custody, generated by a D-score of 2.1 triggering Protocol 4.7(c)'s automated suspension at Guayaquil intermediate station. The D-score of 2.1 appeared to have been calculated using a commercially-anchored comparison set that excluded high-altitude Andean varieties entirely — a calibration mismatch between the scoring methodology and the varietal class being evaluated. The same pattern, a systematic score depression relative to circuit averages for equivalent varietal classes, appeared across forty-three Q'asa Wasi outgoing lots from the last five years, with a gap of more than two full points against the Atlantic circuit average. She was flagging this as warranting methodological review, and specifically raising the question of whether the D-score comparison set for Andean highland varietals should incorporate high-altitude comparators.

She re-read it. It was precise. It said what she had found. She filed it.

The Trust's response arrived the following morning.

She was back at the records table, working through the secondary documentation for the lot series, when the notification came in. She set down her pen and read it.

*Thank you for your escalation submission (ref. MEE-441-2094-Q). The monitoring-gap finding in Lot 441's chain of custody has been noted and will be retained in the lot file. With respect to the D-score methodology question: questions pertaining to the Genetic Diversity Preservation Council's scoring protocols fall outside the scope of routine audit review and should be directed to the Council's methodology review committee via the standard annual submissions process. The Accord's monitoring framework, including Protocol 4.7(c), is functioning as designed. No further action is recommended at this time. We appreciate your thoroughness.*

She read it twice. She held the tablet in both hands the way she held documents that required more patience than they deserved.

The language was professionally correct in every measurable way. It had been composed — she could tell, from the particular quality of its precision, from the passive constructions and the phrase *no further action is recommended* — by someone whose job was to produce responses that were complete, defensible, and brief. Not a scientist. A compliance officer. The kind of person who understood her escalation as a category of communication requiring acknowledgment, rather than as a finding that might benefit from someone thinking about it.

"Noted," she said to the empty room. "Filed." She set the tablet flat on the table. "The monitoring-gap finding has been retained in the lot file." She paused. "Where it will continue to be noted. And retained."

She picked up her pen. In the satchel notebook, under the escalation entry, she wrote the date of the response, its reference number, and its key language — just the facts, no annotation. Nothing that would make it harder to read clearly later, when she needed to.

At the bottom of the entry she wrote: *A.S.T. response time: 16 hrs. Language: correct.*

She underlined *correct*. Then she moved on.

---

She asked for the meeting that evening, before dinner.

Inés came to the records room at the end of the afternoon cooperative session, when the technicians had gone to the common house and the Vault House was quiet except for the climate system's steady breath. She sat across the table from Maialèn with the unhurried quality she brought to everything — not settling in, not crossing her arms, just sitting, present, watching.

Maialèn had her satchel notebook closed on the table. She had thought through how to say this on the morning terrace walk and had settled on the plainest version.

"I've been here four days," she said. "I've done the official documentation review correctly. I've filed my escalation through the proper channel. I want to ask for something that isn't standard audit procedure."

Inés waited. She was very good at waiting.

"Does the cooperative maintain its own germination records — the cooperative's own, separate from the Accord-format archive? The older ones. The records that predate the network's current documentation standards."

The pause before Inés answered was different from the pause on the terrace. That had been two seconds of calibration. This was longer, quieter — not evasive, not hostile, but interior, the look of someone running a calculation that had nothing to do with arithmetic. She looked at the satchel notebook on the table, not at Maialèn, for a moment. Then she looked up.

"Why do you want those records?"

The question was direct. It deserved the same.

"Because if the scoring methodology is misapplying its comparison set to these varietals," Maialèn said, "the cooperative's own performance data would show that. Eighteen years of your germination records, your viability outcomes, your altitude-specific performance notes — that's a comparison set that actually fits these plants. If the D-score is predicting limited genetic value because it's been told to compare these lots to Russet Burbanks, your internal records would tell a different story." She said it plainly. "I want to see if your data matches what the score predicts it should."

Inés studied her. It was the same assessment that had been going on since the receiving yard — the same measuring-out, the same careful calibration of the kind of person who had arrived. Maialèn sat with it. She had learned, over eighteen years of audits, that patience was not passivity — it was a specific skill, the skill of giving the important answers room to arrive.

Finally Inés said: "Come back in the morning."

---

On the way back to her room, in the cold that had settled fully over the terraces now, stars very precise above the stone walls, Maialèn opened the satchel notebook under her arm, walking slowly enough to write.

She wrote: *Three things. D-score floor. Lot 441 suspension. Systematic gap.*

Then below it: *Four. The pause before she answered.*

She underlined it.
