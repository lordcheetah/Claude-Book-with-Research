# Lot 441

The last form in the folder was a germination confirmation for a heritage rye lot out of Bordeaux — forty-two pages, which was eight more than it needed to be, and Maialèn had been holding this opinion for the better part of an hour. She closed the file, dated the cover sheet, and set it in the outgoing stack with more precision than strictly necessary.

The office was nearly empty. Outside the window, the Bay of Biscay caught the late-afternoon light in a way that made the water look older than it was — that particular slant the sun took in October, low and amber and honest about the season. She had always liked this hour in Donostia. The city knew what time it was.

"Right," she said, to no one. "Seventeen lots reconciled. Three pending follow-up. One unnecessary." She tapped the Bordeaux rye with a finger. "Eight unnecessary pages."

Bixente Agirre looked up from the cold-storage manifest he was closing out across the room. He had the patient look of someone who had stopped being startled by her talking to herself three years ago.

"The Bordeaux form," she said. "They duplicated the secondary testing record. Both copies are identical to the sixth decimal. Someone pressed a button twice and nobody removed it."

"Noted," Bixente said. He turned back to the manifest. He was a quiet man, methodical in the way of people who genuinely like cold-storage facilities — the stillness, the low hum, the way climate-controlled air smells like time being properly managed. Maialèn had worked with him for eight years. She trusted the way he moved through a vault the way she trusted her grandmother's handwriting: you could read it and know a careful person had been there.

She pulled her satchel from under the desk and began, by habit, logging the day's completions in the inside-cover notebook she kept there. Not the official log — that she filled correctly and exactly, because that was the job. The satchel notebook was hers. Shorthand, margins, questions she hadn't answered yet. Her sister had sent her a new one for her birthday last month; this one was on its third cover, resewn twice.

The cross-reference list for the week was tucked behind the current page. She pulled it out to check what was left.

Most of the pending items were administrative: a delayed viability certificate from a quinoa lot, two scheduling confirmations for the following quarter's Mediterranean circuit. She ran her finger down the list, clicking each item off in her mind. Bordeaux rye, done. Galician emmer, done. The Canary Islands transfer, done. The Q'asa Wasi reconciliation —

She stopped.

She pulled the Q'asa Wasi folder from the bottom of the stack, where she had set it aside that morning meaning to come back to it. Not because anything looked wrong. Just because the number had been sitting in the back of her mind all day in a way she recognized, the way a half-familiar word sat at the back of your tongue.

The lot was 441 — a seed lot of *Solanum tuberosum* subsp. *andigenum*, a cold-tolerant Andean potato varietal with high genetic diversity value. Eleven months ago she had certified it herself, here in this building, at the Donostia vault: outgoing viability 91.00%, chain of custody intact, lot-transfer documentation signed and filed. Standard northern-Atlantic to high-Andean circuit. Two intermediate stops — a testing station in Lisbon, a second in Guayaquil — before the final leg up to Q'asa Wasi in the Cusco bioregion.

Eleven months ago, she had written 91.00% and meant it.

She laid out the arrival document beside the departure document and looked at both.

Arrival viability: 91.00%.

The room was very quiet. Through the window, the Bay held its late-afternoon amber. Maialèn set down the page and picked it up again.

"Let me say this out loud," she said.

Bixente did not look up. He had learned what that phrase meant.

"Lot four-forty-one left this building with a viability certificate of ninety-one percent. That was my number, certified eleven months ago, day before the autumn close." She held the arrival document at a slight angle, as if better light would change what it said. "It arrived at Q'asa Wasi certified at ninety-one percent. Their own testing station, their own technician. Two people who have never met, eleven months apart." She set the page flat on the desk. "Same number."

Not wrong. Not off by a rounding error or a testing-method variance or the kind of minor drift that meant a technician had a different calibration on his humidity sensor. The same number. Exactly the same number.

*Demasiado limpio.* Too clean.

She pulled out the satchel notebook and wrote nothing yet. Just held it, thinking through the shape of it.

A viability certificate was not a photograph. It was a measurement — a reading taken at a particular moment, by a particular instrument, by a particular human being doing their best in conditions that were never perfectly controlled. Ninety-one percent today was not the same thing as ninety-one percent in eleven months. Not after two trans-oceanic transits, two intermediate storage periods, and the pressure gradient that came with shipping seed stock from sea level to 3,800 meters. A real lot, traveling that distance, through two independent testing stations, would show drift. It might show very small drift. It might show drift only at the third decimal. But it would not show no drift, because no drift was not a measurement; it was a coincidence, and she had been doing this work for eighteen years and coincidences of this quality did not exist.

There were three reasons the number should have moved. Transit time — eleven months was long, even for a well-packaged lot. Altitude change — the pressure and humidity gradient between Donostia and Q'asa Wasi was significant and well-documented. Two independent retest events — Lisbon and Guayaquil — each introducing the minor, unavoidable variance of a different machine, a different technician, a different atmospheric reading.

"Three," she said. "That's three reasons it should have moved." She turned to the cross-reference manifest and ran her finger across the row for Lot 441. Every column correct. Every signature in the right place. "Three is not a coincidence. Three is a pattern."

Bixente had gone quiet in the way that meant he was listening.

"The number is ninety-one percent at departure," she said. "Ninety-one percent at Lisbon retest. Ninety-one percent at Guayaquil retest. Ninety-one percent at Q'asa Wasi receipt. Four identical readings over eleven months across three testing events." She was counting on her fingers now, not performing it for him, just — she needed the numbers to have a place in her hands as well as her head. "That's not a number that traveled. That's a number someone wrote down."

She said it four times in her head before she said it aloud, and then she sat with what it meant.

Bixente crossed the room and read both documents over her shoulder. He was not an auditor; he was a cold-storage technician, which meant he knew, practically speaking, exactly how these numbers behaved. He said nothing for a while.

"You've seen something like this before?" he said.

"No," she said. "I've seen numbers that were wrong. A wrong number is easy — it's a number that doesn't match what the lot actually did, which means the lot is either better or worse than the record says. This is not that." She tapped the arrival document. "This number may be completely accurate. Q'asa Wasi's technician may have opened the lot, run the viability test, and gotten ninety-one percent, fair and true. The lot is probably exactly what it says it is."

She paused. "That is almost the most interesting part of it."

"Then what's wrong with it?"

"What's wrong with it is that the intermediate stations didn't move it. Two independent retests over eleven months, and the number never changed, not even at the margin. Which means either both intermediate stations recorded, to the hundredth of a percent, the exact same reading as my original certification — which is not how real measurements work — or the intermediate test records were filled in from my original certificate, rather than from an actual new test."

Bixente was quiet for a moment. "Not a wrong number. A number that was never rechecked."

"Exactly." She closed the folder and set both documents in her satchel, with the care she gave to things that were going to need to travel with her. "There's a difference."

She closed the satchel. Outside, the Bay of Biscay was losing its amber; the light was going gray and level, the sea becoming just the sea again at the end of a working day.

---

Bixente stayed until she finished her notes. He was being pulled the following week to the southern circuit — a backlog of audit confirmations in the Catalonian seed bank network that had been waiting longer than they should have, and which the Trust's scheduling coordinator had, at some point in the last month, apparently decided counted as more urgent than anything else on his plate. He was not happy about the timing. He showed this by saying nothing about it.

"I'll go alone," Maialèn said. It was not a request for reassurance. It was a fact she was already sorting into the logistics column.

"Q'asa Wasi isn't a difficult circuit," he said. "They're good people. The Council chair — you'll like her, actually, she's about as patient as you are and about as easy to rush."

"Good." She was writing a packing list in the satchel notebook, two columns: vault items and field items. Cold-vault certification kit. Altitude kit. She had done the Cusco circuit before, twice, though not this station. "Tell me what I need to know about the approach."

"High road. Three hours by road from the valley rail head — there's a cooperative transport that runs Tuesdays and Fridays. The station is small; you'll see the terraces before you see the buildings." He pulled his coat from the rack by the door. "The air is genuinely thin up there. Give yourself a day before you trust your own head."

She made a note of this. She had learned not to argue with Bixente about the physical realities of places he had been.

"Keep me posted," he said. It was not a question. They had a long-standing arrangement about this: she told him what she found, before she told the Trust, because he was the one who had to continue working with the facilities after she left and he deserved not to be surprised. She had never once regretted this arrangement.

"Of course," she said. She stood and put her coat on. The Bay was dark now beyond the window, the last light gone, the city settling into its October evening. Donostia at night smelled like salt and brine and the low, clean smell of coastal biofilm from the restoration project along the breakwater — a smell that had taken twenty years to arrive and which she privately thought was one of the best things the Covenant had ever done.

"Safe transit," Bixente said.

"It's a seed vault audit, not an expedition." But she said it warmly.

---

The transit to Lima was eleven hours, the onward connection to Cusco another two, and the cooperative transport up the pass road three after that. Maialèn slept through most of the first leg with her satchel under her head, the way she had learned to sleep in transit years ago — not deeply, but usefully, the kind of sleep that took the edge off the hours without making her lose her sense of where she was in the journey.

She did not sleep through the final approach.

The pass road was narrow and very old — there were sections where the original Inca-period stonework showed beneath the Covenant-era composite resurfacing, a layer of engineering on top of a layer of engineering on top of a layer of engineering, each one saying the same thing about the mountain: that you could go this way if you respected it. The transport climbed in long switchbacks. Below, the valley was a geometry of green — terrace after terrace stepping down toward the river, each one catching the flat early light of afternoon.

She felt the altitude before she saw the evidence of it. Not dramatically — not the way films about high-altitude expeditions showed it, as a wall you walked into. More quietly: a slight widening at the edge of each breath, a reminder that she was working harder for the same amount of air and that this would simply be true from here forward, until she left. *El aire fino.* The thin air. She had a colleague from the Andean bioregional office who called it "the mountain's entry fee," which Maialèn thought was too poetic but also not wrong.

Q'asa Wasi came into view as the road crested the last major switchback before the final approach, and she understood, for the first time, what Bixente had meant about the terraces.

They stepped down the mountainside in long, precise curves, following the natural fall of the slope in a way that looked both inevitable and worked-for: you could see the centuries in them, the accumulated decisions about drainage and sun exposure and frost risk that had produced, over hundreds of years, this particular geometry. Stone edges, some very old, some recently replaced with composite block where the old stone had failed. Green plots, some already turned for the late-season planting, some still holding their final crop of the warm months. Everything maintained. Everything in use.

The buildings came into view as the transport rounded the last curve — low, heavy-walled, stone and composite in the combination that was Q'asa Wasi's particular signature, the new materials married to the old technique so naturally that you had to look carefully to see where one ended and the other began. The Vault House was the largest structure, built half into the slope on the north side of the main terrace cluster, its roof covered in solar sheeting that caught the high-altitude sun with a particular intensity. There was a low-lying nursery terrace complex below it, sheltered from the wind by stone walls on three sides, and from here she could see the green of it even from the road.

The air outside the transport, when she stepped down at the cooperative's receiving yard, was dry and cold and carried a faint mineral sharpness she associated with no other kind of place on earth — the particular smell of high stone and thin sky and an absence of the kind of humidity she was used to. She stood for a moment with her satchel on her shoulder and simply breathed it, giving herself and her body time to register the change.

There were three people standing along the long stone terrace-edge that ran above the receiving yard, looking down the pass road. Looking, she realized, at the transport she had arrived on. One of them was a teenager; the other two were older, one woman and one man, talking quietly without looking at each other. As the transport's driver unloaded the supply crates, they watched with the unhurried attention of people who had been watching this road their whole lives and knew everything that arrived along it.

The Watching Wall. She had read about it in the cooperative's profile document. She had not expected it to look so natural — not a formal installation, just the place where people stood when they wanted to know what was coming.

She walked up the stone steps from the receiving yard to the main terrace level. The cold air went down clean and specific into her lungs. As she passed the nursery terrace wall — three stone courses of it catching the low sun, a gap in the windbreak showing the sheltered interior — she caught the smell of it: warm, green, faintly grassy, the particular alive smell of growing things in managed shelter. It was so different from the cold mineral air outside the wall that the contrast was almost a sound. She stood for a moment at the gap, breathing. Then she kept walking, thinking about Lot 441 and what it meant to have come this far to ask about a number that hadn't moved.

---

The Council chair came to meet her herself, which Maialèn noted: at most cooperative stations, an auditor from the Atlantic Seed Trust was received by a records clerk or a liaison officer, not by the head of the governance council. It meant something, though she was not yet sure what. Possibly courtesy. Possibly calibration.

Her name was Inés Huanca Quispe, and she was perhaps sixty-five — a tall woman for the altitude, with the particular posture of someone who had spent a lifetime on terraces, an uprightness that had nothing to do with formality. Her hands, when she offered one to shake, were calloused in the specific places that came from working with soil rather than paper. She wore a heavy wool jacket over Covenant-era insulated base layers, and she assessed Maialèn with a directness that Maialèn found immediately comfortable: this was a person who looked at what was in front of her.

"Señora Etxeberria," she said. Her Spanish had the cadence of the high Andes in it, Quechua rhythms in the vowel lengths, unhurried and precise. "We have been expecting you. Your transfer request came through last week."

"Thank you for receiving me. I know the timing is late in the season."

"We have the season we have." A slight smile. "Come in. I'll show you the records room before the light goes."

The Vault House entry was a brief darkness between the cold outside air and the vault's climate-controlled interior. On the far side of the entry lobby, through a heavy composite door, was the cold-vault itself — she could feel the temperature difference even standing at the threshold, that specific chilled stillness she had spent eighteen years reading as a sign of something being properly kept. To the left was the records room: a small stone-walled space, one table, two chairs, shelving along three walls holding the official archive in standard Trust format, and a locked cabinet in the near corner that she noted and did not mention.

The light through the narrow window was the flat gold of late afternoon at altitude, which was different from late-afternoon light anywhere else — cleaner, somehow, without the atmosphere to filter it, and slightly harder on the eyes because of it.

"Standard documentation," Inés said, indicating the shelves with the manner of someone who had done this many times. "All lots received in the last twenty years in the standard format. Annual viability checks, certified and logged. Transfer records for outgoing lots in the second section." She paused and looked at Maialèn with that same directness. "You asked specifically about Lot four-forty-one."

"I did. I certified it at Donostia eleven months ago."

"Then you know the record." Not a question. An acknowledgment. "We received it in September. The viability test was run by our own technician on receipt, as per standard protocol. Ninety-one percent, consistent with the departure certificate."

"Yes," Maialèn said. "I'd like to verify the testing records directly, if I may."

"Of course." Inés pulled the relevant folder from the second section and set it on the table. Everything was in order. The incoming lot documentation, the receipt chain of custody, the viability test result from Q'asa Wasi's own station — technician name, instrument calibration date, testing protocol followed. All of it exactly what it should be.

Maialèn sat at the table and read through it twice. She cross-referenced the instrument calibration record against the testing date. She checked the technician's certification number against the Q'asa Wasi staff registry. She ran the calculation herself, on paper, from the raw germination count the technician had recorded: 273 viable seeds out of 300 tested. Ninety-one percent, precisely. The math was correct. The procedure was correct. The lot was real.

She wrote nothing for a long moment.

"Is there something wrong?" Inés said. She had remained standing; she was watching Maialèn with the patience of someone who had hosted many auditors and had learned, over the years, that the important ones went quiet when they found something.

"Not with your records," Maialèn said. "Your documentation is excellent. I mean that as a technical observation, not a courtesy." She set down her pen. "The lot is exactly what the record says it is. Q'asa Wasi received what the transfer document says they received. I can see that clearly."

Inés waited.

Maialèn looked at the departure certificate — her own number, from eleven months ago — and the arrival certificate, and the two intermediate testing records from Lisbon and Guayaquil, which she had printed and brought with her. She laid them in a row on the table. Four documents. Four instances of the same number.

She had almost filed a clean report on the train from Cusco. The anomaly was explained; the lot had arrived as certified; the record was telling the truth. That was, technically, the finding.

*Truth about what, exactly?*

She said it to herself in the voice she used for sequences — the enumerating voice, the one that meant she was about to catch something. The record was telling the truth. The record was saying that this lot had been tested four times and the number had not changed. The record was telling her a true and specific and very interesting thing: that the intermediate stations had looked at this lot and found the same number she had found, to the hundredth of a percent, twice, eleven months apart, with no drift.

The record was not lying. The record was accurate. But a record was not a measurement — it was a claim, made by someone, at a specific moment, for a specific reason. And a measurement that didn't move across eleven months of transit wasn't a falsified measurement. It was a measurement that had never been re-examined.

*Why wasn't it re-examined?*

She looked up at Inés, who was still watching her with that measured patience.

"I'd like to see the monitoring-cycle records for Lot four-forty-one," Maialèn said. Her voice was level, professional, the register she used when she was not yet accusing anything and wanted that to be unmistakable. "Specifically, I'd like to see the re-test schedule that should have triggered at the intermediate station in Guayaquil. The one that should have caught any viability drift before the lot completed its transit."

She said it carefully.

Inés looked at her for a moment — assessing, the way she had been assessing since the receiving yard, calibrating the kind of auditor who had arrived. Then she turned to the second shelf and began to look.

"Yes," she said. "I can find those."

Outside the narrow window, the high-altitude dusk was arriving with the speed it always arrived at this elevation — fast, total, the light cut off by the peaks to the west as if someone had closed a door. The cold outside the Vault House would already be deeper than anything Maialèn felt in Donostia in January. Inside, the climate system ran its low, slow hum, keeping everything steady, keeping everything at exactly the temperature it needed to be.

She uncapped her pen and opened the satchel notebook to a clean page, and waited.
