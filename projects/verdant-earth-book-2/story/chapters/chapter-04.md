# The Subpanel

By two in the morning Maialèn had read the eleven-person roster three times, and the roster had not changed.

She made herself slow down. Reading it a fourth time the same way would only produce the same nothing. She pulled the satchel notebook closer, found a clean page, and started again — not with names this time, but with a question. *Where did the original numbers come from?*

The 2079 working group's charter, buried in an early section she'd skimmed past twice, listed each member's task assignment alongside their affiliation. Six of the eleven had done the actual technical drafting; the other five had served on advisory or review subcommittees that met twice and signed off. She copied the six names into her notebook, then their affiliations beside them, and looked at what she'd made.

Three of the six worked, at the time, for cooperatives with established commercial-crop programs — large-scale, market-facing operations that grew Kennebecs and Russet Burbanks by the hectare. A fourth came from an advisory body whose charter described it as supporting "certification readiness" for cooperatives seeking Trust accreditation. The remaining two were methodology specialists — statisticians, with no agricultural affiliation at all.

*Six people,* she thought. *Three from commercial-crop cooperatives. One from a certification-advisory body. Two statisticians who built the comparison tables from whatever data the other four gave them.*

It wasn't a conspiracy — she could see that, even at this hour, even tired. It was a working group assembled the way working groups got assembled: people who had time, whose institutions could spare them for eighteen months. They already sat on three other panels and could be added to a fourth without anyone needing to find someone new. Nobody had asked, at the time, who wasn't in the room. The room looked like every other room.

The taxonomic tables — the comparison sets that decided which varieties counted as "commercially equivalent" to which others — had been built from a dataset the statisticians cited in an appendix: a multi-region germination and yield survey, 2071 to 2076, covering temperate and subtropical growing zones. Forty-one bioregions. None of them above two thousand meters.

*Of course,* she thought, and the thought arrived not with anger but with the flat, settled quality of a number that finally balanced. *Nobody excluded high-altitude data. Nobody collected it. The survey went where its authors already had contacts, and none of them had contacts at thirty-eight hundred meters.*

*The comparison tables were built from a dataset that simply didn't include us. Not maliciously excluded. Never asked.*

That was the design history. She had it now — six names, three affiliations that mattered, one missing region. But it wasn't the thing she'd been looking for. It explained how the methodology had been built wrong. It didn't tell her who, today, could fix it, or why nobody had.

She read the roster a fifth time. Nothing new surfaced. Her eyes stopped focusing on the page and started focusing on the window instead, where the black had gone the particular deep blue that meant morning was still an hour off but had started thinking about itself.

She closed the notebook. The rest of the roster would still be there after she'd slept.

---

The nursery terraces at dawn were nearly empty — one of the younger seed-keepers doing the first check of the morning, moving along the covered rows with a small lamp, though the sky was already light enough that the lamp seemed more habit than necessity. Maialèn found Inés there, crouched at the end of a row, examining something low to the ground with the unhurried attention she brought to everything.

"You look like someone who didn't sleep," Inés said, without looking up.

"I slept. Four hours, maybe." Maialèn crouched beside her, careful of the soil. "What is this one?"

"Quinoa. First-year trial — a line we've been increasing slowly for eight seasons." Inés touched a cluster of seedlings, barely past the first true leaves, packed close in their bed. "This batch came up faster than last year's. Two days faster, across the whole tray." She said it the way she said most things — observation first, conclusion held back until there was enough of it to be worth stating. Then she straightened, brushing soil from her knees. "You're not asking about quinoa."

"No." Maialèn looked down the row — the warm green smell of the nursery rising around them, the cold mineral air of the open terraces just beyond the windbreak, the two registers sitting side by side the way they always did here. "I found the design history. Six people built the comparison-set tables in 2079, three of them from commercial-crop cooperatives, and the underlying survey never collected any high-altitude data at all. Not because someone left us out. Because nobody who built the survey had ever worked anywhere like here."

"That sounds like an answer to a question I didn't ask."

"It is." Maialèn stood, knees protesting. "I asked who built this. I still don't know who's supposed to fix it — and that's a different question."

"Then you haven't found the one that matters." Inés picked up her lamp, though the light no longer needed it, and turned toward the Vault House. "Eat something before you go back to it. You'll think better."

Maialèn ate. Then she went back to it.

---

Bixente arrived on the morning of Day 8 — exactly on time by his own private calendar, which ran on a logic only he understood and had never, in eight years, been wrong by more than a few hours.

Maialèn heard the supply truck on the pass road before she saw it — the engine note carrying up through the thin air with the particular clarity of sound at altitude — and was at the Watching Wall before she'd consciously decided to go there. The truck took the last switchback slowly, the way every vehicle did on that grade, and she watched it the whole way.

He climbed down from the cab with his own bag and one of the cooperative's transport crates, which he set down carefully before he did anything else. Bixente had never in his life greeted a person before he'd secured whatever he was carrying, and Maialèn had stopped finding this strange around year two.

"You look terrible," he said, by way of hello.

"Thank you. I've been working." She took the bag from him before he could protest, mostly because she wanted something to do with her hands. "How was the southern circuit?"

"Slow. Two cold-vault recalibrations that took longer than they should have, and a transport delay that wasn't anyone's fault, which is the worst kind." He looked past her, up toward the Vault House, then back at her face — really looking, the way he did, cataloging. "You found something."

"I found several somethings." She started walking, and he fell into step beside her, the way they always did, the way that made conversations on a pass road feel like conversations in a records office. "I filed an escalation. Five days ago. MEE-441-2094-Q. To the Trust directly."

He didn't break stride, but something in his face changed — not hurt, exactly. Recalibration, the same word she'd used on herself three nights ago. "Without telling me first."

"Without telling you first." She let that sit for a step or two, because there was nothing to be gained by rushing past it. "You were three days out. It felt like something that needed to go in before I lost the nerve, and I knew if I waited to reach you, I'd talk myself into waiting for your read on it too. I wanted it on record."

Bixente was quiet for a few paces — not a verdict, just how he worked, the same way she talked.

"I'm not upset that you filed," he said eventually. "I noticed it, that's all. Eight years, and I noticed it. I wanted you to know I had, and now I have."

"That's fair." She glanced at him. "For what it's worth — I noticed too. While I was doing it."

"Then we're square." And that, she understood, was genuinely the whole of it — not a wound nursed for later, just two people who'd registered a small break in an old habit and named it, the way you'd note a hairline crack in a wall: worth knowing about, not worth alarming over.

"Come and see what I found," she said. "All of it. I want your eyes on it before I read it again myself."

---

She gave him the records room table, the satchel notebook open to the cross-reference pages, the tablet with the GDPC archive still loaded, and two hours of uninterrupted quiet while he worked through it in his own order — never hers, and exactly why she wanted him to do it.

He read the ledger cross-reference first — fifteen lots, nine years, real numbers against D-scores. Then the 43-lot circuit comparison: Q'asa Wasi's outgoing scores clustered between 2.0 and 3.5, against Atlantic and Mediterranean circuit averages above five. Then the design history — six names, three affiliations, one missing region in the founding dataset.

He read it all twice. Maialèn made tea, mostly so she wouldn't watch him do it.

"Let me see the circuit numbers again," he said. "Not the averages. The individual lots."

She slid the notebook across. He turned to the page where she'd listed all forty-three Q'asa Wasi outgoing lots with their D-scores, then — beside it — a second list: the same varietal classes, routed through other circuits, with their scores.

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she almost asked what he was looking at, and then didn't, because she'd learned, across eight years, that Bixente's silences were doing something and that interrupting them produced worse results than waiting.

"You compared Andean varietals against lowland varietals," he said finally. "Across the whole network. And the Andean ones score lower. That's your finding."

"That's the finding. The comparison set doesn't include high-altitude crops, so anything from up here gets measured against plants it has nothing in common with."

"Right. But look again." He turned the notebook so it faced her, and put one finger on the second list. "Andean potato varietal, routed through the Mediterranean circuit two years ago. D-score: 4.6." He moved his finger down the page. "Same varietal — same *exact* varietal, andigenum, the index number matches — routed through here. D-score: 2.3."

Maialèn looked at the two numbers. She had looked at both of them before, separately, in different columns, on different days, and had not put them next to each other, because she had been looking for a pattern in *varietal*, not in *circuit*.

"Say that again," she said.

"It's not just that high-altitude varietals score low everywhere," Bixente said, slow and careful, the way he laid out a recalibration he wanted to be sure he'd gotten right. "The same varietal scores differently depending on which circuit it travels through. Andean potato, comparable harvest year, comparable testing conditions — through the Mediterranean circuit, 4.6. Through Q'asa Wasi, 2.3. Half."

Maialèn sat very still.

She raised one hand, almost without noticing she was doing it, and traced a shape in the air — two points, a line between them, and then a second line, parallel, lower. She had done this before, she knew, when something resolved into a shape her hands understood before the rest of her caught up. It had been weeks. She hadn't done it once since Donostia.

"It's not taxonomic," she said slowly. "Or — it *is* taxonomic, the comparison-set problem is real, that part doesn't go away. But there's a second layer underneath it. The same plant, the same kind of plant, scores lower specifically when it comes through *here*." She looked up at him. "The bias isn't just about what the plant is. It's about where it's from."

"That's what the numbers say."

"Let me say this out loud." She pulled the notebook back toward herself, found a clean page, and started writing as she spoke — faster now, the words ahead of her hand, the way they came when something had finally found its shape. "One. The comparison-set tables don't include high-altitude data, because the founding survey never collected any. Real, and bad, and it explains part of the gap." She underlined a numeral one. "Two. Even within the comparison sets that *do* exist — comparing andigenum against andigenum, same varietal, no taxonomic mismatch at all — the score is lower when the lot's transfer record shows Q'asa Wasi as point of origin." A two. "Three. That's not a classification problem. A classification problem would affect the varietal everywhere it appears. This affects the varietal *here* more than *elsewhere*."

She set the pen down.

"Three is not a coincidence," she said, quieter now. "Three is a pattern that hasn't finished yet. And I think — " She stopped, looking for the word, the way she always did when the right one was close but not yet in her hand. *Estrecho.* Narrow. Not wrong, but not quite it either — too small a word for what she was looking at, which wasn't a narrow methodology so much as one built by people standing in one place, looking out, never noticing how much of the view their own shadow covered. "Not narrow," she said aloud, half to herself. "Or — narrow, but not the way I mean it at first. The methodology isn't *too tidy* anymore — that was the right formula on the wrong plant. This is too *narrow*. A room too narrow, building something for everyone, with nobody in it from anywhere like here. The dataset was too narrow. The working group was too narrow. And the harm it produces is narrow too — it doesn't fall evenly. It falls on us."

Bixente didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "That's the second thing I came up here to find out, and I've been here two hours."

"You found the half I couldn't see because I was too far inside it." She looked at him properly for the first time since he'd sat down — tired from the road, patient, exactly as he always was. "Eight years, and you still do that."

"Eight years, and you still do the thing with your hands." He nodded at the notebook. "What does this mean for the roster?"

"It means I know what I'm looking for now." She pulled the GDPC tablet toward her, the eleven-person roster still open from the night before. "I was looking for who *built* this. That's done — I have it, six names, three affiliations, one missing region. What I haven't looked for is who's *maintaining* it. The 2079 framework didn't just appear and sit still for fifteen years. Something has to currently hold it — review it, when it's reviewed at all, decide whether it needs changing." She scrolled, searching now with a different question in mind. "A standing body. A subpanel."

It took her eleven minutes to find it — not hidden, just filed under an administrative heading she'd skipped twice as boilerplate: *Comparison-Set Methodology Subpanel, GDPC Technical Council, standing body, est. 2080.* Established the year after the framework's adoption. Six current members, names and affiliations, dates of appointment ranging from three to eleven years.

She read the six names. The fourth one she recognized — not from the roster of eleven, not exactly, but from its *advisory* subcommittee, the five names she hadn't copied down because they'd only signed off, not drafted. One of them — a methodology liaison from the same certification-advisory body that had supplied one of the original six — now sat on the Subpanel that maintained what that body had helped build.

"There," she said, and tapped the name. "One of the original eleven — advisory side, I almost didn't write her down — has a current seat on the Subpanel that oversees the D-score methodology today. Fourteen years, one body or the other."

"Continuity," Bixente said.

"Continuity. On its own, almost nothing — someone has to hold institutional memory. But it's a thread. It connects the design history to the body that's running things now. And now that I have that body — "

She didn't finish the sentence. She was already searching.

---

It took the rest of the morning, and most of it was simply patient, unglamorous cross-referencing — the kind of work Maialèn had done for eighteen years and had never once found boring, because somewhere underneath the tedium was always either nothing or everything, and you didn't know which until you'd checked.

The Subpanel had six members. Public record: names, current institutional affiliations, appointment dates, a one-paragraph biography for each — the kind of disclosure that existed because the GDPC's charter required it, and that almost nobody, Maialèn suspected, had ever actually read end to end.

She read all six, twice. Then she made a list — the same kind she'd made the night before, names on one side, affiliations on the other — and looked at what she'd built.

Four of the six current Subpanel members had professional or institutional relationships with cooperatives or advisory bodies whose primary work was commercial-crop development. Not all the same cooperatives from 2079 — some had merged, restructured, been absorbed into larger bioregional consortia since — but the *kind* of relationship was the same. Three sat on advisory boards for commercial seed-development programs. One had, until two years ago, held a paid consulting role with a certification-readiness body — the same kind of organization, though not the same one, that had supplied a member to the original working group.

"Six people," she said, mostly to herself, mostly because saying it was how she'd know if it held together. "Four of them sit, in some capacity, inside the part of the network that grows commercial crops at scale — the kind of crops the original comparison tables were built around. The other two are the methodology specialists, the ones who actually run the numbers." She looked up. "I want to be careful here, because this is *not* a story about four biased people, and it's not what I'm going to write down. I am not accusing anyone of anything."

"Then what is it a story about?" Bixente asked, though she suspected he already knew, and was asking so she'd say it.

"Who's in the room." She tapped the list. "If most of the people maintaining a methodology have spent their careers inside commercial-crop development — not because anyone chose them *for* that, but because that's where the experienced people in this field mostly are — the room doesn't need anyone to be biased. It just keeps staffing itself from whoever's already nearby, and produces a methodology that fits the agriculture most of the room knows and fits the rest of it badly." She set the pen down. "Show any one of these six the circuit comparison — the 2.3 against the 4.6 — and I think they'd be horrified. I don't think a single one of them designed this on purpose, or has ever seen it laid out this way, because nobody's ever put the circuit data next to the Subpanel roster before. They're not even in the same kind of office."

"So what do you do with it?"

"What I always do." She closed the notebook, gently, the way she closed things she meant to open again soon. "Write it down. Plainly. What the dataset was built from, who built the tables, who sits on the Subpanel now, and what the score distribution does, geographically, to one cooperative's lots. No name goes in a sentence with the word *bias*. The structure goes on the page, and the structure is the finding." She looked at him. "An incentive structure made visible. That's all this is."

One of the four names — an unfamiliar surname, an affiliation with a regional seed-development consortium in the Mediterranean basin — caught at something in the back of Maialèn's memory, the faint, unplaceable tug of a name heard once, secondhand, in a context she couldn't reconstruct. "Does this mean anything to you?"

Bixente leaned over, read it, frowned slightly. "Maybe. A colleague of a colleague — left a consortium post a few years back, around the time of that business in New Accra. I never got the details." He shrugged. "The certification world is small. Smaller than people think."

"Probably nothing," Maialèn agreed, and wrote it down anyway, because writing things down was free and regretting not having written them down wasn't. She turned the page, and didn't return to it.

---

Inés found them at midday, still at the records table, the notebook now thick with cross-references and a second tablet open beside the first. This one displayed the GDPC's public schedule of Technical Council sessions, checked on the theory that a body which hadn't reviewed a methodology in eleven years might at least be meeting about something else in the meantime.

"You've been at this since before I was awake," Inés said, setting down a tray — bread, cheese, the thick soup that seemed to appear at this table whenever anyone had clearly forgotten to eat. She nodded to Bixente — not a stranger's nod; they'd met before, on some earlier circuit. "Welcome back."

"Good to be back." Bixente accepted a piece of bread with the particular gravity he brought to all food. "She found something while I was away. I found something else while she explained it to me. Between us, we have most of an answer."

"Most of," Inés repeated, and there was something almost like amusement in it.

There was a Technical Council session listed for next month — Maialèn glanced at it, not dwelling, the way you'd note a date on a calendar without yet knowing what it meant for you, and set the tablet aside. It wasn't the thing in front of her. The thing in front of her was the notebook, closed now, and the two reveals it held, and what she wanted to ask the woman standing across the table.

"Inés." She said the name carefully, the way she said things she'd been turning over before saying them aloud. "I want to ask you something, and I want you to take whatever time you need before you answer, because it isn't a small thing."

Inés set down the tray and waited.

"The methodology that scores Q'asa Wasi's lots — the comparison-set framework, the D-score system, all of it — was built fifteen years ago by people who never collected data from anywhere like here, and is currently maintained by a body where most of the members come from a part of the network that's never had a reason to notice the gap. The gap is real — fifteen lots, nine years, your own ledgers against the official scores. And it's *geographic* — the same varietal, scored differently depending on which circuit it travels through. There's a vote coming, in some number of weeks I haven't confirmed yet, that I think would make this methodology permanent if nobody objects first." She took a breath. "I don't think I can fix this from inside an audit assignment. It needs to be challenged formally — by someone with standing to challenge a methodology, not just report on a lot. I think that's you. The Council. Q'asa Wasi."

"You're asking if we'll co-file."

"I'm asking if you'd be willing to. Formally. With your name — the cooperative's name — on the record, alongside whatever I can put together. Not because I can't do this alone, but because I think it matters, for what this actually is, that it isn't only an outside auditor saying it. It should be the people who've been saying it for eleven years, saying it again, with someone standing beside them this time instead of in front of them."

Inés didn't answer immediately. She looked at Maialèn for a long moment — the same calibrated look from the receiving yard, the nursery terraces, the records table four nights ago, except there was nothing left in it to calibrate. Then she looked past her, toward the window, the way she so often did, as though the terraces themselves were part of any conversation that mattered.

"Come outside," she said. "Both of you."

---

The Watching Wall held the cold longer than the rest of the terraces, even at midday — a long stone edge where the wind came up the valley unbroken, carrying the mineral smell of high air and, faintly, the green of the nursery terraces below. From here the pass road was visible for most of its length, switchback after switchback dropping toward the valley floor, empty now, the morning's supply truck long since arrived and unloaded.

Maialèn had stood here twice before — once on her first evening, the way any visitor would; once four nights ago, needing somewhere to stand that wasn't a room. Bixente came to stand beside her now without being asked, looking down the pass road with the patient attention of a man who'd spent a career watching roads for things that were coming.

Below them, the terraces stepped away in their long green curves — working land, centuries old, doing exactly what it had always done, the kind of beauty that didn't announce itself because it had never needed to. Inés came to stand on Maialèn's other side. For a while none of them spoke. The wind moved through the irrigation channels below — the small constant sound Maialèn had stopped hearing, and then, this week, started hearing again.

"I need to be honest with you about what this means," Inés said finally. "A formal challenge — co-filed, with the cooperative's name on it — is not small for us either. It means the council votes on it. It means every terrace-holding family has an opinion, and most of them will be right to. It means, if it goes badly, that Q'asa Wasi is the cooperative that picked a fight with the GDPC and lost — and some of the people who'd live with that aren't in this conversation and didn't ask to be."

"I know," Maialèn said. "I wouldn't ask if I thought it was small."

"I know you wouldn't." Inés was quiet a moment longer, then turned, so she faced both of them rather than the view. "I'm going to tell you something, and then I'm going to bring it to the council, because that's how this works here and I won't pretend otherwise."

"All right."

"We have been waiting for someone to ask that question for eleven years."

She said it the way she said most things — without weight, without performance, the way Doña Encarnación had told Maialèn what the plant did. But Maialèn felt it land anyway — eleven years of comments filed and logged and not incorporated, of a council that had kept its own ledgers meticulously, not because anyone outside was watching but because someone, eventually, might ask. And now someone had.

"I'll bring it to the council tonight," Inés said. "I think I know what they'll say. But it isn't mine to say for them, and I won't pretend that either."

"Thank you," Maialèn said, and found that the word, ordinary as it was, didn't feel like enough, and also that there wasn't a better one, and that this — for once — was fine.

The three of them stood at the wall a while longer, looking down the empty pass road, the wind moving steadily up from the valley the way it always did, carrying nothing yet, but carrying, Maialèn thought, the particular quality of air before something arrives.
