# The Old Ledgers

The records room was cold when Maialèn arrived, the climate system still warming itself from the night. Inés was already there — not at the cabinet, but standing by the window with a mug of something steaming, looking out at the terraces.

"Good morning," Maialèn said. She set her satchel on the table, the way she had every morning this week, and waited for the cabinet to come open.

It didn't.

"We're not doing the ledgers this morning," Inés said. She turned from the window. "I want you on the Long Terraces today. Working, not watching."

Maialèn had been ready for several things. Refusal. A delay dressed as politeness. Even, faintly, the possibility that Inés had changed her mind overnight and decided the records were not for outside eyes after all. She had not been ready for this, and it took her a moment to find her footing.

"Working," she repeated.

"There's tuber harvest on the lower platforms. We're short two pairs of hands — one of the families has someone with a fever, nothing serious, but it puts us behind." Inés set her mug down on the windowsill. "You'll go with the crew. You'll do what they do."

"I'm an auditor," Maialèn said, and heard, as she said it, how that sounded — not a refusal, just an honest description of her qualifications, which did not include digging potatoes.

"I know what you are." Inés picked the mug back up. "That's rather the point."

Maialèn looked at the cabinet in the corner — closed, ordinary, a plain wooden cabinet with a small brass lock that had probably not been tested in years. Then she looked back at Inés, who was watching her with the same calibrated patience she'd shown since the receiving yard, four days ago now. Sizing her up. Still.

"Those records were never meant for outside eyes," Inés said. "Not because there's anything in them anyone's ashamed of. Because they're ours — the way a family keeps a ledger of who owes what to whom, and doesn't put it on the wall." She nodded toward the window, the terraces beyond it. "Before I open that cabinet for you, I want to know what kind of person is going to read what's inside it. Not what kind of auditor. What kind of person."

"And the terraces tell you that."

"They tell me something." Inés almost smiled. "Wear something you don't mind getting dirty."

Maialèn thought about it for exactly as long as it took to recognize that there was nothing to think about. "I have a coat I don't mind getting dirty," she said. "Where do I go?"

---

The Long Terraces ran along the southern face of the mountain below the Vault House, stone-walled platforms stepping down toward the valley in long curves. They were wide enough to work, narrow enough that the whole system read, from above, like contour lines made solid. Maialèn had seen them from the Watching Wall on her first day, a green-and-brown abstraction in the middle distance. Walking down into them was different. Each platform was its own small country, with its own soil, its own angle to the sun, its own particular cold.

The crew was already at work when she arrived — six people, two of them children old enough to carry but not yet old enough for the digging fork. They moved along the rows with the unhurried steadiness of people who'd been at it since before sunrise and would be for hours yet. A woman with a weathered face and a red wool cap looked up as Maialèn picked her way down the last terrace wall. She lost her footing slightly on the loose stone and recovered with more arm-waving than the situation strictly required.

"You're the one from the coast," the woman said. It wasn't a question.

"Maialèn." She held out a hand, then realized the woman's hands were both full of soil and tubers, and converted the gesture into something more like a wave. "Inés sent me. I'm told I'm meant to dig."

"Inés sent you to dig," the woman repeated, with the particular flatness of someone filing information for later amusement. "All right. Come here."

Her name was Rosalía, and she showed Maialèn the work in four sentences, then stopped explaining — that was simply how things were taught here. You watched, you tried, you got it wrong, and someone corrected you without making it a lesson. The tubers grew in mounded rows, hilled up through the season against the cold. Harvesting meant the fork at an angle, loosening the mound without spearing what was underneath, then lifting the tubers out by hand. You sorted as you went — by size, by skin, by marks that told an experienced eye whether something belonged in storage, the kitchen, or back in the ground as seed.

Maialèn speared three tubers in her first ten minutes.

"You're digging like the ground owes you something," Rosalía said, not unkindly, and showed her again — the angle of the fork, shallower than felt natural, the wrist doing more of the work than the arm. Maialèn tried again. She speared a fourth.

"In my defense," she said, working the fork free, "I have spent eighteen years making sure other people's paperwork is in order. No one has ever asked me to be careful with a potato."

"Now someone has."

It took the better part of an hour before her hands found something like a rhythm — not competence, but a motion that didn't actively endanger the harvest. The cold was sharper here than at the Vault House, the morning air thin enough to make her aware of her own breathing. Then the sun cleared the eastern ridge and the temperature began its long climb the other way. By midmorning she had stripped off her coat, sweating in the thin air while the cold still sat in the soil under her knees.

*This is the swing they told me about,* she thought. *Not weather. A schedule.*

The crew talked while they worked — not to her, mostly, though not excluding her either. A disagreement about whether the upper platform needed more compost before next planting, conducted with the easy contempt of people who'd had variations of the same argument for years. A child's complaint about being sent for water, answered without much sympathy. Quechua and Spanish moved through it interchangeably, sometimes within the same sentence, and Maialèn found she could follow most of it — not every word, but the shape of it.

"This one," Rosalía said, holding up a tuber for Maialèn's inspection — purple-skinned, knobbed, nothing like the smooth ovals Maialèn associated with the word *potato*. "*Lumtu*. You won't find this on a market shelf in your country. Too ugly, they'd say. Doesn't ship."

"What does it do well?"

"Cold." Rosalía set it in the seed-stock basket, not the kitchen one. "Frost can take the leaves and this one comes back. Most don't." She glanced at Maialèn's basket — a meager handful compared to the others' — and something in her face suggested she was choosing not to comment on it. "You ask the right questions, for someone who can't tell a seed tuber from a dinner one."

"That's most of my job," Maialèn said. "Knowing what to ask. The digging part is new."

"It shows."

By midday the platform had a different texture — turned soil, sorted baskets, a tiredness in Maialèn's lower back and thighs that her usual long days at a records table never produced. She sat on the terrace wall with the crew for the midday meal, a thick soup brought down from the Vault House in a covered pot. She ate without much conversation, hungrier than she'd expected, and the soup was very good.

"You're not bad," Rosalía said eventually, from beside her, "for someone who started this morning thinking a potato was an administrative category."

"I never said that."

"You didn't have to." Rosalía took a long drink from a tin cup, not looking at her. "You held the first one like it was a folder."

Maialèn laughed — a real laugh, the kind she usually saved for evenings with Bixente after a long audit day. "That's fair," she said. "That's completely fair."

---

In the afternoon they moved to the next platform up, where an older woman was already working alone, methodical, unhurried, in a section set slightly apart from the main harvest. Rosalía nodded toward her as they climbed the terrace wall.

"That's Doña Encarnación," she said. "Her line. Don't dig there unless she shows you how — she'll tell you, and she won't be gentle about it."

Doña Encarnación looked up as they approached — a small woman, perhaps seventy, her hands deep brown and creased. She moved through the mounded rows with an economy of motion that made Maialèn's morning efforts look even more chaotic in retrospect. She watched Maialèn come down the last steps of terrace wall with an expression that was assessing, faintly amused, and entirely unbothered.

"The coast woman," she said. "Rosalía said you spear them."

"I've improved," Maialèn said. "Marginally."

"Marginally." Doña Encarnación gestured at the row beside her own — a section already half-cleared, tidy, the soil turned in even waves. "Sit there. Watch first. Then try."

Maialèn sat. She watched. Doña Encarnación's hands moved into the soil and came back with three, four, five tubers at a time, sorted before they'd even left the ground. A flick of the wrist separated seed stock from kitchen stock almost before Maialèn's eye could register the difference.

"Forty years," Rosalía said, from behind them, with the tone of someone repeating something often said and still true. "This line. Same plant, same terrace, forty years."

"Forty-one," Doña Encarnación said, without looking up. "People round down because it's a rounder number. I don't."

Maialèn tried the row beside her. She speared one — fewer than the morning, but one — and Doña Encarnación made a small sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Slower," the old woman said. "You're not digging up something that's hiding from you. It's not hiding. It's just there."

Maialèn slowed down. The next tuber came up whole, and the one after that. By the fourth she had something that felt — not like skill, not yet, but like the absence of active harm, which after a morning of mild agricultural violence felt like an accomplishment worth noting.

"Better," Doña Encarnación said.

They worked in something close to silence for a while, the old woman's pace steady and unhurried, Maialèn's slower but no longer disastrous. The sun was past its peak now, the light flattening toward afternoon. Below them on the terraces the irrigation channels ran with the small constant sound Maialèn had stopped consciously hearing days ago — and now, sitting still enough to notice it again, found she'd missed.

"You're the one asking about the scores," Doña Encarnación said. Not a question, the same way Rosalía's first words hadn't been a question. News here moved the way Inés had told her it did — fast, and through channels nobody bothered to name.

"I am."

"The number that says what this plant is worth." The old woman turned over a tuber in her hand, examined it, set it in the seed basket without looking at where her hand was going. "I've heard about that number. Inés explained it to the council, two years back, when one of the younger ones asked why our lots always came back rated low." She set down the next tuber, considering it longer than the last. "I told her — and I'll tell you, since you're the one asking — the paper says what the paper was given. I know what this plant does."

She said it the way she'd said everything else — without weight, without performance, the way you'd state which direction was downhill. Then she went back to the row, and the moment closed over itself the way moments did out here, folded back into the rhythm of the work. Maialèn found herself turning over a tuber of her own without quite registering that she'd stopped to listen.

She wrote nothing down. There was nowhere to write it, and her hands were full of soil, and in any case it didn't feel, in the moment, like the kind of thing that needed writing down. It felt like something Doña Encarnación had said many times before, to people who had mostly nodded and moved on.

It was only later — much later, back at the Vault House, scrubbing soil from under her fingernails with the thoroughness of someone delaying something else — that the sentence came back to her, whole. It sat there in a way that made her stop, hands still under the running water.

*The paper says what the paper was given.*

She dried her hands and went to find the satchel notebook, and wrote it down exactly as Doña Encarnación had said it, word for word, before she could lose the shape of it.

---

The records room in the afternoon had the same flat light it always had — the narrow window letting in a single shaft that moved, almost imperceptibly, across the stone floor as the day went on. Inés was waiting when Maialèn came in, freshly washed, her terrace clothes exchanged for the same practical layers she'd arrived in four days ago, though she suspected she still smelled faintly of turned earth.

"How was the harvest?" Inés asked.

"Educational." Maialèn sat at the table. "I speared seven tubers over the course of the day. Doña Encarnación informed me this represented an improvement."

"It does." Something in Inés's posture had changed — not dramatically, but Maialèn, who had spent four days reading the smaller signals of this woman's attention, registered it. A kind of settling. A decision arrived at and no longer in motion.

Inés crossed to the cabinet in the corner. The brass lock, when she touched it, turned without resistance — not because it was broken, Maialèn understood, but because it had simply never needed to resist anyone the cooperative had decided to let in.

"These are ours," Inés said, opening the cabinet doors. "Before the Trust. Before the Accord had a name for any of this. Some of these books are older than the Covenant itself."

Inside, the shelves held record books — dozens of them, bound in cloth and board, varying in size and condition. They were organized by year along the spine, in handwriting that shifted, subtly, every decade or so as one careful hand gave way to another. Maialèn recognized the oldest ones by their wear before she could read their dates: softened corners, repaired spines, the patina of books that had been opened often and put away carefully.

"Where do I start?" she asked.

"Wherever you like." Inés pulled three volumes from the shelf — recent ones, relatively, their covers still mostly intact — and set them on the table. "These cover the last fifteen years. The varietal index is in the front of each one, by year." She paused, and for the first time since Maialèn had arrived, something like warmth showed plainly on her face. "Take your time. I'll bring tea."

---

The ledgers were extraordinary, and Maialèn understood within the first twenty minutes that the word wasn't an exaggeration, even though she'd spent eighteen years trying not to use words like that about paperwork.

Each entry was a single line, sometimes two: lot identifier, varietal, planting date, germination at fourteen days and again at twenty-eight, viability at harvest, notes on weather and soil where relevant. The handwriting changed across the decades — careful and cramped in the earliest volumes, giving way in later years to a rounder hand Maialèn guessed belonged to whoever currently kept the records, possibly Inés herself. But the *format* never changed. Whoever had started this, sometime before the 1990s, had built something the cooperative simply kept doing, generation after generation, without needing to be told why.

She started with the *andigenum* lines — the same varietal as Lot 441 — and began cross-referencing.

Lot identifiers in the old ledgers didn't match the Accord's current coding, but Q'asa Wasi's parallel system, the one Inés had mentioned on the terraces days ago, bridged the gap. Maialèn worked methodically: find a ledger entry, locate its modern lot number in the cross-reference index at the back of each volume, then check that lot number against the D-score data she already had from the network database.

Germination at fourteen days: 94%. Germination at twenty-eight days: 91%. Viability at harvest, recorded the following autumn: 89%.

She found the corresponding lot in her D-score records. D-score: 2.3.

She checked the next entry. Germination at fourteen days: 96%. Twenty-eight days: 93%. Viability: 91%. D-score: 2.1.

She kept going. An hour passed, then most of another. The pattern held with a consistency that, in any other context, she'd have found suspicious — except here the consistency ran the opposite direction from the kind that worried her. These weren't four identical numbers traveling untouched across an ocean. These were decades of real, varying, internally consistent measurements, and every one of them described a plant that performed.

She pulled the tea Inés had brought toward her without looking at it and kept writing.

By late afternoon she had fifteen cross-referenced lots in the satchel notebook, in two columns: the cooperative's own recorded outcomes on one side, the Accord's official D-score on the other. Fifteen lots, spanning nine years. Germination rates in the low-to-mid nineties, viability above eighty-five percent, year after year, varietal after varietal: *andigenum*, the high-altitude quinoa lines, two older grain varietals she didn't recognize by name. Their entries were as clean and consistent as everything else.

And on the other side of the page, a column of numbers between 2.0 and 3.5.

---

"Let me say this out loud," Maialèn said, to the room, the way she had four nights ago over a different column of numbers.

Inés, who had been working quietly at the far end of the table on something of her own — Maialèn hadn't asked what — looked up, but didn't speak.

"Fifteen lots. Nine years. Germination rates in the low nineties, every time. Viability at harvest above eighty-five percent, every time." Maialèn tapped the column on the left. "These aren't projections. These are records of what actually happened — what came up, what survived, what made it to harvest at thirty-eight hundred meters, year after year. In conditions that would kill most commercial varietals outright." She tapped the column on the right. "And the D-score for every one of these lots says: limited genetic value. Adequately covered elsewhere. Two-point-one, two-point-three, two-point-eight."

She sat back.

"The methodology isn't lying," she said, slower now, working it out as she said it. "I keep wanting it to be lying, because a lie is simple — someone changed a number, someone benefits, you find them and it's over. This isn't that. Every field on every D-score sheet is filled in correctly. Every comparison is technically valid, if you accept the comparison set." She stopped.

*Demasiado limpio,* she thought. *Too clean.*

No — not clean. That wasn't the right word, and she sat with the wrongness of it the way she sat with a number that almost balanced. Not clean. *Tidy.* Too tidy — a system built carefully, applied consistently, producing with total internal consistency an answer that was wrong every time it was asked about this particular plant.

"It's not too clean," she said slowly. "It's too *tidy*. The methodology is applying the right formula." She looked at the two columns again. "To the wrong plant. And it's been doing it long enough that fifteen lots of evidence have been sitting in a cabinet nobody outside this room ever cross-referenced against the score."

She looked up at Inés.

"How long have you known this?"

---

Inés set down her pen. For a moment she didn't answer, and Maialèn recognized the pause — not the two-second calibration from the nursery terraces, and not the longer, interior pause before *come back in the morning*. This was different again. Settled. A thing that had been sitting, fully formed, for a long time, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

"Eleven years," Inés said.

Maialèn didn't say anything. She had learned, this week, that Inés's silences carried information, and she wanted to hear the rest of it before she filled the space with her own reaction.

"In 2083, the Genetic Diversity Preservation Council opened a methodology review — the comparison-set framework, the one your D-score sheet uses now. They asked for cooperative input. We submitted comments." Inés's voice was even, unhurried, the same voice she used to describe the morning germination check. "Three of them, over the review period. The first asked that high-altitude varietals be evaluated against high-altitude comparators. The second included data — not as much as what's in that notebook now, but enough. The third was shorter. By the third, we had stopped expecting a different answer and were mostly making sure the position was on record."

"And?"

"And they were received." Inés picked her pen back up, turned it over once, set it down again. "Each one was logged. Acknowledged. Included in the review's public comment archive, which I checked, some years later, out of — I don't know what to call it. Not hope. Diligence, maybe." She looked at Maialèn directly. "The 2079 framework was adopted in 2084 with the comparison-set methodology unchanged. Our comments are in the archive. They were not incorporated."

The room was quiet. Outside, the light through the narrow window had gone the color it went in late afternoon — flat, gold, the particular quality of high-altitude light that Maialèn had stopped being surprised by and never stopped noticing.

"Eleven years," Maialèn said. Not a question. She was turning it over, the way she'd turn over a number that needed a moment before it became real. "You've had this — " she gestured at the ledgers, the cabinet, the whole quiet weight of the room " — for eleven years. And you asked. Three times."

"And your escalation, two days ago, made it four." Inés said this without bitterness — Maialèn listened for it and didn't find it, the way she'd check a measurement twice to be sure of a null result. "I'm not telling you this to make a point about your Trust. I'm telling you because you asked how long, and I think you're the kind of person who'd rather have the honest answer."

Maialèn sat with that.

She had come into this room four days ago believing, as she had believed for eighteen years, that a clean record was the beginning of an answer. Gather enough of it, present it to the right office, and the system would correct itself, because that was what systems were *for*. She still believed that, in some basic structural sense, and didn't know how to stop. But she understood, sitting at this table, that she'd just been handed not a discovery but an *inheritance*. A problem already described, accurately and completely, eleven years ago, by people who'd done everything she'd just spent four days doing — and been answered with silence.

"This isn't new," she said slowly. "I thought I'd found something. I haven't found something. I've found the same thing you found, eleven years after you found it, and I filed the same kind of comment you filed, and it got the same kind of answer."

"Yes," Inés said. Not unkindly. Just accurate.

"That's — " Maialèn stopped. She had been about to reach for a word like *infuriating*, or *unjust*, and found that neither one quite fit what she was actually feeling, which was something quieter and colder, a recalibration rather than an emotion. "That's not a new problem. That's an old problem nobody fixed."

"That's closer to it," Inés said.

Maialèn looked down at the two columns in her notebook — fifteen lots, nine years. Beside them, in her mind now, sat eleven more years: comments filed and logged and not incorporated. An institution that had asked to be heard and hadn't been — twice over, and a third time when she herself had tried.

She thought, briefly, of Bixente — somewhere on the southern circuit, three days from arriving — and wished, with a specificity that surprised her, that he were here to see this. He wouldn't have been surprised. He'd have gone quiet, the way he did, and then said something practical, and she'd have felt better for it.

"The 2083 review," she said instead, to Inés. "The 2079 framework that came out of it — do you know who designed the comparison-set methodology in the first place? Before the review."

"No," Inés said. "We've never had a name. Only the document."

"I'd like to find one." Maialèn closed the notebook, carefully, the way she closed things she intended to open again soon. "Tonight, if I can. I want to know who built this, and when it was last actually looked at by a person — not a review process. A person."

Inés studied her for a moment — the same long, assessing look from the receiving yard, the nursery terraces, the records-room table four nights ago. But it had a different quality now, and Maialèn understood, without either of them saying so, that whatever the terraces had been testing for, she had passed it.

"I'll have someone bring you dinner," Inés said. "You'll want to eat before you start."

---

Maialèn ate. She thanked the woman who brought the tray and made herself finish it, despite not being hungry, because the work went better if she did. Then she sat on the edge of the narrow bed with her tablet propped against her knees and pulled up the Genetic Diversity Preservation Council's public methodology archive.

The 2079 framework documentation was longer than she'd expected — over three hundred pages, most of it procedural boilerplate, definitions, cross-references to other Accord instruments. She skimmed past what she already understood, looking for two things: an authorship record, and a review history.

The authorship record was buried in an appendix — a list of working-group members, eleven names, affiliations, dates of service. She read through it once, then again, more slowly. She didn't recognize any of the names, which didn't surprise her. She'd spent her career on the audit side, not the policy side — the methodology architects were, to her, mostly abstractions who'd written documents she had to apply.

The review history was shorter, and more interesting. The framework had been adopted in 2079. It had undergone one substantive review — the 2083 cycle Inés had described, the one that produced three cooperative comments and no changes. After that: nothing. No subsequent review cycle was scheduled, none had occurred. Eleven years of silence, on both sides — the cooperative's comments unanswered, and the framework itself, after that single review, simply never looked at again.

She read the appendix again. Then she went back to the working-group list and started reading more carefully — not skimming this time, but actually reading each entry, the affiliations, the dates, the small print.

Outside her window, the cold had settled fully over the terraces, and the stars had come out in the dense, precise way they did at this altitude — more of them than she was used to, and sharper. She didn't look up at them. She read the next entry, and the one after that, making notes in the satchel notebook as she went, her handwriting getting smaller as the page filled.

It was past midnight when she realized how late it had gotten. She didn't stop. The tablet's glow was the only light in the room, and she sat with it, working through the list one name at a time, looking for the one that would tell her where to go next.
