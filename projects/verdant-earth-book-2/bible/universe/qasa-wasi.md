# Q'asa Wasi

## Overview
Q'asa Wasi — "the house at the pass," in the Quechua naming convention the high-Andes cooperatives revived after the Collapse — is a cooperative seed bank and germination station perched at just over 3,800 meters in the Cusco bioregion's eastern terrace network. It is one of the smaller nodes on the Atlantic Seed Trust's verification circuit: a working cold-vault, a terrace-seed nursery, and a year-round community of perhaps four hundred people who have kept Andean tuber and grain varietals alive through every collapse their region has weathered, including this latest one.

It is not famous, and it does not want to be. Q'asa Wasi's pride is continuity, not recognition — the same families have minded the same terraces and the same seed lines for so many generations that the cooperative's real archive is not the vault's climate-controlled shelving but the seed-keepers' own memory of which lines have always done what, in which years, on which slope. The vault is where the cooperative writes that memory down so it can travel. This is, on the page, exactly the kind of place whose chain-of-custody record matters more than its size would suggest — and exactly the kind of place an irregularity could pass through quietly, on its way somewhere bigger.

## Geography

### Physical layout
- High terrace country: stepped agricultural platforms cut into the mountainside centuries before the Covenant, restored and re-terraced after resettlement-era erosion damage in the 2050s
- The Vault House: a low, thick-walled stone-and-composite structure built half into the slope — old construction technique, new materials, the cooperative's signature combination
- The Nursery Terraces: south-facing growing platforms immediately below the Vault House, where germination trials run before any lot is certified for transfer
- The Pass Road: the single switchbacked supply route connecting Q'asa Wasi to the valley rail head three hours below — the cooperative's only land link to the wider network
- The Watching Wall: a long stone terrace-edge where residents have, for generations, simply stood to look down the valley — informally, the place where news arrives first, because you can see the supply trucks coming a long way off

### Climate
High-altitude Andean: cold nights even in the warm months, thin clear air, intense unfiltered sun at midday. Frost risk shapes the entire agricultural calendar — the nursery terraces are oriented and walled specifically to manage it. Weather changes fast and is taken seriously; an afternoon can turn from brilliant to dangerous in under an hour.

## Infrastructure

### The cold vault
A single climate-controlled chamber cut into bedrock, holding several thousand seed lots — mostly potato, quinoa, and maize varietals collected from across the eastern Andean corridor, plus a smaller reciprocal collection held in trust for partner cooperatives elsewhere in the global network (this is the detail that puts it on Maialèn's circuit: Q'asa Wasi both sends and receives, and every lot that passes through generates exactly the kind of record she audits).

### The germination nursery
Terraced growing platforms where every incoming and outgoing lot is test-germinated under controlled conditions before certification — the cooperative's own internal check, run independently of (and, by tradition, slightly suspicious of) the vault's paper records. "The seed doesn't lie about whether it grew" is a saying here that predates the Covenant by generations.

### Power and water
Micro-hydro off the terrace irrigation channels, supplemented by solar on the vault roof — small-scale, locally maintained, entirely typical of high-altitude cooperative infrastructure. No connection to a larger grid; Q'asa Wasi has always run on what the mountain gives it, and treats this as a strength, not a limitation.

### The surprise
Visitors who expect a remote outpost to feel improvised are wrong. Q'asa Wasi's recordkeeping is, if anything, more meticulous than larger, better-funded nodes — not because of outside oversight, but because the cooperative has spent four hundred years learning that a wrong note about a seed line costs you a harvest, sometimes a generation. Maialèn would recognize this instantly: it is her own faith in the record, independently arrived at, by people who never needed the Atlantic Seed Trust to teach it to them.

---

## Governance
Q'asa Wasi is run by an elected seed-keepers' council — five members, staggered terms, drawn from the families with the longest continuous terrace-holding. The council answers, loosely, to the regional Cusco bioregional cooperative assembly, and — for anything that crosses a border — to the Atlantic Seed Trust's verification framework. In practice, the council guards its independence carefully: it has been keeping these seed lines since long before there was a Trust to certify them, and it cooperates with the wider network as a matter of considered choice, not default deference.

## Sensory profile

### Sight
Stone terraces stepping down a mountainside in long curved lines, green against grey rock. The Vault House's thick walls catch the low-angle mountain light at dawn and dusk. Snow on the high peaks beyond, even in the warm season. The Watching Wall, with its long view down the pass road — usually empty, occasionally crowded when something is expected.

### Sound
Wind, almost constantly — the high terraces are never quite still. Water moving through the irrigation channels in a steady, ordered rhythm. Quechua and Spanish mixed in daily speech, often in the same sentence. The vault itself is close to silent — the climate system runs low and slow, by design, so as not to disturb the cold.

### Smell
Thin, cold, mineral air at altitude — and then, stepping into the nursery terraces, the sudden warm green smell of growing things in managed shelter, a contrast sharp enough to notice every time. The vault smells faintly of stone, dust, and the dry, papery scent of stored seed — Maialèn would know this smell from a hundred other vaults, and would also know, instantly, that this one is exceptionally well kept.

### Temperature
Cold mornings, intense midday sun, cold again by early evening — the daily swing is the defining physical fact of life here, and everything from clothing to building design to planting schedules is built around it.

## Cultural character

### Identity
Q'asa Wasi thinks of itself, correctly, as a keeper rather than an innovator — its claim to importance is that it has simply never stopped doing the job, through resettlement, through the worst Collapse years, through every disruption that took down larger and better-resourced operations elsewhere. This is a quiet, durable kind of pride, closer to Maialèn's grandmother's ledgers than to New Accra's self-conscious civic identity. The cooperative does not need outsiders to validate what it has kept; it would simply like them to get the paperwork right when they take some of it away.

### The cooperative texture
Decisions move through the council, but the real social fabric is the terrace-holding families — overlapping, intermarried, multi-generational, with long memories about who has been careless with a seed line and who has not. An auditor who comes in respectful of that memory, rather than treating it as something to be replaced by better paperwork, will find doors open quickly. One who doesn't, won't.

### The Before Time
Older residents remember the resettlement years directly — when erosion and instability drove people off the higher terraces for the better part of a decade, and the question of whether the old seed lines would survive in lowland storage was, for a while, genuinely open. That the lines came back up the mountain at all, and that Q'asa Wasi is here to receive auditors from across an ocean today, is treated locally less as a miracle than as a debt — owed to the people who carried the seed down and back up again, and which the cooperative intends to keep paying by doing the job right.

## Key locations in the story

### The Vault House records room
A small, plain stone-walled office just inside the vault entrance, where the cooperative keeps both its official transfer ledgers and — in a locked cabinet that is, by long informal custom, never locked when the right person asks — the older handwritten record books that predate the network's standard forms. The kind of room where a careful auditor finds either nothing or everything, depending on what she knows to ask for.

### The Nursery Terraces, dawn
Where the cooperative's germination trials are checked each morning before the day's other work begins — cold, quiet, the kind of grounding, hands-in-the-soil scene that belongs to this book the way Station Seven belonged to Book 1: a place to stand and actually look at the thing the whole investigation is, underneath everything, about protecting.

### The Watching Wall
Where news arrives first, and where a conversation that needs to happen without an audience naturally happens — because everyone knows that if you stand here, you can see anyone coming long before they arrive.

## Notes for writer
- Q'asa Wasi should feel like a place that has been doing this work longer and more carefully than the institutions auditing it — not a backwater being inspected, but a keeper being checked on, with all the quiet dignity that implies
- Its beauty is the terraces themselves: working agricultural land, centuries-old, still feeding the same kind of seed lines it always has — visible proof of what the whole network exists to protect
- This is exactly the kind of small, careful, unglamorous node where a "polite lie" could pass through almost unnoticed — not because Q'asa Wasi is careless, but because its very reliability is what makes it a clean place to launder a record through
- Sensory contrast (thin cold air outside, warm green shelter in the nursery) is the location's signature — use it the way New Accra used water-and-green
