# Maialèn Etxeberria

## Identity
- Full name: Maialèn Etxeberria Goikoetxea
- Nickname(s): "Mai" to colleagues who've earned it; she introduces herself with the full name and lets people shorten it once they've worked with her, not before
- Age: 38
- Role in story: Protagonist

---

## Appearance
- Physical description: Tall, rangy, the kind of build that comes from years of fieldwork in cold-storage facilities and long days walking grain terraces. Silver already coming in at her temples and she has stopped dyeing it — "I don't have time to argue with my own hair." Calloused hands, permanently slightly chapped from cold-vault work.
- Distinctive features: Carries a battered canvas satchel everywhere that is, by now, more logbook than bag — she will not let anyone else touch it. Talks with her hands when she's working through a problem, sketching shapes in the air as if the data had geometry.
- Typical clothing: Insulated fieldwork layers suited to vault and terrace work — practical, well-worn, chosen for warmth and pockets over appearance; switches to plain pressed shirts for review hearings, which she treats the way Yara treats meetings (a necessary discomfort)

---

## Personality

### Core traits (permanent)
- **Faith in the record**: Maialèn believes a clean chain-of-custody record is the closest thing humans have built to an honest witness — paper, properly kept, doesn't have motives. She has organized her entire professional life around this belief.
- **Compulsive enumeration**: She counts things, names things, narrates her own process out loud — not for an audience, but because saying a sequence aloud is how she catches the gap in it. Colleagues have learned that when she starts listing, she's already found something.
- **Generous suspicion**: Unlike an inspector who assumes the worst of people, Maialèn assumes the best of *individuals* and the worst of *processes* — her instinct is "no one person did this on purpose; some sequence of reasonable choices made it possible." This generosity is sincere, and it is also, this book will show her, sometimes a blind spot.

### Strengths
- Can read a chain-of-custody record the way a sommelier reads a vintage — she knows which irregularities are normal wear and which are wrong in a way that matters
- Cross-bioregional fluency: she has worked in enough different cooperative hubs to know that "this is how we've always done it" is not the same claim everywhere, and to spot when someone is leaning on that phrase to avoid a question
- Builds trust with site staff quickly — she audits *with* people, not *at* them, and most of them come to like having her in the building
- Genuinely excellent under the specific pressure of a deadline she understands the shape of (a viability window, a planting cutoff) — she gets calmer, not more frantic, as the clock narrows

### Flaws
- Slow to suspect a person directly, even with evidence in front of her — she will spend two extra days looking for an innocent systemic explanation before she'll let herself conclude someone chose this
- Talks through problems out loud, which means she sometimes hands a half-formed suspicion to exactly the wrong listener before she's sure of it
- Impatient with anyone who treats the record as a formality rather than a trust — this is the one place her famous patience runs out

### Fears
- That she has spent eighteen years trusting a system that was built to be trusted *because* that makes it easier to falsify cleanly — that her faith in the record is precisely what someone planned on
- Being the auditor who signed off on the lot that failed — not abstractly, but concretely: a terrace somewhere, a planting season, a cooperative that trusted her stamp and lost a year because of it

### Desires
- To be able to stand behind every signature she has ever put on a certification document — this is closer to her idea of a clean conscience than anything else in her life
- To understand *how* a falsified record was made to look clean, not just *that* it was — she cannot let a forged pattern go unexplained any more than Yara can leave an anomaly unread

## Background
Maialèn grew up in the Getaria Cooperative on the Basque coast, in a family that had been seed-keepers for five generations before the Cascade Collapse and simply kept being seed-keepers after — her grandmother's hand-annotated ledgers of bean and maize varietals, kept through the worst resettlement years "because someone has to remember what grew here," are part of the regional cooperative archive now, and Maialèn has read every page of them more than once.

She trained in agricultural genetics and chain-of-custody science at the Bordeaux-Bilbao Cooperative Institute, then spent six years doing site-level viability testing before moving into cross-bioregional audit work — the job that now takes her, several months of each year, away from the Bay of Biscay terraces she calls home. She is based at the Atlantic Seed Trust's northern verification office in Donostia, but her actual working life is a circuit: cold-vault inspections, lot-transfer reconciliations, and germination-rate spot checks at cooperative seed banks from the Mediterranean basin to the Sahel corridor to the high Andes — wherever the global network's transfer routes need an independent eye.

She is unmarried, has no children, and describes this without apology or performance — "the seed banks are not going anywhere and neither, it turns out, am I." She keeps a terrace plot back home that her sister tends in her absence and writes to her sister every week, mostly about what's growing.

## Relationships
- **Bixente Agirre** (her usual site liaison at the Donostia vault, a quiet, methodical cold-storage technician): The closest thing she has to a Del — not a co-investigator by training, but someone whose plain competence she trusts completely, and who supplies the local, on-the-ground texture her audits depend on. He notices what she's too far inside the records to see; she notices what he's too close to the routine to question.
- **The Atlantic Seed Trust**: Her employer — a cooperative verification body, structurally similar to (but independent from) the Accord Technical Council. She respects its mission without illusions about its caution; like Yara and the Water Authority, she has watched it choose its own reputation over an uncomfortable finding before, and filed the lesson away rather than let it curdle into cynicism.
- **The seed network itself**: Not a person, but — like Yara's biofilter network — something she relates to as if it were one. She thinks of the global cooperative seed routes the way her grandmother thought of a single terrace: a thing with a memory, that rewards attention and punishes neglect.
- **Possible connective tissue (Book 1 institutional thread)**: Maialèn's audit work runs through the same certification-adjacent ecosystem that produced Altera Technical Services — cooperative verification bodies, advisory panels, and the contractor firms that service them are a small enough world that a name from New Accra's frozen certification review could plausibly cross her desk as a reference, a former colleague, or a name on a panel roster. This is *available*, not assumed; nothing in her file requires it, but if her audit trail leads toward a familiar name, it would not strain credibility.

## Speech patterns

### Voice characteristics
- Tone: Warm, precise, faintly wry — she sounds like someone explaining something she finds genuinely interesting, even when the subject is grim
- Register: Professional Spanish-inflected English with occasional Basque turns of phrase she translates for herself mid-sentence, half out of habit and half for the listener's benefit
- Pace: Quick when she's enumerating, unhurried when she's deciding — the two speeds are both deliberate

### Verbal tics & expressions
- Counts out loud without noticing she's doing it: "That's three. Three is not a coincidence, three is a pattern that hasn't finished yet."
- Translates herself mid-thought: "It's — how do we say — it's not wrong, exactly. It's *too tidy*. Demasiado limpio. Too clean to be the truth and not clean enough to be a lie someone's proud of."
- Uses "let me say it out loud" as a signal that she's about to think in front of someone — colleagues have learned not to interrupt
- Calls a falsified or suspect record "a polite lie" — her driest possible register

### Dialogue examples
> "Lot four-forty-one left Donostia with a ninety-one percent viability certificate. It arrived at Q'asa Wasi registered at ninety-one percent. Eleven months apart, two different testing stations, two different technicians who have never met — and the number didn't move. Not even by the rounding. You understand why that frightens me more than a number that's wrong?"

> "I am not accusing the man. I want to be very clear that I am not accusing the man. I am saying that the form he filled in was filled in correctly for a transfer that, according to everything else in this building, did not happen the way the form says it happened. Someone is telling the truth and someone is telling a polite lie, and I would like, please, to find out which is which before I decide who."

> Bixente: "You think someone did this on purpose?"
> Maialèn, after a long pause: "I think — eighteen years, I have never once had to think it. That is not the same as never. Let me say it out loud and see how it sounds: I think someone built a process that would do this *for* them, so that no one would ever have to."

## Behavior patterns

### Under stress
- Talks more, not less — narrates her own reasoning aloud as a way of keeping it honest, even when alone
- Returns to first principles: re-checks the oldest, most boring records first, on the theory that a lie has to start somewhere quiet
- Writes everything down twice — once in the official log, once in her own satchel notebook, in her own shorthand, "for when the official version disagrees with what I actually saw"

### In conflict
- Stays scrupulously, almost theatrically fair to the person in front of her — and lets that fairness do the work of pressure
- Will restate someone's own claim back to them, plainly, and then simply wait
- Does not raise her voice; when she's truly angry, she gets quieter and more precise, and people who know her find that far more alarming than shouting

### When happy
- Becomes almost aggressively hospitable — feeds people, tells long stories about her grandmother's ledgers, narrates the provenance of whatever's on the table
- A genuine, unguarded laugh that surprises people who've only seen her in audit mode
- The particular, private satisfaction of closing a clean file: "Lot accounted for, beginning to end. That is the whole job, really. Everything else is just finding out why it isn't."

---

## Arc potential
Maialèn begins the story believing that a properly kept record is as close to incorruptible as anything human beings build — that paper, unlike people, has no motive to lie, and that her job is simply to read it accurately. The investigation will force her to confront a harder truth: that a record can be made to lie *more cleanly* than any person could, precisely because everyone — including her — has been trained to trust its cleanliness as proof of its honesty. Her arc is not Yara's "the systems need defending" (she already half-knew that); it is closer to **"the record is not the truth — the record is a claim, made by someone, for a reason, and it is my job to ask whose claim it is before I ask whether it balances."** She ends the book a better auditor for having been, briefly, a worse one — having signed something she shouldn't have, or nearly having, and having to live with how close "nearly" came.

## Notes for writer
- Her expertise should feel as load-bearing and real as Yara's: she should notice things about seed lots, viability curves, and chain-of-custody paperwork that no one else in the room would catch — and the reader should be able to follow *why* it matters without a lecture
- She is more verbal and more socially warm than Yara by design — her investigative method runs through people and conversation as much as through data, which should make her chapters feel different in texture and pacing from Book 1's
- Her faith in "the record" is the emotional engine of this book the way the biofilter network was Yara's — her care for accuracy is a form of care for the people, seasons, and places downstream of every number she signs
- Keep her warmth genuine, not performative — she is not naive, she has simply chosen, deliberately, not to let institutional disappointment curdle into cynicism (a trait she would recognize in Yara, if they ever compared notes)
- Her translation tics (Spanish/Basque phrases, self-translation mid-sentence) should feel natural and occasional — never a gimmick, never slowing the reader down
